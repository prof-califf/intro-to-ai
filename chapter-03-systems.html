Systems That Know You

Most of the AI in your day never announces itself. It decides what song plays next, which video you see, which product sits at the top of the page. This chapter opens up the recommendation system, the most common AI any of us will ever meet, and then looks at the one moment each year when Spotify turns a year of listening into a story about the listener.

How recommenders guess · the loop that shapes taste · where the LLM fits · Wrapped, taken apart · how companies put generative AI to work

Learning objectives
  1. Explain the two main ways a recommendation system guesses what you'll like, and why new users and new songs are hard for it.
  2. Trace the feedback loop from your listening to the next recommendation, and say why it can narrow taste and favor whoever already has an audience.
  3. Tell a predictive part of a system from a generative part, and say what each one can get wrong.
  4. Run the five parts and the five critical AI questions on Spotify Wrapped.
  5. Describe the pattern most organizations follow when they put generative AI into a product, and say why the first uses are narrow.
  6. Decide, with costs, what a recommendation system should be allowed to optimize for.
1

The first Wednesday in December

Every year, in the first week of December, Spotify tells hundreds of millions of people who they are. A top artist. A minutes count. The song someone played 212 times in March. A "listening age." And now, a paragraph about one Tuesday in July that the listener had forgotten, written by a machine. We screenshot it, post it, argue about it, and feel seen by it. For one day, an AI system is the most shared thing on the internet.

Wrapped is the friendly face of a much bigger machine. Underneath it is the system that runs Spotify the other 364 days: the one that builds Discover Weekly, picks the next song when a playlist ends, and decides which of the 100,000 tracks uploaded each day any of us will ever hear. That machine is a recommendation system, and versions of it sit inside TikTok, YouTube, Netflix, Amazon, Instagram, and whatever app we check after class. Learn how one works and we've learned how most of the AI in our lives works.

Here's the chapter's question: who is a recommendation for? The obvious answer is the listener. The honest answer is more complicated, and Wrapped, of all things, is where it shows.

2

How recommendation systems actually work

2.1 Two ways to guess

A recommendation system has one job: predict, out of everything it could show us, what we're most likely to want. Predictive AI, in Chapter 1's terms. There are two classic ways to make that guess.

The first is collaborative filtering: people who liked what we liked also liked this. The system doesn't need to know anything about the music itself. It needs a huge table of who played what, and it looks for neighbors, listeners whose history overlaps with ours, and hands us what they played that we haven't. Spotify's early Discover Weekly worked largely this way, mining the playlists people built; the company has said its models learned from hundreds of millions of user-created playlists. It's powerful because it captures things nobody could write a rule for: that fans of one obscure band tend to like another, for reasons even the fans couldn't explain.

The second is content-based filtering: this is similar to what we liked. Here the system looks at the thing itself. For music, that means audio features the model extracts from the sound (tempo, energy, timbre) plus text about the song from the wider web (reviews, blogs, playlist titles). If someone plays a lot of slow, acoustic, sad songs, it can find another one even if nobody in their listening neighborhood has heard it yet.

Both approaches have a weakness the industry calls the cold start problem. A brand-new listener has no history, so collaborative filtering has nothing to match. A brand-new song has no plays, so nobody's neighbors have heard it. Content-based methods help with new songs, which is one reason real systems blend both. Keep the cold start problem in mind. It explains something uncomfortable later.

Collaborative vs. content-based filtering

Collaborative filtering recommends from behavior: people like you played this. Content-based filtering recommends from features: this sounds like what you played. What people get wrong: they picture the system "understanding" music. It doesn't. It finds patterns in plays and in sound, and the patterns work well enough that understanding isn't needed.

2.2 The signals, and the taste profile

What counts as "liking" something? The system can't ask, so it watches. Spotify has been open about some of this: a play counts as a listen after 30 seconds, a skip before that is a negative signal, and saves, repeats, and adds to playlists count for more than a single stream. Skips matter enormously. A song that gets skipped early is a song the model learns to stop suggesting.

All of that behavior gets compressed into a taste profile: a mathematical summary of a listener, a long list of numbers that places them near listeners and songs they're likely to enjoy and far from the ones they're not. Nobody sees their own. The system also learns that each of us is several people: the workout self, the studying self, the 1 a.m. self. Modern recommenders keep a few of these profiles and guess which one is listening from the time, the device, and the last song played.

Run the five parts on this and the shape is clear. Data: every play, skip, and save, from each of us and from hundreds of millions of others. Model: the filters above, which turn the data into a taste profile and a ranking. Output: the next song, the Monday playlist, the top of the home screen. Feedback loop: what we do with the output becomes tomorrow's data. Humans: the listener, the data scientists who choose what the model rewards, and the people who make the music.

2.3 The loop, and who it favors

That feedback loop is where recommendation systems stop being neutral. The system shows us something; we play it or skip it; the model updates; it shows us more of what we played. Over months, it doesn't just learn our taste. It shapes it, because we can only like what we're shown. Engineers call the extreme version a filter bubble. A gentler word is drift: our recommendations get more like our recommendations.

The loop also has a direction: it favors whoever already has data. A popular artist generates millions of signals, which puts them in more neighborhoods, which generates more plays, which generates more signals. A new artist, by definition, has few. That's the cold start problem with a paycheck attached. The system doesn't dislike new artists. It just can't see them, and invisibility on a platform where the algorithm drives a large share of listening is a business problem with a real dollar cost.

Now add the part that isn't about the listener at all. Since 2020, Spotify has run Discovery Mode, a program in which artists and labels accept a roughly 30 percent lower royalty on certain streams in exchange for their songs being favored in algorithmic recommendations like Radio and Autoplay. There's no upfront fee; you pay in royalties. Members of Congress asked Spotify in 2020 whether this risked a race to the bottom, where the only way to get recommended is to accept less pay. Spotify says the program is one of thousands of signals and that its guide to recommendations discloses that commercial considerations can play a role. In November 2025, a listener filed a class action arguing that a service promising a personalized experience was steering her toward the same major-label tracks and not telling her which recommendations were paid. However that case ends, the point for this chapter is structural: the system is optimizing for more than one thing, and only one of them is the listener.

Engagement optimization

Training a system to maximize a measurable stand-in for your attention: streams, minutes, sessions, return visits. What people get wrong: they think the algorithm prefers outrage, or major labels, or sad songs. It prefers nothing. It finds whatever holds people and whatever it's paid to favor, and it can't tell a listener who loves a song from one who just stopped skipping.

2.4 Where the LLM fits

Everything so far is predictive AI: a ranking. It never writes a sentence. But since 2024, systems like Spotify's have added a second kind of AI on top: a large language model that generates text, the same technology behind chatbots, Chapter 1's Moment 4. The two do different jobs, and confusing them is the most common mistake people make about "the algorithm."

The recommender decides what. The language model decides how to say it. Spotify's AI DJ is the clearest example: a recommendation system picks the songs, and a language model writes the DJ's patter between them, read out in a cloned voice. Wrapped works the same way. The numbers come from counting. The paragraph about someone's July comes from a language model handed those numbers and told to write something warm. Each layer fails differently. The ranking can be wrong about what we want. The language model can be wrong about what happened: it can overstate, invent a detail, or say something true in a way that lands badly, and it does all of it fluently.

Predictive layer, generative layer

In most consumer AI today, a predictive model ranks and a generative model narrates. What people get wrong: they blame "the AI" as one thing. Ask which layer failed. A bad recommendation is a data or ranking problem. A bad sentence about a person is a generation problem. They get fixed in different rooms.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: which concept is doing the work? Decide, then click to check.
1. "Because you watched a documentary about coral reefs, here's one about deep-sea fish."

Content-based filtering. It matched features of the thing you watched; it needed nothing about other people.

2. A podcast with 40 total listeners never shows up in anyone's recommendations.

The cold start problem. Collaborative filtering can't recommend what nobody has played, so new and small things wait at the door.

3. You skip three sad songs on a bad day; a week later the app has stopped suggesting slow music entirely.

The loop. Your behavior became the next input, the taste profile moved, and the system now shows you less of what it decided you don't want.

4. Your Wrapped tells you, warmly, that you "found comfort in" an album in October. The album is a workout playlist.

The generative layer. The counts are from the predictive layer; the sentence about comfort is the language model narrating, and narrating wrong.

5. A label pays for a "boost" and its new single reaches you as "recommended for you."

A paid signal in a system that says it's personal: Discovery Mode, and the question Chapter 3's case turns on.

3

The case: Spotify Wrapped as an AI system

Background From a top-tracks list to a story about you

Wrapped launched in 2016 as a list of your top tracks. By 2025 it was an animated story with nearly a dozen data stories: minutes listened, top songs and artists, top albums for the first time, a "listening age" comparing your taste with your age group, and share cards built to be posted. Spotify publishes how it's made, which is rare and makes this case possible.

The data. Wrapped counts your listening from January to mid-November. A listen is a play of more than 30 seconds. Listening in Private Mode counts toward your total minutes but doesn't shape your taste stories, which means the company keeps two versions of you: the one it counts and the one it tells stories about. To get a personalized Wrapped at all you need to have played at least 30 songs for more than 30 seconds and at least five artists. Everything Wrapped knows, the recommender already knew; Wrapped is that year of data, read back to you.

The model, in two layers. The predictive layer is the counting and ranking: top songs by number of listens, one stream is one listen whether the song is three minutes or ten, and Spotify's data scientists say they spend the year testing which ranking methods feel most like the listener. The generative layer is new. For 2025's "Listening Archive," Spotify's engineers used a set of priority-ordered rules to pick up to five remarkable days from your year (a day you binged an album, a day your listening spiked), and then handed each day's details to a language model to write a short summary. By the company's own account the system pre-generated about 1.4 billion reports for roughly 350 million users. That's an LLM writing a paragraph about you, at a scale no editor could ever check.

The output and the loop. The output is cards designed to be shared, and the sharing is the loop. Every posted Wrapped is an ad, a nudge to your friends to open the app, and a burst of December listening, which becomes next year's data. Wrapped is the one day a year the recommendation system's product is you, and it's also the day the system sells the most subscriptions.

The humans. You, choosing what to post. The data scientists, choosing what a "listen" means and which days are "remarkable." And the year it went wrong, the humans who trusted a model to speak for them. In 2024, Wrapped added an AI-generated podcast built with Google's NotebookLM, two synthetic hosts chatting about your year. It was broadly panned; critics called the whole launch AI slop, and the company got the message. The 2025 edition went analog on purpose, styled like mixtapes and scrapbooks, and kept its language model in the background, writing a few sentences per day rather than performing. Same technology, different design decision, opposite reaction.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

Two layers. A predictive system that counts and ranks a year of listening, and a generative system that writes about it. It turns your data into a story you'll share, which turns your friends into December listeners. Name both layers, because they fail differently.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

Officially, so you can see yourself in your sound. Commercially, because it's the most effective marketing Spotify has ever built and it costs the company almost nothing but compute. You benefit, and people like it, which is real. Artists benefit from the exposure. Spotify benefits most: attention, sign-ups, and a fresh reason to keep a year of your listening data, which is also the fuel for everything in section 2. The benefit and the cost run on the same data.

Who doesn't benefit: artists outside the neighborhoods the recommender already favors, who never make it into anyone's Wrapped; listeners whose "taste" was partly paid promotion, read back to them as if it were theirs; and anyone whose remarkable day the language model describes wrong.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

The predictive layer fails quietly: a ranking that feels wrong, a top artist you'd rather not admit to. You find out, and you shrug. The generative layer fails loudly, because it speaks. A language model writing 1.4 billion paragraphs will get some wrong, and some right in ways that hurt: the day you listened to one song on repeat was a funeral, not a vibe. Spotify's engineers describe safety loops and testing for exactly this; the scale is why it's hard. And the 2024 podcast failed in public, which is the visible kind of failure that gets fixed fast. The failure nobody sees is the loop: a year of taste shaped by recommendations, some of them paid, read back to you as if it were all you.

Business implications What this means for anyone building a "your year" feature

Wrapped is the most copied feature in consumer software, and every copy makes the same two decisions Spotify made. First, what data you keep: a year-in-review needs a year of behavior, so shipping one means deciding to retain it, and retention is a liability as well as an asset. Second, what the generative layer is allowed to do: describe facts the system already counted, or interpret the person. The first is cheap to check and hard to get badly wrong; the second is where a warm sentence becomes a wrong one at scale. There's a third decision most companies skip, and it's the one the Discovery Mode lawsuit is about: whether anything commercially boosted is labeled as such inside a feature sold as personal. The costs of doing these well are engineering time, a smaller, plainer feature, and a harder conversation with whoever pays for placement. The cost of doing them badly, in 2024, was a launch that got called slop in public.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

"Wrapped is your year" is Spotify's framing, and it's mostly true, with exclusions the company documents. "Personalized" is also Spotify's word, and the Discovery Mode lawsuit is a fight over whether it's entirely true. "AI slop" was the critics' verdict on 2024, and the company's response tells you it took the claim seriously. How would we know if Wrapped is accurate? Spotify publishes its methodology, which is more than most systems offer. How would we know how much of your taste was steered? That's the number nobody publishes.

How to keep it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it: people like Wrapped, it's transparent about its methods, and 2025 showed the company can learn from a bad year. The cost: it normalizes keeping a year of your behavior, and it keeps working as marketing whether or not you think of it that way.
  • Fix it: label which recommendations were commercially boosted, let people see and edit the taste profile Wrapped is built from, and keep the language model to short, checkable facts. The cost: less magic, fewer surprises, and a harder conversation with the labels who pay for Discovery Mode.
  • Drop it: opt out, or never open it. The cost is small for you and nothing for Spotify; the data is collected either way. Dropping Wrapped doesn't drop the recommender.
How to make it good

Wrapped is close to a good version already, which is why it's worth studying. Keep the published methodology and the human-checked exclusions. Fix the part the fun hides: show people the profile the system has built, mark what was paid promotion, and let the language model describe rather than interpret. Drop any generated line the system can't trace to a fact in the data. The cost of all three is a slightly less flattering mirror. The benefit is a mirror you can trust.

4

Wrapped as a pattern: how organizations are putting generative AI to work

Step back from the music and Wrapped is something more useful: a clear example of how a large company actually put a language model into a product used by hundreds of millions of people. Most organizations are somewhere on this path right now, and most of them are doing it the way Spotify did, whether they've noticed or not.

Start with a narrow, low-stakes job. Spotify didn't ask a language model to pick your music or run the app. It asked it to write a few warm sentences about a day that the company's own rules had already selected. The model narrates; it doesn't decide. That's the pattern across industries in 2026: the first generative AI most companies ship writes summaries, first drafts, product descriptions, and customer-service replies, not decisions that can be appealed.

Keep the facts outside the model. The numbers in Wrapped come from counting, not from the language model. The model is handed the facts and told to describe them. Organizations that get this right treat the model as a writer with a fact sheet, not as a source. Organizations that get it wrong end up in Chapter 10.

The model is the easy part. Spotify's engineers said as much: at their scale, the language-model call was simple, and the real work was capacity planning, cost discipline, replay and recovery when things fail, and safety loops to catch bad outputs before they reached anyone. A prototype takes an afternoon. Running it for 350 million people takes a team, a budget, and a plan for the day it breaks.

Learn in public, then pull back. The 2024 AI podcast was a bet that more generative AI meant a better product. The reaction said otherwise, and 2025 kept the model in the background. That's a pattern too: companies are finding that the best use of generative AI is often the least visible one.

Nobody can read all of it. At 1.4 billion reports, human review of each output is impossible, so the guardrails have to be built in: what the model is allowed to say, what it must never say, and tests that run before launch. Every organization deploying generative AI at scale faces the same math, and the ones with consequential outputs, a loan decision, a medical note, keep a human on the loop for that reason. Chapter 9 picks this up as an economic question: a company that adds a chatbot has adopted AI; a company that reorganizes work around it is doing something else.

Where generative AI lands first

In most organizations, generative AI arrives as a narrator, not a decider: summaries, drafts, replies, descriptions, with the facts supplied from outside the model. What people get wrong: they judge a company's AI by how visible it is. The mature deployments are usually the quiet ones, and the loud ones are usually prototypes.

5

Decide: what should it optimize for?

Every recommendation system optimizes for something, and someone chose it. Minutes, sessions, return visits, revenue, or, in the rare case, something like the listener's own stated goals. The choice is invisible to us and it's the whole product. When you build or buy one of these systems, and many of you will, the decision isn't whether to use AI. It's what number the loop chases and who gets to see the number.

That's also the Prototype project's question in miniature. A recommendation feature is the most likely piece of AI in whatever you design, and the same three choices apply: what it optimizes for, whether people can see why they got what they got, and whether anyone can pay to move the ranking. Answer those three and you've done the hard part.

The lab puts you on the other side of the glass. You are the recommendation system, your user is a first-year student, and your score is engagement. You'll find out what the metric does to your decisions, and then what a second metric would have caught. It's the second half of Lab 2, which covers both this chapter and the last one; you'll run the first pass in class right after this case and finish the rest at home.

Key terms

Recommendation system

Predictive AI that ranks what to show you next from everything it could show you. The most common AI in daily life.

Collaborative filtering

Recommending from behavior: people whose history overlaps yours also played this.

Content-based filtering

Recommending from features of the item itself: this sounds like what you played.

Cold start problem

New users and new items have no data, so the system can't see them. It favors whoever already has an audience.

Taste profile

The system's mathematical summary of you, built from plays, skips, and saves. You never see it.

Engagement optimization

Training a system to maximize a stand-in for attention. It can't tell love from habit.

Discovery Mode

Spotify's program letting artists trade lower royalties for a boost in algorithmic recommendations. A paid signal inside a "personal" system.

Narrator, not decider

The pattern most organizations follow first: the model writes, with facts supplied from outside it, and people keep the decisions.

Predictive layer, generative layer

The recommender decides what; a language model decides how to say it. They fail differently and get fixed in different rooms.
