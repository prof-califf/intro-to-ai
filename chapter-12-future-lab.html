First Contact, Revisited

Week 1, you judged seven claims about the world as it already was. Today you write the ten claims about 2036 that next year's class will judge — each one built the way this course taught you: a mechanism, an instrument, and the five questions. Then your own Week 1 positions come back to you.

Ten claims for 2036 · one instrument each · the bookend to Lab 1

1

Re-answer Week 1

Before anything new: open Lab 1 in another tab and mark the seven claims again, from what you know now — no peeking at your Week 1 screenshot until you're done. Then put the two screenshots side by side. The claims you doubted then and believe now are the weeks that moved you. The ones you still doubt are your homework for life.

2

Write the docket for 2036

Ten claims about a decade from now, one per row. Each needs three things: the claim itself (specific enough to be wrong), the instrument that would show it coming true, and the one Monday question that matters most for it. Rough is fine — this is scenario thinking, not prophecy, and the next class gets to grade you.

Your ten claims · compile them when you're done
The 2036 docket

3

Read the room

Pair up and trade dockets. For your partner's most confident claim, ask the two questions that have run this whole course: what would we need to see — is the instrument they named actually capable of showing it? — and who benefits if it comes true. Then swap the hardest one: the claim you each least want to be true. Say out loud what it would take to prevent it. That sentence is usually a condition from Chapter 12's list.

4

Run the five questions on your own docket

1

What is it, and what does it do? Count your ten claims: how many are predictive, generative, embodied, agentic? If they cluster, that's a reading of where you think the decade goes.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? For each claim, who benefits if it comes true? If the same names keep appearing, you've written a forecast about power, not just about technology.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Which of your ten has the least visible failure? Name the institution that would catch it. If none exists yet, you've found something worth building.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Each of your ten is a claim you're making. What would you need to see by 2036 to know you were wrong, and who would be positioned to tell you?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Pick the claim with the highest stakes and write the decision it forces: keep, fix, or drop, and what it would cost the people in it.

5

The capstone writeup

Due by the end of finals week · post to Canvas
  1. Both dockets. Your Week 1 and finals-week First Contact screenshots side by side, plus your compiled 2036 docket.
  2. The movement. Which Week 1 claims flipped for you, and the specific case that flipped each one. One sentence each.
  3. The claim you'd bet on. Your single most confident 2036 claim, with its instrument and the mechanism chain behind it — written as a scenario, not a prediction.
  4. The five questions. Your answers on your own docket, including the accountability chain for your highest-stakes claim.
  5. The commitment, kept. Read back every commitment card you wrote this quarter (they're in your writeups). Choose one to keep past this course, and say — in one sentence a future employer could violate — what it commits you to. That's the last thing this course asks of you, and the first thing the hybrid society does.
