Who Makes the Rules?

Every chapter in this book has ended with someone deciding: keep it, fix it, or drop it. This chapter is about who that someone is when the decision becomes a rule. In 2026 the loudest fight in AI policy is not whether to regulate AI. It is who gets to.

The question every chapter has been asking

Look back at where our cases ended. In Chapter 6, a job applicant sued Workday over its screening software, and New York City already required bias audits for hiring tools. In Chapter 10, a political consultant was fined $6 million by federal regulators over a cloned-voice robocall, and then acquitted by a jury under state law that predated voice clones. In Chapter 2, China wrote rules for chatbots that act human. Every time we asked "keep it, fix it, or drop it," the real answer depended on a rule someone else had written, or hadn't.

So this chapter asks the question hiding under Question 5. When a society decides to fix an AI system, who does the fixing? A city council? A state legislature? Congress? The European Union? The company itself? A judge? In the United States right now, all of them are trying, and they don't agree about who should win.

The point of this chapter, in one sentence: AI rules get made at many levels at once, the levels fight over who gets the last word, and a rule can fail just as quietly as an AI system does.

What this chapter teaches. Four ideas, then a case to use them on.

  • First, why rules exist at all: markets that can't see their own costs.
  • Second, the pacing problem: why the rules always arrive late.
  • Third, three models: how the EU, the U.S., and China each answer the question.
  • Fourth, floor or ceiling: the fight over whether states can set their own AI rules.

Then the case, a few miles from our classroom: the license plate cameras in Bellingham parking lots, whose data reached federal immigration agents without the local police knowing, and the Washington law that took effect this fall in response. We'll run the five critical AI questions on it, and ask how to make it good.

Your Prototype project is due that Wednesday, the last day of class, at 11:59 p.m. If your project system touches hiring, lending, housing, health, or insurance, this chapter tells you which rules it lives under.

2

The concepts: why rules exist, why they lag, and who writes them

2.1 Why rules exist: markets that can't see their own costs

A common picture of regulation is the government on one side and business on the other. Economists start somewhere else. They ask when a market, left alone, produces results almost nobody wants. They call those cases market failures, and two kinds show up in every chapter of this book.

The first is an externality: a cost paid by people who never agreed to the deal. The power bill from a data center in Chapter 5 is one. So is a cloned voice calling a voter in Chapter 10. The company and the customer both got what they wanted. Someone else paid.

The second is an information asymmetry: one side knows far more than the other. The company that built a résumé screener can test it for bias. The applicant it rejected can't even see it. No amount of shopping around fixes that, because the applicant never gets to shop.

History shows what rules can do in cases like these. Britain's Factory Act of 1833 barred children under nine from textile mills, required working children to get schooling, and created government inspectors to check. The owners said it would ruin them. It didn't, and the schooling requirement helped build the demand for public education. In 1938 the U.S. Fair Labor Standards Act set a federal minimum wage, required overtime pay, limited child labor, and put the country on the road to the 40-hour week. Rules like these didn't stop the technology. They changed who paid for it.

Market failure

A situation where a market, left alone, produces outcomes that are harmful or unfair, usually because outsiders pay the costs (an externality) or one side knows much more than the other (an information asymmetry). What people get wrong: they treat regulation as the opposite of a market. Often it's what lets a market work, because people will use a system they can trust.

2.2 The pacing problem: why the rules always arrive late

If rules help, why are they always late? In 1980 the scholar David Collingridge described a trap that every technology falls into. Early on, a technology is easy to change, but nobody knows what harm it will do. Later, the harm is obvious, but the technology is built into everything and very hard to change. You can have knowledge or control, and rarely both at the same time.

AI is deep inside that trap. The Chapter 6 hiring tools were already screening millions of applicants before anyone measured what they did. That's the pacing problem: the technology moves in months, and lawmaking moves in years.

The Collingridge dilemma

A technology's harms can't be fully known until it's widely used, but once it's widely used it's hard to control. What people get wrong: they read this as a reason to wait. It's a reason to write rules that can be adjusted, and to build ways of watching (audits, disclosures, dashboards) before the harm is obvious.

2.3 Three models: Brussels, Washington, and Beijing

The three biggest AI economies have answered "who makes the rules?" in three different ways.

The European Union writes one big law. The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024. It sorts AI systems by risk. A few uses are banned outright. "High-risk" uses, which include AI in hiring, education, credit, and law enforcement, get heavy requirements: testing, documentation, human oversight. Lower-risk systems mostly have to be honest that they're AI. This is risk-based regulation: the rules scale with the harm a use can do.

Even the EU runs into the pacing problem. The high-risk rules were supposed to start on August 2, 2026. In May 2026 EU lawmakers agreed to push them back: standalone high-risk systems like hiring tools now start on December 2, 2027. The same deal added a new ban on apps that generate fake nude images. The delay became law in late July 2026, days before the old deadline.

The EU law also reaches outside Europe. A company that sells to Europeans has to follow it, and building one version of a product is cheaper than building two. So the strictest rulebook often becomes the default everywhere. That's called the Brussels effect, and it happened before with Europe's privacy law.

The United States has no single AI law. As of September 2026, Congress has not passed a comprehensive one. Instead, existing agencies apply existing laws (discrimination law in Chapter 6, fraud law in Chapter 10), a few narrow federal laws target specific harms, such as the 2025 TAKE IT DOWN Act on fake intimate images, and the states write their own. They write a lot. By one count, states enacted 159 AI laws in 2025 and another 109 in the first half of 2026. California's SB 53, in effect since January 2026, requires the developers of the most powerful models to publish safety frameworks, report serious safety incidents, and protect employees who blow the whistle.

China writes many small rules, fast. It issued rules for recommendation algorithms in 2022, for deepfakes and for generative AI in 2023, and, as we saw in Chapter 2, for chatbots that act human. The state writes the rules and also sets national goals for how fast AI should spread, so the rules and the rollout come from the same place.

The Brussels effect

When one large market's strict rules become the global default, because companies would rather build one compliant product than many. What people get wrong: they assume a U.S. worker only lives under U.S. rules. If your employer sells in Europe, you may already be working under the EU AI Act.

2.4 Floor or ceiling: who gets the last word

In the U.S. system, federal law can override state law. That's called preemption. The current fight is about whether federal AI policy should preempt the state laws in 2.3.

The case for preemption goes like this. AI products cross state lines. Fifty different rulebooks cost money, slow companies down, and let a few big states set rules for everyone. The case against goes like this. Congress hasn't passed a national standard, so preemption now would wipe out protections and replace them with nothing. Critics put it as a choice between a floor and a ceiling: should state laws be a minimum that states can build on, or a maximum they can't go past?

Congress has tried preemption and backed off. In July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip a ten-year freeze on state AI laws out of a budget bill. A bipartisan House draft in June 2026 proposed a three-year version, and it drew fire from both directions. Meanwhile the White House acted on its own, and Colorado shows what that looks like on the ground.

In May 2024 Colorado became the first state to pass a broad law on "high-risk" AI, the kind of software that helps decide who gets a job, a loan, or an apartment. It required companies to prevent algorithmic discrimination and assess their systems every year. Its start date slipped, eventually to June 30, 2026. Then, in December 2025, a presidential executive order created a task force to challenge state AI laws in court and singled out Colorado's rule. In April 2026, xAI sued Colorado, the Justice Department joined in, and a federal court paused the law. In May the legislature replaced it with a narrower version: tell people when software is used on them, explain a bad outcome within 30 days, let them correct wrong data, and offer human review. It starts January 1, 2027. Add it up: a law passed in 2024 to protect people from biased screeners never protected anyone for a single day. A rule that never starts is a quiet failure, and it's worth running the five questions on rules, not just on systems.

Underneath the fight is a collective action problem: a situation where each player does what's best for itself and everyone ends up worse off. Each company that slows down to test for bias loses ground to one that doesn't. Each state that writes strict rules risks losing businesses to states that don't. Each country fears falling behind its rivals. Nobody wants to go first. That's the classic reason rules get written at a higher level, and it's also the argument each side uses about the other.

Preemption

When a higher level of government overrides a lower level's laws on the same subject. What people get wrong: preemption doesn't have to mean fewer rules. It can set a strong national standard or a weak one. The question is always what replaces the rules it removes.

2.5 The rule-makers nobody elected

Governments aren't the only rule-makers. Courts make rules case by case: Air Canada learned in Chapter 2 that it owned what its chatbot said, and the lawyers in Chapter 10 learned what happens when a brief cites cases that don't exist. Companies make rules for themselves, through safety policies, internal audits, and decisions about what they won't sell. And industry groups write standards that often become the practical definition of "reasonable."

Self-rules move faster than laws. They also have a known weak spot. Regulatory capture is when the people being regulated end up shaping the rules in their own favor, through lobbying, through staff moving between industry and government, or simply because the government has to ask the industry how its own technology works. With AI, government often has fewer experts than the companies it's checking, so the risk is high.

Regulatory capture

When a regulator ends up serving the industry it's supposed to oversee. What people get wrong: they picture bribery. Usually it's quieter: the only people who understand the system work for the company that built it.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: who is making the rule, and is it acting as a floor (a minimum others can build on) or a ceiling (a maximum)? Decide, then click to check.
1. A state requires any company using AI to reject tenants to tell the applicant, and cities in that state may add more requirements.

A state legislature, acting as a floor. The phrase "may add more" is the tell: the state sets a minimum and leaves room above it.

2. A federal law says states may not pass their own rules on how chatbots are trained, and sets no training rules of its own.

Congress, acting as a ceiling, and an empty one. This is the version critics fear: preemption that removes state rules and replaces them with nothing.

3. A software company announces it will stop selling its face-matching tool to police.

The company itself. Self-rules are real rules for that company's customers, but no one outside the company can enforce them, and the company can reverse them.

4. A U.S. clothing retailer changes its hiring software for all its stores after an EU law requires the change for its European stores.

The EU, reaching into the U.S. through the Brussels effect. No American lawmaker voted on this rule, and it applies anyway.

5. A judge rules that a bank can't blame its loan model's vendor for a biased denial.

A court, making a rule case by case. It's neither floor nor ceiling in the legislative sense, but every bank's lawyers will read it as a new minimum.

3

The case: the cameras in the Home Depot parking lot

Background A camera on a pole on Telegraph Road

Drive into the Home Depot on Telegraph Road and a camera on a pole reads your license plate. It's an automated license plate reader (ALPR): a camera that uses AI to capture plate numbers and vehicle details and turn them into a searchable record of where cars have been. The best-known maker is Flock Safety, which by one count has nearly 100 law enforcement clients in Washington, plus more than 100 businesses and homeowners associations. Police use the searches to find stolen cars and suspects, and chiefs who use them say they help solve cases.

Then the failure surfaced, and it was a quiet one. In October 2025, the University of Washington's Center for Human Rights used public records to show that at least eight Washington agencies had turned on direct sharing of their camera networks with U.S. Border Patrol during 2025, and that Border Patrol appeared to be searching the networks of at least ten more departments that had never approved it. Some departments said they were caught off guard and shut off nationwide access. The people whose cars were in those records had no way to know.

Washington responded with a rule. The state's first law on license plate readers, the Driver Privacy Act, was signed in March 2026, and agencies have until September 26 to comply. As introduced, the bill called for these systems to be accountable, short-term, logged, and limited in what they collect, and the law requires cameras to come down from sensitive places like health care facilities and places of worship. The day after it was signed, the Bellingham Police Department, which runs no plate readers of its own, ended its agreements to search the Flock cameras at Lowe's and Home Depot. Not everyone waited for the law: this summer, someone cut down two of the four cameras at that Home Depot, part of a trend spreading online. Vandalism isn't a rule, and it isn't a fix. It's a sign that people didn't trust the rules that existed.

One more twist, from a different Bellingham. In August, the police chief in Bellingham, Massachusetts, dropped Flock partly because he learned the cameras offered an AI search tool, "Free Form," that went beyond the plate reader he thought he had bought. The system had grown past what the buyer understood.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

A predictive system: cameras read plates and vehicle details, and software matches them across a network. The five parts:

  • Data: every plate that passes, with time and place. Nobody in those cars agreed to be in the data.
  • Model: image recognition that reads plates and describes vehicles, plus search tools that match them, including the plain-language search the Massachusetts chief didn't know he had.
  • Output: a hit on a plate, or a list of where a car has been.
  • Feedback loop: every camera added makes every search more complete, which makes the network worth more to the next buyer.
  • Humans: the officer searching, the store that hosts the camera, the company that runs the network, and the driver, who is out of the loop entirely.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

To find cars fast: stolen vehicles, suspects, missing people. Police benefit when it works, stores benefit from safer lots, and Flock benefits from every contract and every camera on the network. Drivers with nothing to hide benefit a little and pay in privacy they never priced. Who doesn't benefit: people in immigration cases, who could pay far more than privacy; the departments that lost control of their own data; and the stores that found out from a newspaper what their parking lots had been feeding.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

The failure wasn't a misread plate. It was the sharing settings. Data collected for local crime flowed to a federal agency for a different purpose, and the departments that owned the cameras didn't know. Who found out? Not the drivers, not the chiefs. University researchers, through public records requests, months later. That's the visibility rule again: the people closest to the system were among the last to know.

Business implications What this means for a store, a city, or a vendor

Three organizations made decisions in this case, and each is a business lesson. The store that hosted the camera got a safer parking lot and, without meaning to, became a data collection point for a federal agency; the lesson is that hosting someone else's sensor makes you a party to whatever their sharing settings allow, and you should read them before the sign goes up. The city that contracted for search access got faster investigations and a public-records story; the lesson is that "we don't run the cameras" is not the same as "we're not responsible for what we search," and that a contract's audit terms are the product, not the fine print. And the vendor, whose business depends on every camera making every search more valuable, learned in Massachusetts what happens when the product grows past what the buyer understood: the buyer leaves. The costs of doing this well are a plainer product, slower investigations, and contracts that say no to features the sales team likes. The costs of doing it badly are now a state law, a canceled agreement, and two cameras on the ground.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

Flock says its customer data has never been breached, which may be true and isn't the question; the question is who's allowed in the front door. Police chiefs say the cameras solve crimes, and some do, but few departments publish how many cases the cameras actually closed. Critics say the cameras track everyone, which is literally what they do. How would we know which claims matter? The audit logs exist. The new law makes them mandatory. Whether a city council reads them is the test.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it for the thing it's good at: searching for a specific car tied to a specific crime, with the reason logged. The cost: the network still exists, and every rule about it has to hold.
  • Fix it: sharing closed by default, every outside search visible in an audit someone reads, data deleted after a short time, and no feature the buyer didn't knowingly choose. The cost: slower investigations, as the Massachusetts chief admitted, and less revenue for the company.
  • Drop it: no cameras on public roads or store lots at all. The cost: some stolen cars stay stolen, and the private cameras that remain aren't covered by a police contract or its audits.

How to make it good

The useful part of this system is real, and so is the harm. What separates them isn't the camera; it's the rules around the camera: who can search, for what, who sees the log, and how long the data lives. Washington's rule is being tested right now. By the time we discuss this chapter, the September 26 deadline will have passed. Check what changed in Whatcom County, and ask whether the cameras that are left look more like the good version or the bad one.

Decide: where you fit in the rules

4.1 You will work under these rules

Most of you won't write AI laws. Almost all of you will work under them. If you end up in HR, you may be the person who sends Colorado's 30-day notice in 2027. If your company sells in Europe, the EU's hiring rules reach your desk in December 2027 whether or not Congress acts. If you buy software for a team, you may be the one reading the vendor contract clause Colorado just made void.

That makes the practical skill simple to say and hard to do: for any AI system you touch, know which rules it lives under, and know which ones are still being fought over. That is exactly what your Prototype project asks.

4.2 Taking a position without picking a team

This topic is political, and I'm not going to tell you which side to take. Reasonable people disagree about whether AI rules should come from states, Washington, or somewhere else. What the course asks is that your position survive the five questions. If you favor one national standard, say what it should contain, because preemption with nothing behind it is a different policy from preemption with strong rules behind it. If you favor state rules, say how a company selling in fifty states should comply, and who pays for that. Either way, name the cost. That's the Rules page applied to policy: no fighting without evidence, and no loving or hating a position because of who else holds it.

And remember that rules have comment periods. Colorado's attorney general has to write the details of the new law before it starts, and that process takes public input. Rule-making isn't only something that happens to you.

4.3 Three takeaways

Rules are systems too

A law has data (what it requires companies to record), outputs (notices, fines), and failure modes. Run the five questions on it the way you would on a model. The Colorado law's quietest failure was never starting.

Ask who gets the last word

When you read about an AI rule, the first question is what it requires. The second, in 2026, is whether it will survive: a federal challenge, a delay, a rewrite. The EU delayed its own high-risk rules by sixteen months. A rule on paper is a claim, and Question 4 applies.

Floors and ceilings are the real debate

Almost nobody in this fight says "no rules ever." The disagreement is about who sets the minimum and whether anyone may go above it. Once you can hear that difference, most AI policy news gets easier to read.

Key terms

Automated license plate reader (ALPR)

A camera that uses AI to capture plate numbers and vehicle details into a searchable record. Useful for finding stolen cars; risky when the records are shared or kept without limits.

Market failure

When a market left alone produces harmful or unfair results. The main economic reason rules exist.

Externality

A cost paid by people who weren't part of the deal, like a data center's strain on the local grid.

Information asymmetry

One side knows far more than the other, like a company that can test its screener and an applicant who can't see it.

The Collingridge dilemma

Early on, a technology is easy to change but its harms are unknown; later, the harms are known but it's hard to change.

Risk-based regulation

Rules that scale with the harm a use can do. The EU AI Act's model: banned, high-risk, limited, minimal.

The Brussels effect

When the strictest large market's rules become the global default because one product is cheaper than many.

Preemption

A higher level of government overriding a lower level's laws. The question is always what replaces them: a floor or a ceiling.

Collective action problem

Each player does what's best for itself and everyone ends up worse off, so nobody wants to go first.

Regulatory capture

When a regulator ends up serving the industry it oversees, often because only the industry understands the technology.
