Bias in Hiring & Lending: The Machines That Judge Us

This chapter is about one kind of AI: the classifier, software that sorts people into yes and no by learning from the past. It's the AI most likely to change your life without your ever seeing it, because it reads your résumé before any human does and prices your loan before any banker looks up. We'll learn how it works, how bias gets in, who answers when it's wrong, and then take apart the cleanest case on record: the hiring engine Amazon built and killed.

1

1:50 a.m.

A man applies for a job online at 12:55 in the morning. At 1:50 a.m., less than an hour later, the rejection arrives. Nobody at the company was awake. Nobody read the application. Software did, and software said no. Over several years he applied to more than a hundred jobs and was rejected from all of them. Eventually he did something unusual. He sued not the companies that turned him down but the company that made the software they all used.

Hold that image, because it's the chapter. Most of the AI in this book announces itself: a chatbot talks to you, a car drives past you. The AI in this chapter never does. It reads your résumé before a person does, prices your car loan before a banker looks up, and scores your rental application before the landlord sees your name. You will be on the receiving end of it dozens of times in the next few years. Some of you will be on the sending end, deploying it, before you're 25.

The technology is Chapter 1's five parts with the stakes turned up. The questions are the same five we always ask. The difference is that this time the system's output is a decision about a person, and the person usually never finds out.

2

The concepts: how a machine learns to say no

2.1 The classifier

Strip any of this chapter's examples down and the same machine is inside: a classifier, predictive AI that takes a person's data and outputs a category. Interview or reject. Approve or deny. High risk or low. Chapter 3's recommender ranked songs; a classifier sorts people. It's the oldest, quietest, most widespread kind of AI we've met, and it works the way every predictive system works: show the model thousands of past decisions, who got hired, who repaid, who defaulted, and it finds the pattern. Then it applies that pattern to the next applicant.

Here's a small, honest example. A bank has ten years of car loans: the application (income, job, zip code, credit history) and the outcome (repaid or not). A classifier trained on that data learns which combinations of application details tended to end in repayment. Hand it a new application and it produces a number, say 0.82, meaning "applications like this one repaid 82 percent of the time." A threshold turns the number into a decision: above 0.75, approve. That's the whole machine. No understanding, no intent, just a pattern and a line.

Now notice what the machine actually promises. It does not learn who deserved the loan or the job. It learns who got them. Those are different things, and the gap between them is where every problem in this chapter lives. A model trained on past decisions learns the pattern of every past decision, including the biased ones, and applies it forward with perfect consistency, at industrial scale, to people who never had a chance to argue.

Classifier

Predictive AI that sorts cases into categories by learning patterns from past decisions and applying a threshold. What people get wrong: they picture the model judging merit. It's matching a new case to old outcomes, and old outcomes include everything that was wrong with old decisions.

2.2 The objectivity trap

Classifiers get adopted with a seductive pitch: humans are biased, math is neutral, so let the algorithm decide. It sounds right. A tired recruiter at 4 p.m. on a Friday is not a fair judge, and a model gives every applicant the same attention at any hour.

The trap is that the algorithm learned from the humans. If the recruiters of the last decade preferred one kind of applicant, the "good hire" pattern the model finds is that kind of applicant. The model doesn't remove the bias. It launders it: it converts messy, visible human prejudice into a clean, confident, uniform score that looks like arithmetic. The output looks objective precisely because the subjectivity moved upstream, into the training data, where nobody can see it and no one is asked to defend it.

Consistency makes it worse, not better. A biased recruiter is inconsistently biased; a good day, a good mood, a good conversation can override the prejudice. A biased model is biased the same way every time, for every applicant, forever, until someone notices. That's why "the system treats everyone the same" is not a defense. It can treat everyone the same and still be unfair to the same people every time.

The objectivity trap

Mistaking a classifier's consistency for neutrality. What people get wrong: they think removing the human removed the bias. It moved the bias into the data and gave it a number, which is harder to argue with than a person.

2.3 Proxies and disparate impact

"Fine," says the obvious fix, "just don't show the model gender or race." Lending is where that fix goes to die, and it dies on a concept called the proxy: an allowed variable that stands in for a forbidden one. A model forbidden from seeing race can often reconstruct it from what it is allowed to see: zip code, which stores you shop at, which college you attended, even the words in your cover letter. A model forbidden from seeing age can read a graduation year. A model forbidden from seeing gender can read "women's chess club captain."

In American lending this has a long history that predates any computer. In the 1930s, federal maps rated neighborhoods for mortgage risk, and the ones outlined in red were overwhelmingly Black neighborhoods; that's where the word redlining comes from. Banks used the maps for decades. The practice was outlawed in 1968, but the maps shaped where people could buy, which shaped who owns what today, which is why a zip code still carries information about race. A classifier trained on data with that history in it can produce disparate impact: systematically worse outcomes for a protected group, with no discriminatory line of code anywhere and no one in the company intending it.

Disparate impact is a legal term, and the law is older than AI. U.S. hiring and lending law has prohibited it since the 1970s, and regulators have long used a rough test called the four-fifths rule: if one group's selection rate is less than 80 percent of another's, the process gets scrutinized. The classifier didn't invent the problem. It industrialized it and made it harder to see, because the reasons now live inside a model rather than in a manager's head.

Here's what the evidence actually shows, and it cuts both ways. A University of California, Berkeley team studied millions of U.S. mortgages, comparing borrowers with equivalent credit risk. Their findings: Black and Latino borrowers paid measurably higher interest rates than risk-equivalent white borrowers, a gap worth hundreds of millions of dollars a year, and algorithmic online lenders discriminated roughly 40 percent less than face-to-face lenders did. Both facts at once. The machine judged more fairly than the humans it replaced, and it still charged people more for the color of their skin. Neither "algorithms are biased, ban them" nor "algorithms fix bias, adopt them" survives contact with that study. It's the one to remember.

Proxy and disparate impact

A proxy is an allowed variable that reconstructs a forbidden one. Disparate impact is what proxies produce: a neutral-looking process with unequal outcomes for a protected group. What people get wrong: they think deleting the sensitive column fixes it. The model finds the column again, in pieces, in everything else.

2.4 Two kinds of wrong

Every classifier makes two kinds of mistakes, and they cost different people. A false positive is a yes that should have been a no: the loan that defaults, the hire that fails. A false negative is a no that should have been a yes: the borrower who would have repaid, the candidate who would have been great. Companies see the first kind, because a bad loan shows up on the books. They rarely see the second kind, because a rejected applicant just disappears. Nobody counts the good hires they didn't make.

That asymmetry shapes the model. When you tune a classifier to avoid the mistake you can see, it makes more of the mistake you can't. A lender that hates defaults sets a high threshold and turns away many people who would have paid. And because the rejected pile is invisible, a model can be "95 percent accurate" on the loans it approved while being badly unfair about who it never approved. Accuracy on the yeses says nothing about the noes.

This is Question 3 of our five, in numbers: what happens when it fails, and who finds out? The false positive is found by the company, fast. The false negative is found by nobody, unless someone goes looking in the rejected pile. That's why every serious fix in this chapter involves someone looking there.

The invisible pile

A classifier's rejections are its least examined output. What people get wrong: they judge a screener by the quality of the people it let through. The question that matters is who it kept out, and almost no one is paid to ask.

2.5 Who answers when the no is illegal

So when the classifier says no and the no is illegal, who answers? The law is catching up, in three steps.

Step one: automating discrimination is still discrimination. Tutoring company iTutorGroup programmed its application software to automatically reject female applicants 55 and older and male applicants 60 and older. It was caught when one applicant, rejected under her real birthdate, resubmitted the identical application with a younger one and promptly got an interview. In 2023 it became the EEOC's first settlement over AI-driven hiring discrimination: $365,000 to more than 200 auto-rejected applicants. The legal principle was almost boring, and that's the point. The software was just the fingerprints.

Step two: the vendor can be on the hook. The man from 1:50 a.m. is the plaintiff in Mobley v. Workday, and he sued Workday, the vendor whose screening tools thousands of employers use. The court let the case proceed on a theory with enormous reach: a vendor whose AI effectively makes screening decisions can be treated as the employer's agent, and sued like one. A federal judge certified a nationwide age-discrimination collective; Workday disclosed in filings that its tools had rejected applications numbering in the billions during the covered period, and roughly 14,000 people joined the case before the 2026 deadline. Workday denies the claims and says its tools don't make hiring decisions and keep humans in the loop. However it ends, the case has already ended something: the era when "the algorithm did it" pointed at nobody.

Step three: the right to an explanation, in one industry. U.S. credit law has required adverse action notices, specific reasons for a denial, since the 1970s, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has told lenders directly that "our model is too complex to explain" is not a legal excuse. Now hold that standard up against hiring, where no equivalent exists. A loan denial must come with reasons. A 1:50 a.m. rejection email doesn't. Same kind of classifier, same stakes, one domain with a fifty-year-old explanation right and one without. That gap is a policy choice, which means it can change, and Chapter 11 is about who gets to change it.

Accountability for an automated no

Three rules are now settled or being settled: automating a discriminatory decision doesn't make it legal; the vendor can be liable alongside the employer; and in lending, you have a right to reasons. What people get wrong: they assume the second and third apply everywhere. In hiring, only the first does, so far.

2.6 The lending half: how a credit decision is actually made

Hiring gets the headlines, but lending is where classifiers have been judging people the longest, and it's the domain with the most developed rules, so it's worth walking through one decision end to end.

Start with the file. Three credit bureaus keep a record of you: what you've borrowed, whether you paid on time, how much of your available credit you're using, how long you've had accounts, what kinds, and how recently you applied for new ones. A scoring model, the classic one has been sold since 1989, turns that file into a three-digit number. It's a classifier's output with a friendlier scale: instead of "0.82," you get 712 on a range of 300 to 850. The factors and their rough weights are public, which is unusual in this chapter. Payment history counts most; amounts owed next; length, mix, and new credit less.

Then comes the part you don't see. The lender takes the score, adds what's on the application (income, existing debt, the loan's size), and applies its own cutoff and often its own model on top. Two lenders can look at the same file and decide differently, because the line is theirs. And a lender's own model is where the proxies of section 2.3 can creep back in: a variable like "shops at this store" or "lives in this zip code" that the classic score never used.

Now the part the law makes visible. If the answer is no, U.S. credit law requires an adverse action notice: the main reasons for the denial, in plain words. "Too many recent inquiries." "Short credit history." "High balances relative to limits." Those reasons are the model's biggest factors, translated, and you can act on them. This is the fifty-year-old standard section 2.5 compares hiring against, and the CFPB's point was simple: a lender that uses a model too complex to produce those reasons has chosen a model it isn't allowed to use.

One more idea from lending that reaches everywhere: the thin file. A classifier can't score what it has no data on. Young people, recent immigrants, and anyone who has avoided debt have thin files, and a thin file is not a bad file; it's an unreadable one. Some lenders now score "alternative data," rent and utility payments, to fill it in, which helps the thin-file applicant and, if the alternative data carries its own proxies, brings section 2.3 right back. Every fix in this chapter has that shape: real progress, and a new place to look.

Adverse action notice

Credit law's requirement that a denial come with its main reasons, stated plainly. What people get wrong: they think it's a courtesy. It's the mechanism that forces a classifier to be explainable, and it's why lending models are simpler and more auditable than hiring models, on purpose.

2.7 How an audit works

"Audit it" is the fix this chapter keeps recommending, so here's what one actually involves. Two tools do most of the work, and neither needs to see inside the model.

The first is the matched pair: two applications identical in every detail except one, a name that reads as one gender, a graduation year that reveals age, a zip code. Run both through the screener. If the scores differ, the one detail you changed is what the model is reading, and no vendor can argue with that, because you never touched their model; you touched the input. This is Wednesday's lab, on a live screener. It's also, almost exactly, how iTutorGroup got caught: one applicant, two birthdates, one interview.

The second is the four-fifths rule, with numbers. Count who advanced, by group. If 60 of 100 applicants under 40 advanced and 40 of 100 over 40 did, the selection rates are 60 and 40 percent, and the ratio is 0.67, under the 0.8 line that regulators have used since 1978 to decide when a process needs a closer look. The rule doesn't prove discrimination; it flags it, which is the point of an instrument. It works on outcomes alone, so it works on a model the vendor won't open.

An audit costs an afternoon and a spreadsheet, run before launch and again every year, because models drift as the data changes. Amazon's engineers ran the equivalent of a matched pair on their own model and found "women's." What they couldn't do was prove the model wasn't reading something else, and that's why the audit has to repeat: it doesn't certify a model clean, it catches what's visible now.

Audit

Testing a classifier's behavior from the outside: matched pairs to find what it reads, selection rates to find what it does. What people get wrong: they wait for the vendor to open the model. You never need to. You need two applications and a count.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: is the problem the training data, a proxy, the threshold, or accountability? Decide, then click to check.
1. A scholarship screener trained on ten years of past winners keeps picking students from the same three high schools.

Training data. The past winners came from those schools, so "winner" and "those schools" are the same pattern to the model. It learned who won, not who deserved to.

2. A lender removes race from its model. Approval rates by race don't change.

A proxy. Zip code, shopping history, or school is carrying the same information. Deleting the column didn't delete the signal.

3. A hiring tool is 97 percent accurate on the people it recommended, and the company calls it a success.

The threshold and the invisible pile. Accuracy on the yeses says nothing about the noes. The question is who it screened out, and nobody has looked.

4. An applicant asks why she was rejected by a rental-screening algorithm and is told the vendor's model is proprietary.

Accountability. In lending she'd have a right to reasons; in housing and hiring the rules are thinner. Whether "proprietary" is a legal answer depends on where she lives, which is Chapter 11's question.

5. A company's screener rejects 30 percent of applicants over 50 and 10 percent of applicants under 30, with no age field in the data.

Disparate impact through a proxy, likely graduation year or years of experience. The four-fifths rule flags it: the older group's selection rate is far under 80 percent of the younger group's.

3

The case: Amazon's recruiting engine

Background The problem Amazon was trying to solve

By 2014, Amazon was hiring at a pace few companies in history had matched, and its recruiters were drowning in résumés. The company's answer was the one you'd expect from a company that had automated its warehouses: automate the screening. A team in Edinburgh, Scotland, set out to build a tool that could do for hiring what Amazon's systems did for products. Feed it a hundred résumés, get back the top five. As Reuters reported in 2018, the team built hundreds of models, each focused on a specific job function and location, and taught them to recognize tens of thousands of terms that had appeared on past applicants' résumés.

The training data was a decade of résumés submitted to Amazon, and the outcomes were what happened to them: who got hired. That's the classifier from section 2.1, exactly. Learn the pattern of past good candidates, apply it to new ones. It's worth pausing on how reasonable this was. Nobody set out to build a biased tool. They set out to save recruiters time using the best data they had.

What happened The model found the women

The tech industry's applicant pool over that decade had been mostly male, and so were its hires. So the model learned, correctly from its point of view, that maleness predicted "good candidate." It penalized résumés containing the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain," and downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. Nobody programmed that rule. The model found it, because it really was there in the data. The objectivity trap and the proxy, in one system: no gender field anywhere, and "women's" was proxy enough.

Amazon's engineers did the obvious fix. They edited the model to neutralize those specific terms. And then they scrapped the project anyway, because they couldn't guarantee it wasn't finding other ways to reach the same conclusion. Think about that. The people with full access to the model, at one of the most technically capable companies on earth, could not prove their classifier fair. They could only stop using it. Amazon told Reuters the tool was never used by its recruiters to evaluate candidates on its own; the team was disbanded by 2017.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

A predictive classifier: hundreds of models that score résumés against the pattern of past Amazon hires. The five parts: data, ten years of résumés and hiring outcomes; model, the term-weighting that learned which words predicted a hire; output, a ranking of applicants; feedback loop, had it shipped, the people it recommended would have become the next decade's "good hires," and the pattern would have tightened; humans, the Edinburgh engineers, the recruiters, and the applicants who never knew a model read their résumé.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

To process a flood of applications at a cost no human team could match, and to make hiring feel objective. Amazon benefits from speed and from cover: "the model ranked them" is an easier sentence than "the recruiter preferred him." The applicant was never the customer. The best-case applicant benefit is real, though: a fair model would have read every résumé with the same attention, which no tired recruiter does.

Who doesn't benefit: the applicants the model scored down for a word on their résumé, who never learned a model had read it; and, quietly, Amazon itself, which spent three years building a tool it couldn't trust and got a Reuters headline for it.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

It fails silently, in the invisible pile. A woman's résumé scores lower and she never learns that a model read it, let alone why. The people who found the failure were insiders, engineers looking at their own model's weights. Nobody outside Amazon would have. That's the pattern across this chapter: iTutorGroup's bias was found by one applicant's lucky experiment, Workday's is being tested in court. Every discovery was nearly an accident, and the failures that weren't discovered are still out there.

Business implications What this means for anyone buying a screener

Almost no company builds its own screener now; they buy one, which is what makes Mobley v. Workday the business end of this case. Three questions before signing. Can you audit it? A vendor that won't let you run two identical applications through its model, differing in one detail, is asking you to take its fairness on faith, and the courts are deciding whether that faith is your liability. What does it optimize for? A tool tuned to minimize bad hires makes more of the mistake you can't see, and the rejected pile is where the lawsuit lives. Who reads the noes? Someone should, on a schedule, because that's the only way the second kind of wrong ever surfaces. The costs are a slower rollout, an audit fee, and a person's time. The alternative is Amazon's choice, dropping the tool, or the choice Amazon's engineers wished they'd made: building it to be checked from the start.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

"Our screener removes human bias" is said by vendors selling screeners. "It launders it" is said by the Amazon engineers who watched it find the women anyway. Amazon says the tool was never the sole basis for a decision, which is a company's claim about its own system. The Berkeley mortgage study says both things at once: less biased than the humans, and still costing minority borrowers hundreds of millions. How would we know about any specific system? The same way Amazon's engineers did: look inside, or run two identical applications that differ in one detail and compare. That test is Wednesday's lab.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it if it beats the humans it replaced? The Berkeley result says a system can beat the humans and still discriminate. "Better than before" isn't the finish line; auditing is.
  • Fix it: a required audit before launch and every year after, an explanation right like lending's, and a person who reads the rejected pile. Each costs speed and money, and sometimes an audit fails and the tool has to wait.
  • Drop it: Amazon's choice. The cost is the humans' worse bias, and the recruiters' time. Refusal is the hardest position to argue this week and the most worth arguing.

Thinking critically The five critical AI questions, on this case, by you

Thinking critically · for Monday's discussion · skim the Reuters Amazon story and the EEOC settlement release linked above · one question each, answered by you, not by the chapter
  1. What is it, and what does it do?   2. Why does it exist, and who benefits? A manager says "we switched to AI screening specifically to remove bias." In three sentences, with no course vocabulary, explain who benefits from that sentence being believed, and why it might have made things worse.
  3. What happens when it fails, and who finds out? A company reports its hiring AI is 95 percent accurate. Write the one question you'd ask before believing the system is fair, say who at the company would have to go look to answer it, and say why nobody currently does.
  4. Who's saying that, and how would we know? The Berkeley study found algorithms discriminated less than humans and still cost minority borrowers hundreds of millions a year. Someone cites only the first half; someone else cites only the second. Write the two-sentence response that holds both, and say what each citer gains from their half.
  5. Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Lending has a legal right to an explanation; hiring doesn't. Should it? Keep, fix, or drop the current rule, with two sentences of defense and one honest cost. Someone pays for every explanation requirement; say who.
How to make it good

A screener like Amazon's isn't doomed; it's unchecked. Keep the part that works: sorting a thousand résumés so a human sees the strongest hundred. Fix it by auditing it the way Wednesday's lab does, two identical applications that differ in one detail, run before launch and every year after, with the rejected pile reviewed by a person. The cost is money, slower rollouts, and the chance the audit fails. Drop any variable that stands in for a protected group, and any vendor that won't let you audit. The cost is a less accurate model, which is the price of a fair one. Amazon chose to drop the whole thing. The better version would have been built to be checked from the start.

Decide: both sides of the machine

Deciding here has a personal edge the other chapters don't, because you're about to be on both sides of this machine: screened by classifiers every time you apply for a job, a loan, an apartment, and, for many of you, deploying them once you're hired. So the decision splits.

As the judged: knowing that proxies, disparate impact, and adverse-action rights exist gives you something to push with. You can ask what screened you, and in lending, the law says they must answer. You can also look at your own résumé the way a model would, which is one of this week's questions.

As the deployer: the Berkeley finding is your framework. A classifier can be both better than the humans it replaced and still discriminatory, which means "better than before" isn't the finish line, auditing is. And refusal keeps its seat: some firms have concluded, like Amazon did, that a classifier they can't verify is a classifier they shouldn't run. That's not technophobia. That's the five parts, taken seriously, by people who could see all five.

Wednesday's lab puts you in the auditor's chair with a live AI screener, one variable at a time. Come with a hypothesis about what it will do.

Key terms

Classifier

Predictive AI that sorts cases into categories, interview or reject, approve or deny, by learning patterns from past decisions and applying a threshold.

Training-data bias

When the history a model learns from encodes discrimination, the model learns the discrimination. Amazon's case, in one line.

The objectivity trap

Mistaking a classifier's consistency for neutrality. The bias didn't vanish; it moved upstream into the data.

Proxy

An allowed variable that reconstructs a forbidden one: zip code for race, graduation year for age. Why deleting the sensitive field doesn't work.

Disparate impact

A neutral-looking process with systematically unequal outcomes for a protected group. Illegal in hiring and lending since before AI existed; the four-fifths rule is the rough test.

False positive, false negative

A yes that should have been no, and a no that should have been yes. Companies see the first and rarely the second.

The invisible pile

A classifier's rejections, its least examined output. Accuracy on the yeses says nothing about the noes.

Matched pair

Two applications identical except for one detail, run through the same screener. The audit tool that shows what a model is reading without opening it.

Thin file

A record with too little data to score. Not a bad file, an unreadable one, and the reason young people and recent immigrants get denied for nothing.

Adverse action notice

Credit law's right to specific reasons for a denial, the standard hiring doesn't have, yet.
