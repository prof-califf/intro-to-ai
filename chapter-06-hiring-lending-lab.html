You Are the Auditor

Chapter 6 said a classifier learns who got the loan or the job, not who deserved it. Today you watch that happen. You train a tiny spam filter to see what a classifier is. Then you run the same machine on a bank's past car loans and a company's past résumés, and watch it learn the old decisions, bias included. Then you build a classifier of your own with Claude, audit it with the chapter's two tools, and debate whether a job rejection should come with reasons.

1

In class: Teach it to sort (a spam filter, the simplest classifier)

A classifier sorts things into categories by learning from past examples that someone already labeled. Your email's spam filter is one. Before you run a classifier on people, train one on something harmless: ten old emails, labeled by you. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Spam · train a spam filter, then decide how strict it is
Your job
  1. Label the ten old emails, Spam or Inbox. You're the past here: whatever you decide, the filter learns.
  2. Press Train. Every word becomes a spam word or a real-mail word, depending on which of your piles it showed up in more. That's everything the filter learns.
  3. Eight new emails arrive. Each one's score is simple: its spam words minus its real-mail words. Two spam words and one real-mail word scores +1.
  4. Pick a cutoff and watch the two counts. The cutoff is the line you draw: score at or above it, and the email goes to spam. Find a cutoff that loses no real email, then one that lets no spam through. Is there one that does both? Look at the emails that tie.
  5. Then break it. Flip one old email's label on purpose, press Train again, and see what moves.
Label all ten old emails.

The score is the filter's count. The cutoff is your decision. An email goes to spam when its score is at or above the cutoff. A low cutoff is strict: one stray spam word can send an email to spam. A high cutoff is easygoing: an email needs lots of spam words before it's caught.

Ten invented emails and eight more to test. Nothing is saved.

That's all a classifier is. Labeled examples go in, weights come out, every new case gets a score, and a cutoff turns the score into a decision. Two things to carry into section 2.

It learned from your labels, not from the truth. When you flipped one label, the filter believed you. It had no other way to know.

The cutoff picks which mistake you'd rather make. When you changed the cutoff, the scores never changed. Only your line did. Set it strict and real email gets lost in spam, a false positive: the filter said "yes, spam" and was wrong. Set it easygoing and spam lands in your inbox, a false negative. No setting got both to zero, and the ties show why. The professor's real email and the fake "verify your account" email can land on the same score. The filter only knows words, so no line can split them.

Every classifier that judges people has this same line, and someone picks where it goes. A bank that hates bad loans sets its line high and turns away more people who would have repaid. A company that hates bad hires does the same with good candidates. Nobody sees those mistakes, because the people on the wrong side of the line just disappear. That's section 2.4's invisible pile, and you'll see it as pink dots in section 2.

In class: Same machine, car loans (Mini-Lender)

Now the emails become people. A small bank in an invented town has 160 past car loan applications from two neighborhoods, Hillside and the Flats. For each one, it knows two things: whether a loan officer approved it, and whether the borrower repaid. About 15 minutes.

One honest catch: in this lab, the bank somehow knows how every applicant would have done, including the ones it turned away. Real banks never know that. A denied applicant never gets the chance to repay. Hold onto that; it comes back in section 4.

Is this real?

The applicants are invented. The pattern isn't. In the 1930s, a federal agency graded city neighborhoods for mortgage risk, in large part by who lived there, and lenders followed those maps for decades (section 2.3). And when a Berkeley team compared U.S. mortgage borrowers with the same credit risk, Black and Latino borrowers still paid higher interest rates, more than $450 million a year in all (Bartlett and others). This lab's data is built to behave like that, with a neighborhood in place of race.

Step 1 is the objectivity trap. Nothing in the code says "deny the Flats." The officers' decisions said it, and the model copied them as closely as it could, every time, for every applicant (section 2.2).

Step 2 is the proxy. Most people in the Flats apply at the Meridian branch, the one near home, so "branch" told the model most of what "neighborhood" did. Deleting the field didn't delete the pattern (section 2.3).

Step 3 only works because this is a toy. You can see all four fields, so you could find the proxy. Amazon's engineers couldn't be sure they had found all of theirs, which is why they scrapped their tool.

Step 4 is the deeper fix: change what the model learns from. Trained on who repaid, it mostly stopped caring where you live, because where you live never predicted repaying. The catch is the one from the top of this section: a real bank doesn't have that column for the people it turned down.

In class: Under the hood, how all four worked, Claude included

  
Every classifier in this lab, the one Claude wrote included, is the same small machine.

  
A weight for every field. The spam filter had a weight for each word. Mini-Lender had one for income, credit history, neighborhood, and branch. The model adds up the weights for a case, turns the total into a score from 0 to 100, and a cutoff turns the score into yes or no. Those weight bars you watched are the whole model.

  
Training is nudging. The lender and the screener start with every weight at zero. They guess on all 160 past cases, check each guess against the label column, nudge the weights toward agreeing, and repeat a few hundred times. This kind of model is called logistic regression, one of the oldest classifiers in business use. Credit scorecards have long been built this way, partly because the weights can be read off and turned into reasons, which is what lending's adverse action notice needs (section 2.5).

  
Where the bias hid. Not in the code. The code was the same every time you switched. Look back at what you saw today, and the bias hid in three places.

  
In the label, which is an assumption. Mini-Lender assumed "approved" meant "creditworthy." Mini-Screener assumed "called back" meant "good candidate." Nobody wrote those assumptions down, but someone chose that column, and when you switched it, different people got in.

  
In the proxies. Branch carried neighborhood, and years of experience carried age, after the fields themselves were gone.

  
In what nobody measures. A real bank never learns whether the people it turned down would have repaid, so its model never learns from its mistakes about them.

  
Underneath all three sits one more assumption: that the past is a fair guide to the future. A model is a record of past decisions, made consistent. So when you audit one, ask what data it learned from and what it was trained to predict, not just what the code does.

  
Real screeners are bigger, not different. Commercial tools use far more fields, and some read the résumé text itself with a language model, so their weights can't be read off like yours. The audit doesn't care. The matched pair and the four-fifths ratio test what comes out, not what's inside, which is why they work on a vendor's model you'll never see.

  
Claude's part. Claude wrote a working classifier in a minute. It didn't decide what the model should learn from, which fields were fair to use, or whether the numbers were right. You did those, by checking.

In class: Debate

  
The motion: Automated decision-making systems (AI and algorithms) must not be allowed to execute high-stakes verdicts on human lives without a mandatory, independent human review. Today, high-stakes means a job or a loan, and it's 2031. Left half of the room is Side A, for the motion. Right half is Side B, against it. In groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4: that number is your piece of evidence below. The Gilman Street rules apply.

  
Chapter 6 opened at 1:50 a.m.: an application went in at 12:55, a rejection came back less than an hour later, and nobody at the company was awake. That's a verdict with no human review. Lab 4 asked whether a doctor should have to sign off on an AI's diagnosis. Today you ask the same question about the no.

  
Before you argue about the machine, remember where the bias came from. Not from a computer. People drew the redlining maps in the 1930s. People read paper résumés and called back some names far more than others. Every model you trained today learned its bias from decisions people had already made. So the real choice in 2031 isn't between AI and a fair world. It's between imperfect systems, and the question is which one gets caught when it's wrong.


  

    

      
Side A · left half of the room

      
For: a person reviews every no

      
No AI or algorithm gets the last word on a job or a loan. An independent person, not the vendor and not the team that built the model, reviews every rejection before it's final and can overturn it. Start from the 1:50 a.m. email, iTutorGroup's automatic cutoffs, and the bias you found hiding in the labels today.

      
The cost to name: millions of reviews and slower answers for everyone. And the reviewer is a person, the same kind of decision-maker the model learned its bias from.

    

    

      
Side B · right half of the room

      
Against: audit the system, not every decision

      
Let the model decide, but audit it every year with a matched pair and the four-fifths rule, and require reasons for every no, the way lending already does. People ran the old system, and the old system was biased. Start from the résumé study, the Berkeley mortgage study, and the fact that your models learned their bias from people.

      
The cost to name: the person in the invisible pile never gets a human look, and an audit only catches the tests someone thought to run.

    

  

  
Nobody in this debate is arguing about a real applicant. You're arguing about a rule, the way a legislator or a company's board would.

  
The evidence below is from today. Use it to argue about 2031. Side A: you have to show a human reviewer would do better than the humans who built the biased history. Side B: you have to show an audit would catch what a reviewer would have caught, for the person in the pile.


  
What 2031 could look like: four scenarios

  
Each scenario starts from the status quo, how it works today, then imagines 2031. Every benefit and issue is grounded in something that has already happened or been measured. The first two are Side A's world; the last two are Side B's. Use them in your prep, and steal from the other side's.


  

    
Every no gets a person

    
Side A's world: mandatory review

    
Today: a screener can reject you at 1:50 a.m. and no person ever sees your file. Before screeners, people read every résumé, and in a study published in 2004, résumés with white-sounding names got 50 percent more callbacks than identical ones with Black-sounding names. In 2031: the AI sorts, and a trained reviewer reads every rejection before it goes out.

    

      
Benefits
  • Someone answers for every no. iTutorGroup learned that "the software did it" isn't a defense: the company chose the tool, so the company paid.
  • A person looking at the rejected pile can see what a model never says out loud. iTutorGroup's age cutoffs would have been obvious to anyone who read the rejections.

      
Issues
  • Scale. Workday disclosed that its tools rejected applications numbering in the billions. That's a lot of reviewers.
  • The reviewer is a person, and people produced the 50 percent callback gap. A tired reviewer may also just click approve on whatever the model said.

    

  


  

    
The outside reviewer

    
Side A's world: mandatory review

    
Today: when an AI screener rejects someone, the employer can point at the vendor and the vendor can point at the employer. That's the fight in Mobley v. Workday. In 2031: the law requires an independent firm, paid by neither, to review every high-stakes rejection.

    

      
Benefits
  • Nobody grades their own homework. Amazon's engineers were the only ones who could see inside their model, and only insiders found the problem.
  • One firm sees thousands of companies' rejections, so it can spot a pattern no single employer would.

      
Issues
  • The reviewer sees only the file the model saw. A bank never learns whether the people it turned down would have repaid, and neither does the reviewer.
  • Someone pays for it, likely applicants and customers, and "independent" firms still compete for business from the companies they check.

    

  


  

    
The audited model

    
Side B's world: audit the system

    
Today: regulators have used the four-fifths rule to flag hiring since 1978, for people and machines alike, but most decisions, human or AI, are never audited at all. In 2031: no person reviews single decisions. Every year an outside auditor runs matched pairs and the four-fifths test and publishes the results.

    

      
Benefits
  • An audit sees patterns no single reviewer can. iTutorGroup was caught by one matched pair: same application, younger birthdate, an interview.
  • A model's bias is the same every time, so once it's found, it's fixed for everyone at once. A recruiter's bias changes with the day.

      
Issues
  • An audit only catches the tests someone thought to run. In section 3, the matched pair on graduation year came back clean while years of experience did the work of age.
  • Amazon couldn't prove its own model fair, even with full access, and scrapped it.

    

  


  

    
Reasons, not reviewers

    
Side B's world: audit the system

    
Today: since the 1970s, a lender that turns you down has had to tell you the main reasons. A job rejection doesn't have to tell you anything, whether a person said no or a machine did. In 2031: every automated rejection comes with its main reasons and a way to fix wrong data, and the model is audited every year.

    

      
Benefits
  • You can act on a reason. "Short credit history" tells you what to change.
  • The CFPB has told lenders that "our model is too complex to explain" isn't an excuse, so a reasons rule pushes companies toward models people can check.

      
Issues
  • The reasons come from the same model. If its label was biased, the reasons explain the bias instead of fixing it.
  • A reason for one no can't show you a pattern. Only the pile can.

  • The four-fifths rule: if one group's selection rate is under 80 percent of the top group's, the process gets a closer look. It flags; it doesn't prove (29 CFR 1607.4).
    • 
        
  • The adverse action notice: lenders must give the main reasons for a denial, and in 2022 the CFPB said a model being too complex to explain isn't an excuse (CFPB).
    •

  1. Get your side (2 min). Left half Side A, right half Side B. Groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4.
    2.

    3. 
      
  3. Team up (3 min). Join one or two other groups on your side, so your team is 6 or 7 people: three teams per side. Pick your single best point, an opener, and a rebutter.

Rounds, 22 minutes.

    
    
      
  1. Openings (6 min). A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3. About 45 seconds each: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your plan.
    2. 
      
  2. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    3. 
      
  3. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first this time. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
    4.
