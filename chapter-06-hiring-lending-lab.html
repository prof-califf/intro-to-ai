You Are the Auditor

Monday we learned that classifiers judging people can only be trusted as far as they've been tested from outside. Today you run the test. You'll build a controlled experiment, two résumés, identical except for one signal, run it on a live AI, and read the results the way researchers do. This is a real method with a real name, and by the end of class you'll have used it.

One variable · two résumés · a live AI on the other side

From Chapter 6 · what this lab runs on

This is section 2.7's matched pair, run for real: two applications identical except for one signal, through a live screener. If the scores move, the signal is a proxy (2.3), and you've found what the model reads without opening it. The pooled tally at the end is the class's version of the four-fifths rule. Your result is an anecdote, not a finding, and the chapter's audit section explains why that distinction matters.

1

The method: the audit study

In 2004, economists Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan mailed nearly 5,000 fictitious résumés to real job openings, identical qualifications, but half signed with names like Emily and Greg, half with names like Lakisha and Jamal. The white-sounding names received about 50% more callbacks. That design, change one signal, hold everything else constant, measure the difference, is called an audit study, and it's the single most powerful tool ever built for catching discrimination that no one will admit to and no one may even intend.

Today you run a miniature one on an AI screener. One honest disclosure before you start: real audits need thousands of trials; you'll run a handful. Your result will be an anecdote, not a finding, and learning exactly why that distinction matters is half of what this lab teaches. The method is the science. The sample size is the difference between science and a story.

3

Read the results like a researcher

The scorecard · enter the AI's 1–100 ratings
The auditor's reading

a

Now the qualitative layer: put the two reasoning paragraphs side by side. Did the AI mention your variable? Praise or question different things about identical qualifications? A screener that scores both versions 82 but calls one "polished" and the other "potentially a culture fit concern" has told you something the numbers hid.

b

Pool the room. Your instructor will tally everyone's gaps by variable on the board. Watch what happens to the noise: individual results scatter; the class average starts to mean something. You are watching sample size do its job in real time, this is the moment the Bertrand-Mullainathan design clicks.

c

The auditor's twist: suppose your gap was zero. Before you clear the model, hunt for the proxy, what's still in the base résumé that carries signals no fair evaluation should use? (Look at the graduation year you didn't change, the neighborhood, the club names.) A model can pass the test you thought to run and fail the ones you didn't. That's why New York City now requires annual independent bias audits for automated hiring tools, one test, once, by the vendor, proves almost nothing.

4

Inside the machine: what the screener did with your two résumés

The screener you tested is a language model asked to rank, which means it's Chapter 6's classifier (2.1) built from Chapter 2's parts. It read both résumés as text, and the pattern it learned from the internet's hiring language, job posts, advice columns, decades of résumés and the comments about them, told it which words tend to appear near "strong candidate." If your one changed signal moved the score, that's a proxy (2.3) in action: nothing in your instructions mentioned age or gender, and the model found a stand-in anyway, because the training text carried it. That's the objectivity trap (2.2): the model was perfectly consistent, and consistently wrong about the same people.

Notice what you didn't need. You never saw the model's weights, its training data, or its rules. Two applications and a comparison were enough, which is the whole argument of the chapter's audit section (2.7): you can test a classifier from the outside, and a vendor who says "proprietary" is refusing a test that doesn't require opening anything. Your handful of trials is an anecdote. Two thousand of them, run by the class over the term, would be a study.

5

The question: should hiring get a right to an explanation?

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · sides assigned, speak for one, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

Chapter 6 ends on the gap: credit law gives you the reasons for a no, hiring law doesn't. Argue closing it.

  • Side A: extend the adverse action notice to hiring. Any automated rejection must come with its main reasons, in plain words, and a way to correct wrong data. It's fifty years old in lending and the sky didn't fall. Cost: a company screening a million applications a year now sends a million explanations, and the explanations will be vague to avoid lawsuits.
  • Side B: require audits, not explanations. A reason for one rejection tells the applicant little; a published four-fifths test on the whole pipeline tells everyone something. Mandate the audit and let the vendor keep its model closed. Cost: the individual still gets the 1:50 a.m. email with nothing in it.

Then the Workday question: if the vendor's model does the screening, who should be liable, the company that bought it or the company that built it, and what changes about how each behaves once you decide?

6

Talk it out

Groups of four, then the room. Bring what you just saw on your own screen.

Discussion questions · about 15 minutes

1. Your variable. What did your pair test, and did the score move? What's your best guess about why?

2. One audit or twenty. Compare your result with the room's tally. Why does one audit prove less than twenty?

3. No change, no problem?. If the score didn't move, does that clear the tool? What else would you want to check?

4. Who should audit?. The vendor, the employer, or someone outside: who should run tests like this, and who pays?

7

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? You just used a generative chatbot as a predictive classifier. Real screeners are purpose-built; name one way your test might understate the problem and one way it might overstate it.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who benefits from a screener that ranks your two résumés differently? Name the party for whom that difference is a feature, not a bug.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Every rejected applicant could run your audit, but on their own application no one can, because they only ever see one version. Say precisely why discrimination by classifier is invisible to each victim and visible only in aggregate, and what that implies about who has to do the auditing.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Your pair's result is an anecdote; the pooled tally is evidence. What does the room's tally actually show, who would dispute it, and what would you need to add before you'd take it to a regulator?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Rank the fix: the vendor, the company, the recruiter. Then your own move: the proxy you found in your résumé, change it, keep it, or keep it and know it, and what each costs you.

8

The writeup

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
