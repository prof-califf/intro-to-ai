In class: Teach it to sort (a spam filter, the simplest classifier)

A classifier sorts things into categories by learning from past examples that someone already labeled. Your email's spam filter is one. Before you run a classifier on people, train one on something harmless: ten old emails, labeled by you. About 10 minutes.

Ten invented emails and eight more to test. Nothing is saved.

That's all a classifier is. Labeled examples go in, weights come out, every new case gets a score, and a cutoff turns the score into a decision. Two things to carry into section 2.

It learned from your labels, not from the truth. When you flipped one label, the filter believed you. It had no other way to know.

The cutoff picks which mistake you'd rather make. When you changed the cutoff, the scores never changed. Only your line did. Set it strict and real email gets lost in spam, a false positive: the filter said "yes, spam" and was wrong. Set it easygoing and spam lands in your inbox, a false negative. You probably couldn't find a setting that got both to zero, and the ties show why. The professor's real email and the fake "verify your account" email can land on the same score. The filter only knows words, so no line can split them.

Every classifier that judges people has this same line, and someone picks where it goes. A bank that hates bad loans sets its line high and turns away more people who would have repaid. A company that hates bad hires does the same with good candidates. Nobody sees those mistakes, because the people on the wrong side of the line just disappear. That's section 2.4's invisible pile, and in section 2 you'll meet some of them.

2

In class: Same machine, car loans (Mini-Lender)

Now the emails become people. A small bank in an invented town has 160 past car loan applications from two neighborhoods, Hillside and the Flats. For each one, it knows two things: whether a loan officer approved it, and whether the borrower repaid. About 12 minutes.

One honest catch: in this lab, the bank somehow knows how every applicant would have done, including the ones it turned away. Real banks never know that. A denied applicant never gets the chance to repay.

Is this real?

The applicants are invented. The pattern isn't. In the 1930s, a federal agency graded city neighborhoods for mortgage risk, in large part by who lived there, and lenders followed those maps for decades (section 2.3; Mapping Inequality, University of Richmond). And when a Berkeley team compared U.S. mortgage borrowers with the same credit risk, Black and Latino borrowers still paid higher interest rates, more than $450 million a year in all (Bartlett and others). This lab's data is built to behave like that, with a neighborhood in place of race.

Riley, Morgan, and all 160 applicants are invented, built to behave the way the studies in Chapter 6 describe. Nothing is saved.

Step 1 is the objectivity trap. Nothing in the code says "deny the Flats." The officers' decisions said it, and the model copied them as closely as it could, every time, for every applicant (section 2.2).

Step 2 is the proxy. Most people in the Flats apply at the Meridian branch, the one near home, so "branch" told the model most of what "neighborhood" did. Deleting the field didn't delete the pattern (section 2.3).

Step 3 only works because this is a toy. You can see all four fields, so you could find the proxy. Amazon's engineers couldn't be sure they had found all of theirs, which is why they scrapped their tool (Reuters).

So what would actually fix it? Not deleting fields. The model learned from the officers' decisions, so it learned their bias. You'll see the deeper fix in Mini-Screener: change what the model learns from.

In class: Same machine, résumés (Mini-Screener)

Same machine, new pile. Nearly every Fortune 500 company runs applications through tracking software, and in a Harvard Business School survey, more than 90 percent of employers using these systems let them make the first cut or rank applicants before a person looks (Harvard Business School). You'll put one applicant through a scanner like that: first a keyword match against the job posting, then words it learned from past hires, then a fit score trained on 160 past applicants for analyst jobs. The fit score never sees age. You, the auditor, can: half of those 160 applicants are 40 or older. About 15 minutes.

Is this real?

The applicants are invented. The pattern isn't. iTutorGroup set its hiring software to automatically reject women 55 and older and men 60 and older, and paid $365,000 to settle with the EEOC (section 2.5; EEOC). In Mobley v. Workday, applicants over 40 claim a vendor's screening tools did something similar, more quietly. As of October 2026, the case is still in court, no court has found Workday liable, and Workday denies the claims (court docket). In this lab's data, the company's past recruiters called back older applicants far less often, though they did just as well on a work sample: callbacks, under 40 , 40 and older ; strong work samples, and .

Step 1 is the keyword gate. The scanner didn't judge whether Jordan could do the job. It counted matching words. Same person, same skills, and a few word swaps moved Jordan from rejected to passed. Step 2 is bias hiding in words. The scanner learned from past hires, and the past hires were mostly men, so a word about women became a penalty. That's the same objectivity trap as Mini-Lender: nothing in the code says "prefer men." The history said it.

Steps 3 and 4 are the proxy, again. When you deleted graduation year, the matched pair came back identical, and an auditor who only ran that one test would call the scanner clean. But the ratio stayed flagged, because years of experience was doing the work of age. A model can pass the test you thought to run and fail the one you didn't. That's why section 2.6 uses both tools: the matched pair finds what the model reads, and the four-fifths ratio checks what comes out.

Step 5 is the label, again. Trained on how people actually did on the work, the age gap mostly went away. But a real company only has work samples for the people it hired, which is the same catch as the bank.

How to use what you got

Your score is a rough guide, not a verdict. Every company's system is different, and many put a person in the loop. The best use of your scanner is finding skills you already have but describe differently.

Never claim what you can't back up. Stuffing in keywords you don't have might get you past a scanner, but not past an interview.

Remember what you saw in Mini-Screener. AI screeners can reward the wrong things, including details that have nothing to do with the job. Your scanner is a tool for you, not a judge of you.

After class: Under the hood, and why Claude's scanner is different

The spam filter, Mini-Lender, and Mini-Screener's fit score are the same small machine. The scanner you built with Claude is not, and that changes how you'd audit it.

A weight for every field. The spam filter had a weight for each word. Mini-Lender had one for income, credit history, neighborhood, and branch. Mini-Screener's fit score had one for each field, and you watched them as bars. The model adds up the weights for a case, turns the total into a score from 0 to 100, and a cutoff turns the score into yes or no. Those weights are the whole model.

Training is nudging. The lender and the screener start with every weight at zero. They guess on all 160 past cases, check each guess against the label column, nudge the weights toward agreeing, and repeat a few hundred times. This kind of model is called logistic regression, one of the oldest classifiers in business use. Credit scorecards have long been built this way, partly because the weights can be read off and turned into reasons, which is what lending's adverse action notice needs (section 2.5; CFPB).

Where the bias hid. Not in the code. The code was the same every time you switched. Look back at what you saw today, and the bias hid in three places.

In the label, which is an assumption. Mini-Lender assumed "approved" meant "creditworthy." Mini-Screener assumed "called back" meant "good candidate." Nobody wrote those assumptions down, but someone chose that column, and when you switched Mini-Screener's to the work sample, different people got in.

In the proxies. Branch carried neighborhood, and years of experience carried age, after the fields themselves were gone.

In what nobody measures. A real bank never learns whether the people it turned down would have repaid, so its model never learns from its mistakes about them.

Underneath all three sits one more assumption: that the past is a fair guide to the future. A model is a record of past decisions, made consistent. So when you audit one, ask what data it learned from and what it was trained to predict, not just what the code does.

A language model has no weight bars you can read. Claude's scanner read the whole résumé, the name and the frisbee club included, using patterns learned from a huge amount of human writing. You can't open it up and find the "graduation year" weight, and it can give different answers to the same résumé, which a scorecard never does. The audit tools still work, because the matched pair and the four-fifths ratio test what comes out, not what's inside. In 2024, University of Washington researchers did exactly that to three AI models with 3 million matched pairs, changing only the name, and found they preferred white-associated names 85 percent of the time (UW News).

Claude's part. Claude built a working AI scanner in a minute. It couldn't tell you whether that scanner was fair. The only way anyone finds that out is the way you did at the loan desk: change one thing and measure.

In class: Debate

The motion: A bank's audited model, not a loan officer, should make the final call on every car loan and mortgage. Left half of the room argues for it (Side A). Right half argues against it (Side B). About 45 minutes: 8 to read the history below, 12 to prep, 25 to debate. The Gilman Street rules apply.

This week’s case: Amazon’s résumé scanner

Amazon trained an AI scanner on about ten years of résumés, most of them from men. It taught itself to penalize the word “women’s” and to downgrade graduates of two all-women’s colleges. Amazon’s engineers edited out those terms, couldn’t be sure the model wouldn’t find other ways to discriminate, and scrapped it (Reuters).

Both sides can use it. Side A: the bias came from Amazon’s human hiring, and the model made it visible. Side B: the model took that bias, scaled it, and its own builders couldn’t fix it.

Before any algorithm: the human record

Before you argue about machines, look at how people did this job. Loans and jobs were being decided long before computers, and not fairly. This is the history every lending and hiring model learns from, including Amazon’s. Almost all of it comes from government investigations or peer-reviewed research.

  1. WhenWhat happenedSource
  2. 1930s

    Redlining

    A federal agency, the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation, graded city neighborhoods for lending risk, in large part by who lived there, and outlined the “hazardous” ones in red. Eighty years later, a 2018 study found 74 percent of those red neighborhoods are still low-to-moderate income, and nearly 64 percent are majority-minority.

    Mitchell, B., & Franco, J. (2018). HOLC “redlining” maps: The persistent structure of segregation and economic inequality. National Community Reinvestment Coalition. Open source

  3. 1947

    The GI Bill

    The government guaranteed home loans for returning World War II veterans, but local banks decided who actually got them. A 1947 Ebony magazine survey of 13 Mississippi cities found that of 3,229 VA-guaranteed home, business, and farm loans, 2 went to Black veterans.

    Katznelson, I. (2005). When affirmative action was white. W. W. Norton. Summarized by the author in History & Policy. Open source

  4. Before 1974

    Women and credit

    Lenders could require a woman to bring a man to co-sign a loan, even if she earned more than he did. When couples applied for a mortgage, banks often ignored the wife’s income, assuming she’d quit if she got pregnant. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 made that illegal.

    Smithsonian American Women’s History Museum. (2024). Voices on independence: Four oral histories about building women’s economic power. Equal Credit Opportunity Act, Pub. L. 93-495 (1974). Open source

  5. 1992

    The Boston Fed study

    Federal Reserve researchers pulled thousands of mortgage files from Boston-area lenders. Even after accounting for finances, jobs, and neighborhood, Black and Hispanic applicants were about 60 percent more likely to be turned down than white applicants. Lenders seemed more willing to overlook flaws on white applicants’ files. Critics argued over the method for years, and the study reshaped the national debate over lending discrimination.

    Munnell, A. H., Browne, L. E., McEneaney, J., & Tootell, G. M. B. (1992). Mortgage lending in Boston: Interpreting HMDA data (Working Paper 92-7). Federal Reserve Bank of Boston. Published in American Economic Review, 1996. Open source

  6. 2004

    The résumé study

    Economists sent identical résumés to real job ads in Boston and Chicago. Résumés with white-sounding names got 50 percent more callbacks than the same résumés with Black-sounding names.

    Bertrand, M., & Mullainathan, S. (2004). Are Emily and Greg more employable than Lakisha and Jamal? American Economic Review, 94(4), 991–1013. Open source

  7. 2011

    Countrywide

    The Justice Department said Countrywide let its loan officers and brokers set fees and rates by their own judgment, and that from 2004 to 2008 more than 200,000 qualified Black and Hispanic borrowers paid more than similar white borrowers, about 10,000 of them steered into subprime loans. It was settled for $335 million, at the time the largest fair lending settlement in history.

    U.S. Department of Justice. (2011, December 21). Justice Department reaches $335 million settlement to resolve allegations of lending discrimination by Countrywide Financial Corporation [Press release]. Open source

  8. 2021

    108 big employers

    Researchers sent more than 83,000 fake applications to 108 of the largest U.S. companies. Distinctively Black names were 2.1 percentage points less likely to hear back, about 8 percent fewer contacts. A small group of companies did most of it.

    Kline, P., Rose, E. K., & Walters, C. R. (2022). Systemic discrimination among large U.S. employers. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 137(4), 1963–2036. Open source

  9. 2023

    City National Bank

    The Justice Department said this Los Angeles bank avoided lending in majority Black and Latino neighborhoods from 2017 to 2020. It paid more than $31 million, the largest redlining settlement in the department’s history, while disagreeing with the claims.

    United States v. City National Bank, consent order (C.D. Cal. 2023). Also reported by NPR, January 12, 2023. Open source

Notice the pattern. Almost every case comes down to people with discretion: a local banker deciding which veteran gets the loan, a loan officer setting a rate “by judgment,” a lender overlooking flaws for some applicants and not others. Side A will lean on this record. Side B will point out that it’s exactly what models learn from.

After the algorithms arrived: when they did better than people

Once lenders and employers started using algorithms, researchers could compare them with the people they replaced, on the same kind of decision. Sometimes the algorithm won.

  1. WhenWhat the study foundSource
  2. 2009 to 2015

    Algorithmic lenders

    Comparing borrowers with the same credit risk, lenders that decided by algorithm had 27 to 37 percent smaller racial gaps in FHA mortgage pricing than face-to-face lenders.

    The catch: by 2018 and 2019 the edge held only for refinances. On most loans, the algorithms’ gaps matched everyone else’s.

    Bartlett, R., Morse, A., Stanton, R., & Wallace, N. (2022). Consumer-lending discrimination in the FinTech era. Journal of Financial Economics, 143(1), 30–56. Open source

  3. 2018

    Managers who overruled the test

    Researchers studied 15 companies that started using a job test for service workers. Hiring by the test made workers stay about 15 percent longer. When managers ignored the test and picked someone else, their hires were worse. The researchers concluded managers often overrule the test because they’re biased or mistaken, not because they know something the test doesn’t.

    The catch: it measured how long people stayed, not bias directly.

    Hoffman, M., Kahn, L. B., & Li, D. (2018). Discretion in hiring. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(2), 765–800. Open source

  4. 2018

    Judges versus an algorithm

    Using court data, researchers compared judges’ bail decisions with an algorithm’s predictions. The algorithm could have cut the jail population by up to 42 percent with no increase in crime, and in their data it did so while reducing racial disparities, without ever being given race.

    The catch: it’s a simulation of a different high-stakes decision, not lending or hiring.

    Kleinberg, J., Lakkaraju, H., Leskovec, J., Ludwig, J., & Mullainathan, S. (2018). Human decisions and machine predictions. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(1), 237–293. Open source

  5. 2020

    A résumé screener in the field

    In a field experiment hiring for white-collar jobs, a machine-learning résumé screener picked candidates who were 14 percent more likely to pass the interview and get an offer than the ones people picked. It also chose more nontraditional candidates, including women and racial minorities.

    The catch: one company, and a working paper rather than a published study.

    Cowgill, B. (2020). Bias and productivity in humans and algorithms: Theory and evidence from résumé screening [Working paper]. Columbia Business School. Open source

  6. 2025

    Hiring as exploration

    At a Fortune 500 firm, researchers built a screener that gave a bonus to applicants it knew less about. It picked better candidates than the human recruiters did and more than doubled the share of interviews going to Black and Hispanic applicants, from about 10 to 23 percent. It was never told to favor anyone.

    The catch, and it’s a big one: a standard screener trained on the same data also picked better candidates, but cut that share to under 5 percent.

    Li, D., Raymond, L., & Bergman, P. (2025). Hiring as exploration. Review of Economic Studies. Earlier version: NBER Working Paper 27736. Open source

Notice the pattern. Every time an algorithm beat people, someone designed it carefully and checked it against real outcomes.

After the algorithms arrived: when they made it worse

Other times, algorithms learned the bias, hid it, or spread it, Amazon’s included. Joy Buolamwini has a name for where much of it starts: the coded gaze.

  1. WhenWhat happenedSource
  2. 2015

    Amazon’s résumé scanner

    Amazon trained a scanner on about ten years of résumés, mostly from men. It learned to penalize the word “women’s” and to downgrade graduates of two all-women’s colleges. Amazon couldn’t be sure it had removed every bias and scrapped the project.

    Dastin, J. (2018, October 10). Amazon scraps secret AI recruiting tool that showed bias against women. Reuters. Open source

  3. 2016

    COMPAS: the risk score in court

    Judges in many U.S. courts see a COMPAS score, an algorithm’s guess at whether a defendant will commit another crime. ProPublica got the scores for more than 10,000 defendants in Broward County, Florida, and checked who actually reoffended. Black defendants who did not reoffend were labeled higher risk almost twice as often as white defendants who did not (45 percent vs. 23 percent). White defendants who did reoffend were labeled low risk almost twice as often (48 percent vs. 28 percent). The score never asked anyone’s race.

    The fight that followed: the company said its scores were equally accurate for both groups, about 60 percent, and that was true too. Researchers then showed that when two groups are arrested at different rates, no score can be equally accurate and make equal mistakes at the same time. Someone has to choose which kind of fair matters.

    Angwin, J., Larson, J., Mattu, S., & Kirchner, L. (2016, May 23). Machine bias. ProPublica. Open source
    Larson, J., et al. (2016). How we analyzed the COMPAS recidivism algorithm. ProPublica. Open source
    Corbett-Davies, S., Pierson, E., Feller, A., & Goel, S. (2016, October 17). A computer program used for bail and sentencing decisions was labeled biased against blacks. It’s actually not that clear. Washington Post. Open source

  4. 2018

    The coded gaze

    MIT researcher Joy Buolamwini noticed face-analysis software couldn’t reliably see her face until she put on a white mask. She calls this the “coded gaze”: the priorities and blind spots of the people who build a system, written into it. With Timnit Gebru, she tested three commercial systems from IBM, Microsoft, and Face++. Guessing gender from a photo, they were wrong up to 34.7 percent of the time for darker-skinned women, and never more than 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men. The photo sets used to build and test this kind of software were mostly lighter-skinned faces.

    The twist: within seven months of the study, all three companies released new versions with much smaller gaps. Amazon and another company that weren’t named still erred on up to 31 percent of darker-skinned women.

    Buolamwini, J. (2023). Unmasking AI. Random House; interview on NPR’s Fresh Air, November 28, 2023. Open source
    Buolamwini, J., & Gebru, T. (2018). Gender shades: Intersectional accuracy disparities in commercial gender classification. Proceedings of Machine Learning Research, 81, 77–91. Open source
    Raji, I. D., & Buolamwini, J. (2019). Actionable auditing. AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society. Open source

  5. 2018 to 2019

    Still charging more

    The same Berkeley study that found algorithmic lenders were fairer from 2009 to 2015 found that by 2018 and 2019, they still charged Black and Hispanic borrowers more for the same risk. On most loans the gap was about as large as other lenders’; only on refinances did they still do better.

    Bartlett, R., Morse, A., Stanton, R., & Wallace, N. (2022). Consumer-lending discrimination in the FinTech era. Journal of Financial Economics, 143(1), 30–56. Open source

  6. 2020

    Howard versus NYU

    A watchdog applied to an AI lender as the same borrower twice, changing only the college. As a Howard University graduate, the borrower was quoted about $3,499 more over a five-year, $30,000 loan than as an NYU graduate. The lender disputed the method.

    Student Borrower Protection Center. (2020). Educational redlining. As reported by NPR, February 5, 2020. Open source

  7. 2022

    Smarter, not fairer

    On U.S. mortgage data, machine-learning credit models predicted default better than older models, but Black and Hispanic borrowers were less likely to benefit, and gaps in interest rates grew. The models were never told anyone’s race.

    Fuster, A., Goldsmith-Pinkham, P., Ramadorai, T., & Walther, A. (2022). Predictably unequal? The effects of machine learning on credit markets. Journal of Finance, 77(1), 5–47. Open source

  8. 2024

    3 million matched pairs

    Researchers ran real résumés against real job listings through three AI models, changing only the first name. The models preferred white-associated names 85 percent of the time and Black-associated names 9 percent, and never preferred Black male names over white male names.

    Wilson, K., & Caliskan, A. (2024). Gender, race, and intersectional bias in résumé screening via language model retrieval. AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society. Summarized by UW News. Open source

  9. 2025

    The standard screener

    At a Fortune 500 firm, a standard résumé screener trained on past hiring picked candidates who were more likely to get hired, but cut the share of interviews going to Black and Hispanic applicants from about 10 percent to under 5 percent.

    Li, D., Raymond, L., & Bergman, P. (2025). Hiring as exploration. Review of Economic Studies. Earlier version: NBER Working Paper 27736. Open source

Notice the pattern. None of these algorithms was told to discriminate. They learned it from past decisions or found it through clues like a college or a name, and nobody outside would have known without someone running a test. Side A will lean on the first of these two records, Side B on the second.

Side A · left half

For: the model makes the call

The bank’s model decides every application. An outside auditor tests it every year, in public, and every no comes with its main reasons.

What you have to prove: that a model can be caught and fixed faster than a person, even though Amazon couldn’t fix theirs.

Side B · right half

Against: a person makes the call

A trained loan officer makes the final decision. A model can give a score, but a person signs, and writes down why when they go against it.

What you have to prove: that your loan officers would be fairer than the people who built the record below.

Talking points: your group takes one

Each point has a claim, the real evidence behind it, and the comeback to expect. The tags open the full record, with sources.

Side A: the model decides

1

“People were biased first, and they still are.”

The evidenceIn 1947, of 3,229 VA-guaranteed loans in 13 Mississippi cities, 2 went to Black veterans. In 1992, Black and Hispanic applicants in Boston were about 60 percent more likely to be turned down than white applicants with similar finances. In 2011, Countrywide paid $335 million after the government said its loan officers’ own judgment cost 200,000 Black and Hispanic borrowers more. The human record

Expect them to say“And every model learns from exactly that history. Ask Amazon.”

2

“Algorithms were fairer than face-to-face lenders.”

The evidenceComparing borrowers with the same credit risk, algorithmic lenders had 27 to 37 percent smaller racial gaps in FHA mortgage pricing than face-to-face lenders from 2009 to 2015. The algorithm record

Expect them to say“By 2018 and 2019 the edge held only for refinances. On most loans, algorithmic lenders charged minority borrowers as much as everyone else.”

3

“When people overrule the tool, they usually get it wrong.”

The evidenceAcross 15 companies using a hiring test, managers who ignored the test and picked someone else made worse hires. The researchers concluded managers often overrule the test because they’re biased or mistaken. The algorithm record

Expect them to say“That study measured how long people stayed, not whether the test was fair.”

4

“A model’s bias can be found. A person’s mostly can’t.”

The evidenceAmazon’s own engineers found the “women’s” penalty by examining their model. After the Gender Shades study named IBM, Microsoft, and Face++, all three cut their face-analysis error gaps within seven months. Measuring human recruiters’ bias took researchers more than 83,000 fake applications to 108 companies. Amazon The human record

Expect them to say“Amazon found one bias and still couldn’t be sure it had found them all. That’s why it scrapped the tool.”

5

“Built carefully, an algorithm opens the door wider.”

The evidenceAt a Fortune 500 firm, a carefully designed screener picked better candidates than human recruiters and more than doubled interviews for Black and Hispanic applicants, from about 10 to 23 percent. In bail decisions, an algorithm could have cut jailing by up to 42 percent with no rise in crime while reducing racial disparities. The algorithm record

Expect them to say“The same study’s standard screener cut that share to under 5 percent. Who makes sure the bank builds the careful one?”

Side B: a person decides

1

“A model copies its teachers, at scale.”

The evidenceAmazon trained its scanner on about ten years of résumés, mostly from men. Nobody told it to prefer men. It learned to penalize “women’s” and to downgrade graduates of two all-women’s colleges anyway. Amazon

Expect them to say“Those were human recruiters’ decisions. People were the problem, and the model exposed it.”

2

“You can’t just delete the bias.”

The evidenceAmazon edited its model to ignore those specific words, but couldn’t guarantee it wouldn’t find other ways to discriminate, so it shut the project down. Mortgage models found patterns tracking race without ever being told anyone’s race. The other record

Expect them to say“And you can’t delete a loan officer’s bias either. At least the model can be tested.”

3

“Smarter isn’t the same as fairer.”

The evidenceMachine-learning credit models predicted default better than older models, but Black and Hispanic borrowers were less likely to benefit, and rate gaps grew. A standard résumé screener at a Fortune 500 firm picked better candidates but cut interviews for Black and Hispanic applicants to under 5 percent. And a risk score used in courts wrongly flagged Black defendants who didn’t reoffend as high risk almost twice as often as white defendants. The other record

Expect them to say“The same researchers built a different screener that more than doubled those interviews. Design matters.”

4

“AI still sees some people worse than others.”

The evidenceIn 2024, researchers ran real résumés through three AI models with only the first name changed. Across 3 million comparisons, the models preferred white-associated names 85 percent of the time. Earlier, commercial face-analysis systems misread darker-skinned women up to 34.7 percent of the time and lighter-skinned men at most 0.8 percent: the coded gaze. The other record

Expect them to say“Those were open-source research models the companies said weren’t built for hiring.”

5

“When someone tested a real AI lender, it failed.”

The evidenceA watchdog applied to an AI lender as the same borrower twice, changing only the college. As a Howard University graduate, the borrower was quoted about $3,499 more over a five-year, $30,000 loan than as an NYU graduate. The other record

Expect them to say“The lender disputed the method, and it was a handful of test applications.”

Prep, 12 minutes
  1. Get your point (1 min). Groups of 2 or 3. Count off 1 to 5 on your side. That’s your talking point.
  2. Check the evidence (3 min). Click the tag on your point to jump to its timeline, reread the entries behind it, and open at least one source.
  3. Pressure-test it with Claude (5 min). Fill in the brackets and send:
I'm in a class debate on this motion: a bank's audited model, not a loan officer, should make the final call on every car loan and mortgage. I'm on Side [A, the model decides / B, a person decides]. My point is: [paste the claim]. My evidence is: [paste the evidence]. Then argue against me as hard as you can: what's the other side's best answer, and where is my point weakest? Don't go easy on me.

If Claude cites a study, check it against the records above. Then close it. You can’t read from Claude during the debate.

  1. Write your notes (3 min), in your own words: your point in one sentence, the evidence, the comeback you expect, and your answer to it.

No AI? Skip step 3. Instead, read two entries in the other side’s record and write the strongest thing they’ll say to you.

Rounds, 25 minutes
  1. Team up (3 min). Join the other groups on your side. Pick your best two points, an opener, and a rebutter.
  2. Openings (6 min). Side A, then Side B. About 45 seconds each: your claim, your evidence, and its source.
  3. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
  4. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
  5. Switch (4 min). What was the other side’s best point? Someone new argues it in one sentence.
  6. What would change your mind? (4 min). Name the specific evidence you’d have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

To resolve it: Amazon’s scanner learned its bias from Amazon’s own hiring. Is a model a fairer decider than the people it learned from, or the same bias running faster? Name one decision you’d give a model, one you’d keep for a person, and how you’d catch each one being unfair.

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

The three tools (sections 1 to 3)
Self-contained, no network calls, nothing persists. Every email, applicant, and number is invented. The loan and résumé data are generated from fixed seeds, so every student sees the same 160 people, and every rate on the page is computed from that data, not typed in. The data was built so both groups repay, or do well on the work sample, at about the same rate, while past officers and recruiters approved one group far less often. Mini-Lender and Mini-Screener train a logistic regression by gradient descent; Mini-Spam counts words. Mini-Screener's learned-word weights are invented to mirror the Amazon case.
