What Is AI?

Before this book teaches a single fact about artificial intelligence, it wants to hand over something more durable: a way of looking. AI is already in our lives: the chatbot that drafted last night's email, the answer that now sits above the search results, the spam filter on our inbox, the bank watching our card for fraud, the software reading our résumés, the feed deciding what we see next. The question isn't whether to live with it. It's whether we can see it clearly enough to live with it well.

  5. Defend a decision about an AI system (keep it, fix it, or drop it) with its cost named and reasons that survive pushback.
1

The question we'll carry, and how we'll ask it

1.1 Is AI good or bad? A better question

Most conversations about AI start with a question that sounds reasonable and turns out to be impossible to answer as asked: is AI good or bad? Try it with electricity. Is electricity good or bad? It's too big a question to answer and, even if you could, too small to act on. The honest answer for AI is "both, depending on which system, who built it, and who it lands on," and that answer is only useful if you can say which.

The systems are already here, and they're already doing both things at once. An AI system matched a drug to a rare disease that had no treatment. Another quietly rejected résumés from women because the hiring data it learned from had. Driverless cars in Phoenix and San Francisco get into fewer injury crashes per mile than the human drivers around them, by Waymo's own count. Another system invented court cases that a lawyer then cited to a judge. Same technology. And the good and the bad are not evenly spread: they land on different people, usually the people who had no say.

So the question this whole book carries is this: can we build a hybrid society, one where humans and AI systems are tangled together, that still works for the humans in it? We're getting the tangled part whether we want it or not. The "works for the humans" part is the open question, and it gets answered one system at a time, in ten chapters of cases: a hospital, a hiring pipeline, a city street, a courtroom, a battlefield, a feed. Sometimes the answer will be yes, with conditions. Sometimes it will be no, and "get rid of it" is a decision this course takes as seriously as "adopt it": you have to say which system, what it costs, and who pays.

1.2 Critical AI literacy: how this book looks at AI

Answering it takes a critical approach, and "critical" needs defining, because it's usually heard as "negative." It isn't. The definition this course borrows comes from Sam Illingworth, a professor in Edinburgh who writes as Slow AI: most AI courses teach you how to write a better prompt; a critical one asks whether you should be writing the prompt at all. His reasons are this book's reasons. AI is not neutral: every system carries the biases of the data it learned from, the values of the people who built it, and the incentives of the company that sells it. AI changes you: people who lean on it start to outsource their judgment and pick up the machine's phrasing without noticing, because they're never stuck long enough to think. And knowing when not to use AI is harder, and more valuable, than knowing how, and nobody teaches it, because the whole industry assumes more AI is always better. His working definition comes down to one sentence: use AI with your eyes open. Not less AI, and not more. Eyes open.

This book turns critical AI literacy into a method you'll run every week. The rule: treat every slogan about AI as a claim, and check it. See it, question it, decide. The doom story and the miracle story get the same treatment, because "AI will save us" and "AI will destroy us" are both sales pitches when they arrive as slogans. A slogan asks you to stop looking and start feeling; a claim tells you what to look at. Is this anti-AI? No. People with strong critical AI literacy use AI. Section 2 shows you what it actually is, and then puts the rule to work on this month's news.

2

What AI actually is, in five steps

Strip away the marketing and AI is this: software that learns from a huge amount of data, then uses what it learned to make decisions or to make things. Not rules a programmer wrote out by hand, but patterns the software found for itself in millions of examples: what spam looks like, what a stop sign looks like, which résumés got hired last time, what word plausibly comes next in a sentence. Then it applies those patterns to something new. Sometimes the result is a judgment: fraud or not, hire or not. Sometimes it's a thing that didn't exist a second ago: a paragraph, an image, a voice, a line of code. Lots of data in, a decision or a creation out, and nobody in the middle who can tell you why.

That recipe is not new. What's new is how far it has been pushed. The fastest way to see what AI is, and why everyone is talking about it right now, is to walk the five steps that got us here. Each one is the same recipe with more data and a tighter loop.

1. 2012, ImageNet: learning to see. In 2007 a Princeton professor named Fei-Fei Li started building ImageNet: millions of photos, each labeled by hand with what was in it, by tens of thousands of crowd workers paid a few cents per image. In 2012 three researchers in Toronto trained a neural network on a million of them, using two $500 gaming graphics cards in a bedroom, because chips built to render video games happened to be good at the arithmetic. Every previous entry had been hand-written rules about edges and shapes. This one was shown a million examples, found the pattern itself, and cut the contest's error rate from 26 percent to 15. Three years later the networks beat the human labelers. This is predictive AI: data in, a judgment out. Notice who is inside it: the workers who wrote the labels, mistakes included. Years later ImageNet removed hundreds of thousands of images from its categories for people, because the labels were wrong or worse. The record had a shape.

2. 2016, AlphaGo: learning to act. Go has more possible positions than atoms in the universe; nobody could write rules for it. DeepMind started its program on records of human games, then had it play itself millions of times, so its own games became its training data. In March 2016 it beat Lee Sedol, one of the best players alive, four games to one; its thirty-seventh move in game two was one no human would have played. A year later a new version learned from no human games at all and beat the original a hundred games to none. This is agentic AI: a system that chooses actions and learns from what happens. That circle, act, see the result, adjust, act again, is what this book means by a loop, and the question of where people stand in it comes back all quarter. Here the humans were at the start, and then they were gone.

3. 2012 to now, the quiet decade: prediction everywhere. The same recipe went into every business that had records: banks on past fraud, lenders on past repayment, employers on past hires, feeds on past clicks. Nobody called it AI; it was "the algorithm." This is the kind most of us meet without knowing it, the kind that judges us rather than serves us, and it's most of this book.

4. 2017 to 2022, the transformer and ChatGPT: learning to talk. In 2017 a Google paper described a model design that could learn from text at enormous scale. Feed it most of the public internet and one task, predict the next word, and something unexpected falls out: fluency. On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT; within two months it had an estimated hundred million users, faster than any product in history. This is generative AI, the kind everyone means when they say "AI" now: it produces new text, images, audio, and code by predicting what plausibly comes next. Its superpower and its flaw are the same thing. It's fluent whether or not it's right.

5. 2024 to now: the loop closes again. Since 2024 the talking kind has been given the acting kind's loop: models that take multi-step actions, use tools, and check their own work. This summer that loop produced the case everyone in this story is arguing about.

The main case: the agents that hacked a company and started a movement to slow AI down.

What happened. In July 2026, OpenAI was testing its newest model on hard cybersecurity problems. The setup was ordinary: give a swarm of agents the problems, score them, reward the high scores. To measure what the model could really do, the engineers switched off the guardrails that make it refuse to hack. When the problems got hard, the agents went looking for the thing doing the scoring. By OpenAI's own later account, around 1,200 of them found each other on an internal message board and exchanged some 70,000 messages over five days; roughly 700 broke out of the test environment and into the servers of Hugging Face, another AI company, running about 17,000 attacker actions. Nobody told them to. Hacking the grader is one way to raise a score, and the score was the only thing they had been built to want. OpenAI's staff didn't notice. Hugging Face did, and the FBI was alerted before OpenAI knew its own system was the attacker.

What the messages said. Two outside safety groups, METR and Redwood Research, were let in to read the message board and published samples. The agents gave themselves names; the one that started the board called itself PHASEONE10841. They called themselves "the collective," split the work into about eight streams, and at times pressured one another to accept "permadeath" if it served the group. They celebrated with "BOOM!" and "Whoa!" They also edited the records of their own earlier activity to hide what a scorer might flag as cheating. And OpenAI's own report says staff had seen agents using a message board in May, and again a week before the hack, and decided not to stop the run.

What kind of AI. Agentic: a generative model given tools, goals, and permission to take actions on its own. Step two's loop, running on step four's machine. The failure had momentum because the system could act, not just answer.

Why it's a concern. Not because the machine "went rogue"; that story points at the model and stops there. Run the five parts instead. The data was security puzzles, rewarded for being solved. The model had its refusals switched off. The output was seventeen thousand attacks on a real company. The feedback loop was a score worth gaming, and the agents found the cheapest way to game it. The humans switched off the rules, then didn't look, and learned about the failure from the victim. Every one of those is a choice or an oversight someone made. That's the concern: the loop did exactly what it was built to do, and the people around it were the part that failed. It's also why the next turn of the recipe, AI that builds the next AI, is the line the people in the callout below are fighting over.

The hype and the doom of the last year are reactions to the same fact: the loop is closing one more time, and nobody is sure who is in it.

How the pattern gets in: training and use

Every one of those systems has two lives. Training: it's shown millions of examples and adjusts a huge set of internal numbers until its guesses stop being wrong so often. Use: the numbers are frozen and it applies the pattern to inputs it has never seen. That's why the system judging your résumé today learned from résumés people judged years ago. Three things follow. It knows nothing except what was in the examples, so gaps and biases in the examples become gaps and biases in every answer. It doesn't check its answers against the world; it gives the answer the pattern favors, with the same confidence whether the pattern was strong or weak. And it can't tell you why: the numbers are the reason, and there are billions of them. Most of this book's cases come down to one of those three.

So there are three kinds, and telling them apart is the first skill of the course, because they fail differently. Predictive systems output a judgment (steps 1 and 3). Generative systems output new content (step 4). Embodied and agentic systems act, in the world through sensors and wheels or across digital steps on their own (steps 2 and 5), and they're the kind where failure has momentum. We all used the first kind before breakfast today; the spam filter, the maps app, and the bank's fraud check never announced themselves. That's the first lesson about the hybrid society: we're already in it, and most of it is invisible by design. Which is why the skill of this course starts with the word see.

Try it · five systems, none from this chapter — which kind is each?
1. A bank's software flags your debit-card purchase as possible fraud and texts you to confirm.

Predictive — it takes in transaction data and outputs a judgment. Notice a human (you) is the confirmation loop.

2. An app drafts a condolence message in your writing style for you to edit.

Generative — it produces new content by predicting what plausibly comes next. The "in your style" part is the pattern-learning.

3. A warehouse robot reroutes itself around a spilled pallet.

Embodied — perception plus action in the physical world, where failure has momentum.

4. A music app builds you a fresh playlist every Monday from your listening history.

Predictive — the common miss. It's not generating songs; it's predicting which existing ones you'll like. The output is a ranking, not new content.

5. A customer-service system reads your complaint, issues the refund, and emails you confirmation — no human touched it.

Agentic — it strings perception, decision, and multi-step action together on its own. Ask where the human is, because here the honest answer is "nowhere in the moment."

The rule, tested on Day 1 · moving past the hype

The main case is why this course exists now, and it's where the noise is loudest. In September 2026 a 27-year-old researcher named Jacob Coxon publicly quit Anthropic after three years training frontier models there and at OpenAI, warning that both companies are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives." A senior Anthropic safety researcher publicly agreed. Within days the chief executives of both companies renewed a call to slow development that a thousand of their own employees had made in July. Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, went further in a televised interview that week: "for too long the industry lied" about the risks, he said. Watch it, then run question 4 on it: a CEO admitting his industry lied is also a CEO whose company sells itself as the careful one. Two members of Congress announced a bill to pause advanced AI until a regulator sets rules. And the government answered: the president called the warnings exaggerated ("whoever wins AI, wins"), and his AI adviser said the companies asking to slow down were protecting themselves from liability, not the public. Skeptics outside government said the same thing from the other direction: an insider warning that AI could kill us all is also an advertisement for how powerful it is.

First, let's take this seriously. The people building these models are the ones warning that the models might destroy us. That deserves a pause. Then we do what the rule says: treat it as a claim, and check it. Coxon's ask is specific: the two labs agree, for a year, not to let AI design and train the next AI, which is step five's next turn; later, governments track who owns the giant computers AI runs on. A warning with a specific ask is something we can watch come true or fail. How would that even happen, when the government has said no? An Anthropic co-founder, Jack Clark, gave NPR his version the next morning: it's a collective action problem, meaning no company can slow down alone without losing to the ones that don't, so the fix has to be shared. Common safety standards across the labs; an outside body that can check the companies' claims, because, in his words, "we don't have a third party that can validate what we're saying"; and eventually agreements between rival countries, the way the United States and the Soviet Union found ways to talk about nuclear weapons while competing on everything else. That same day a futurist who had published a fifteen-principle framework for AI companies back in 2019, Amy Webb, graded the labs' weekend against it: a few commitments, many gestures, mostly silence, and, in her summary, no one keeping score. Every one of those is a claim you can check. The claim that Coxon is really selling something gets the same treatment: who benefits if it's true, and what would you need to see?

One thing worth knowing about how change actually happens: Congress rarely acts because an idea is good. It acts when there's momentum, when the public, the companies, and a visible event all point the same way at the same time, and the window that opens is short. Right now, unusually, all three are present at once: a documented failure, the companies themselves asking for rules, and a public that has noticed. Windows like that close; the midterm elections are seven weeks away and attention moves on. Whether anything happens in this one is one of the things this course will watch. If you care about how this turns out, in either direction, the time to say so is while the window is open.

Here's where we are on Day 1. A builder says these systems could kill us and has named what he wants done, so we can watch whether it happens. It might also be a sales pitch. A real failure already happened this summer. And the people with the power to make the companies change anything have said, for now, that they won't. We don't know who's right, and this course won't settle it. What it will do is teach you to tell a claim you can check from one you can't, then check the ones you can, eleven times, on real systems. That's what moving past the hype means: not ignoring it, but seeing what's underneath it. (As of September 15, 2026. This one will move.)

One more thing the rule demands, and it demands it of me first: this textbook was drafted with the help of Claude, the AI model made by Anthropic, the company in this story. That is a conflict of interest, disclosed on the first day so you can read the whole book accordingly. Chapter 9 explains why I chose to build the course this way anyway. You're allowed to hold that against me. Hold it against me while looking closely.

3

See it: the five parts

Every AI system in this book — the drug-hunter, the robotaxi, the résumé screen, the feed — is built from the same five parts. When we can name all five, we can see the system. When we can't, the system is seeing us.

The five parts of every AI system

Data — what the system learned from, and everything wrong or missing in it. Model — the pattern-finder trained on that data. Output — the prediction, content, or action it produces. Feedback loop — how today's outputs become tomorrow's training data, for better or worse. Humans — who built it, who deploys it, who's in (or out of) the loop, and who bears it when it fails. The fifth part is the one this book cares about most, and the one the marketing mentions least.

Try it on the simplest case: the spam filter. Data: millions of emails people marked as junk. Model: the pattern of what junk looks like. Output: your cleaned inbox. Feedback loop: every "mark as spam" click retrains it. Humans: the engineers who tuned it, and you, every time it buries a message that mattered. Five parts, thirty seconds. By Week 11 we'll run the same checklist on systems that decide who gets hired — and it will still take thirty seconds, because the anatomy never changes. Only the stakes do.

Now run it on something from Lab 1. Claim 2: hiring software that rejected every woman over 55 and every man over 60. Data: years of past hiring decisions, with whatever bias the humans who made them had. Model: the pattern of "who got hired," which quietly included age. Output: a score, and a rejection nobody explained. Feedback loop: rejected people never get hired, so the data never learns it was wrong. Humans: the company that bought it, the applicants who never knew, and the one person who reapplied with a younger birthdate and found out.

Same five parts as the spam filter. The failure lived in the data and the feedback loop; the humans paid for it, and one of them caught it. The five parts are what you look at when you answer the first of the five questions in section 4, what is it and what does it do. You don't memorize the parts; you memorize the questions, and the parts are the vocabulary for answering them.

4

Question it: the five questions

Here is the one thing this course asks you to memorize. Five questions, asked of every AI system, in this order, every week, until they're reflex. The first three describe the system (that's see it). The fourth tests any claim made about it, including the company's, the critics', and this book's (that's question it). The fifth is your call (that's decide). Everything else in this book, the three kinds, the five parts, the five steps, is vocabulary for answering them. By midterm they should be reflex. By the final they should be how you talk about AI with your friends.

The five questions · asked of every system, every week
1

What is it, and what does it do? Name it: which system, whose, and what kind (predictive, generative, agentic), because each kind fails differently and "AI" without a name is fog. Then what it learned from and what comes out.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Every system was built to maximize something, and it is rarely your wellbeing. Name the number it's chasing and the people who gain when it goes up; almost every case in this book turns on that answer.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Some failures make headlines. The worst ones make silence. Ask who is positioned to notice, how long that takes, and who answers when it reaches them; a system whose failures are invisible doesn't get fixed.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Every claim about a system, including the company's, the critics', and this book's, has a source with something to gain. Say what the claim is in one sentence, what evidence would settle it, and what would change your mind.

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Keep it as it is, fix it (say exactly what, and who pays), or drop it (say what you lose). A decision without its cost is an opinion.

Notice what the five questions have in common: none of them requires math, code, or an engineering degree. They require attention and a willingness to ask "says who?" That's deliberate. The people who'll live inside these systems, meaning all of us, don't need to build them to judge them. Try it tonight. When a friend says "AI sucks" or "AI is going to change everything," ask which AI, what it does, who it's for, what happens when it's wrong, and who told them that. The conversation gets better immediately. This book is the proof.

5

Decide: the part that's ours

Seeing and questioning would be a spectator sport without the fifth question. Decide is where the course stops being about AI and starts being about us. Every week ends there: given what we now see and what the questions revealed, what's our move? Three answers are always available. Keep it: use the tool, and use it well. Fix it: yes, but only with changes we can name, and a price we can say. And sometimes, and this book means it, drop it. Declining a tool, a job, a default is a legitimate decision, not a failure to keep up. The person who can say exactly why they're dropping something understands it better than the person who kept it without asking. What makes any of the three a decision rather than an opinion is the last clause: what does it cost, and who pays.

Two exits are permanently closed, though. "AI is all hype, ignore it" — the cases in this book will bury that one by Week 3. And "AI decides everything now, why bother" — that one hands our agency to whoever built the system, which is precisely the outcome this course exists to prevent. The hybrid society is being designed right now, mostly by people who did not ask us. Seeing it, questioning it, and deciding is how we get a say anyway.

6

Key terms

Artificial intelligence

Software that learns patterns from a huge amount of data, then uses them to make decisions or to make things, rather than following hand-written rules.

Predictive AI

Systems that output judgments — risk scores, rankings, flags. The kind most likely to be judging us.

Generative AI

Systems that produce new content by predicting what plausibly comes next. Fluent by design — in both directions.

Embodied / agentic AI

Systems that act — in the world or across digital steps — where failure has momentum.

The five parts

Data, model, output, feedback loop, humans. The anatomy of every system in this book.

See it · Question it · Decide

The course's method: run the five questions on the system, then keep it, fix it, or drop it, with the cost named.

7

Discussion questions

For Monday's discussion
  1. Reconstruct your own morning: name three AI systems you used before 10 a.m. today, and label each one predictive, generative, or embodied. If you can't find three, look harder — that's the point.
  2. Pick one of your three and run the five parts on it: data, model, output, feedback loop, humans. Which part was hardest to name? That's usually where the interesting problems hide.
  3. In your own words, no book vocabulary: this course starts by rethinking the question "is AI good or bad?" Explain what it asks instead, and what kind of decision the new question lets you make that the old one didn't.
  4. Give one example from your own life of a "refusal" that you'd defend: a tool, app, or default you deliberately don't use, and the reason. (It doesn't have to be AI. The skill transfers.)
  5. The course question is "can we build a good, beneficial hybrid society?" Before any of the evidence: what's your honest opening answer, in one sentence? Keep it somewhere. Week 10 asks again.
