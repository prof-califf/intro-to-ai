1

At home: Read what AI costs

Before you build your calculator, read a bit about how technology, and AI in particular, affects the environment.

AI data centers are in the news as the environment's newest villain. Asking how a new technology affects the environment is exactly the right instinct, and AI deserves that scrutiny like any other. The harder question is how much concern the evidence supports, so before you decide, read what has actually been measured. Below are four articles: one from a company about its own product, one from journalists who did the math themselves, one that went viral, and one that zooms out to every data center in the country. About 20 minutes.

Skim each one. You don't need every word. Under each article is a list of the details you'll hand to Claude in section 3, so there's nothing to copy down. Just get a feel for who's saying what. (If an article hits a paywall, the details on the card are enough to keep going.)

New to watt-hours? A quick guide

A watt-hour (Wh) is a small amount of electricity, about what a microwave uses in a few seconds. 1,000 Wh is a kilowatt-hour (kWh), the unit on your electric bill. One Wh is 3,600 joules, which matters because Article B uses joules.

Article A · The company's own number

Google, August 2025: "Measuring the environmental impact of AI inference", by Amin Vahdat and Jeff Dean.

  • A median text prompt to the Gemini app uses about 0.24 Wh of electricity, emits about 0.03 grams of CO2e, and uses about 0.26 mL of water, about five drops.
  • Google compares the energy to watching TV for less than nine seconds.
  • Google says the energy of its median prompt fell about 33 times in one year.
  • The catch: the water counts what cools Google's own chips, not the water used to make the electricity. Critics pointed that out the same week.
  • "Median" means the middle prompt. Half used more, half used less.

Who's saying it: the company selling the product.

Article B · Journalists who did the math

MIT Technology Review, May 2025: "We did the math on AI's energy footprint", by James O'Donnell and Casey Crownhart.

  • They measured open models, because the big companies wouldn't share numbers.
  • A small text model used about 114 joules per reply. A large one used about 6,706 joules (about 1.9 Wh).
  • One standard AI image: about 2,282 joules (about 0.6 Wh).
  • One five-second AI video: about 3.4 million joules (about 944 Wh), more than 700 times an image.
  • Epoch AI's estimate for a typical ChatGPT message: about 0.3 Wh.
  • Their sample day (15 questions, 10 images, 3 short videos) used about 2.9 kWh.
  • Reasoning models were found to use 43 times more energy on simple problems.
  • Electricity used by data centers was 48 percent more carbon-intensive than the US average.

Who's saying it: independent journalists, with researchers at Michigan and Hugging Face doing the measuring.

Article C · The one that went viral

The Washington Post, September 2024: "A bottle of water per email: the hidden environmental costs of using AI chatbots", with researchers at UC Riverside.

  • Writing one 100-word email with GPT-4 used about 519 mL of water, a little more than a standard bottle.
  • The same email used about 0.14 kWh of electricity.
  • The water number counts cooling water at the data center and water used at power plants to make the electricity. That's why it's so much bigger than Google's five drops.
  • An email was modeled as several model calls, not one prompt.
  • The researcher behind it, Shaolei Ren, has since said the figure shouldn't be read as an accurate measure of today's AI.

Who's saying it: a newspaper and university researchers, estimating from outside, about a 2024 model.

Article D · The neighbors' view

NPR, October 2025: "Data centers are booming. But there are big energy and environmental risks", by Michael Copley.

  • A typical AI data center uses as much electricity as 100,000 households, and the largest ones being built will use 20 times more (International Energy Agency).
  • In Georgia, some residents reported problems getting drinking water from their wells after a data center was built nearby. In Arizona, some cities have restricted water deliveries to big water users, including data centers.
  • Cooling is a trade-off: evaporative systems lose water as steam, closed-loop systems use less water but more electricity.
  • Companies worry that sharing their water and energy numbers could help their rivals, so the public often can't see them.

Who's saying it: public radio, quoting residents, researchers, and the industry. NPR notes that Google is one of its financial supporters.

One more · The comparison everyone repeats

Luccioni, Gamazaychikov, Alves da Costa, and Strubell, June 2025: "Misinformation by Omission: The Need for More Environmental Transparency in AI", researchers at Hugging Face, Salesforce, Ekimetrics, and Carnegie Mellon.

  • You'll see this claim everywhere: one chatbot question uses about 3 Wh, "ten times a Google search." The researchers checked 100 news articles about ChatGPT's energy use and found about half repeated it.
  • The "ten times" started as one remark in 2023 by Alphabet's chairman, John Hennessy, about what a chatbot exchange likely cost. Alphabet doesn't run ChatGPT, so he was talking about someone else's system.
  • The Google search number, about 0.3 Wh, comes from a Google blog post from 2009, years before search ran on today's AI.
  • As of October 2026, Google has not published an energy number for a search that shows an AI Overview. The 0.24 Wh in Article A is for a prompt in the Gemini app, a different product.
  • AI Overviews are now most of what Americans see. In a 2026 MIT study, not yet peer reviewed, 67 percent of US searches tested in 2025 got an AI answer, up from 42 percent in 2024.

Who's saying it: researchers who measure AI's energy use, tracing a famous number back to where it started. They also argue companies should publish more, so they have a point to make too.

The takeaway

All four articles are about the same machine, and their numbers don't line up. Nobody here is lying. They drew the line in different places: one prompt or one email, the chips' water or the power plant's water too, a 2024 model or a 2025 one, measured by the company or estimated from outside. That's the disclosure gap from Chapter 5, section 2.4, and now you've seen it for yourself. The only company number is from the company, and it's the smallest. And the boom behind the headlines is real: US data center electricity is projected to more than double by 2030, and AI is the main reason. Now hold onto a second question: is AI the only thing in that building?

2

At home: Read what our existing technology habits cost

You streamed something this week. I did too. We all did. That's how it works now. You probably scrolled TikTok or watched YouTube, and maybe you gamed. All of it runs in the same kind of building as a chatbot. And remember Chapter 3: the feeds that pick your next video are recommendation systems, which are predictive AI. If you scroll, you're already using AI. Here are four articles about those everyday habits, starting with the newest one. About 20 minutes, same routine: skim, and notice who's saying what.

Article E · The whole building, June 2026

Associated Press via PBS News, June 2026: "Energy, water use and pollution of AI and data centers rival most countries", by Seth Borenstein, on a United Nations University report.

  • Data centers worldwide used about 448 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025, more than all but 10 countries.
  • That produced about 189 million tonnes of CO2, about the same as Argentina, and making that electricity used about 1.2 trillion gallons of water.
  • Only about 20 percent of data centers' energy is due to AI today. The report expects about 40 percent by 2030. The rest is everything else: streaming, cloud storage, social media, websites.
  • A typical chatbot query uses about 200 times the energy of the simple AI that sorts your spam folder.
  • Cutting the words in your prompts by 30 percent can cut the energy by about 25 percent.

Who's saying it: a UN research institute, reported by the AP, with an industry group and an environmental group both quoted.

Article F · Netflix and streaming video

International Energy Agency, 2020: "The carbon footprint of streaming video: fact-checking the headlines", by George Kamiya.

  • One hour of streaming video used about 0.077 kWh (77 Wh) and emitted about 36 grams of CO2 in 2019.
  • That counts the data center, the network, and your screen. Most of the energy goes to your TV or phone screen and the internet network that carries the video, not to the data center.
  • The article checks a viral claim that 30 minutes of Netflix emits 1.6 kg of CO2. It finds that number was far too high. The French think tank behind it made a math error and later cut its estimate about eight times.
  • The real number depends heavily on your device, your connection, and the video quality.

Who's saying it: an international energy agency, correcting a viral claim.

Article G · TikTok and social media

Global Citizen, March 2022: "How Does Your Social Media Use Impact the Planet? Use This Calculator to Find Out", by Joe McCarthy, reporting a Compare the Market analysis of measurements by Greenspector.

  • Scrolling TikTok: about 2.63 grams of CO2 per minute, the highest of ten apps.
  • Reddit about 2.45 g per minute, Pinterest about 1.3, YouTube about 0.46 g per minute.
  • 145 minutes of TikTok a day for a year comes to nearly 140 kg of CO2.
  • The catch: the per-minute numbers were measured by a French consulting firm in 2021 on one phone, a Samsung Galaxy S7, then repackaged by a price-comparison website.

Who's saying it: an advocacy site, citing a marketing analysis, citing a consultant's lab test. Trace it back before you trust it.

One more · TikTok, disputed

Fortune, December 2024: "Doomscrolling has reportedly made TikTok's annual carbon footprint almost the same as the entire country of Greece", by Sydney Lake, on a study by the carbon-accounting company Greenly.

  • Greenly estimates one minute of TikTok at about 2.9 grams of CO2e, close to Greenspector's 2.63. Only YouTube was higher per minute.
  • At about 46 minutes a day, that's about 49 kg a year per user, like driving an extra 122 miles in a gas car.
  • Scaled up to TikTok's roughly 1 billion users, Greenly puts TikTok at about 50 million tonnes of CO2e a year, close to all of Greece.
  • TikTok disputes it. It doesn't publish its user numbers or its carbon footprint, and a spokesperson said parent company ByteDance's total 2023 emissions were less than 20 percent of Greenly's estimate.

Who's saying it: a company that sells carbon accounting, against a company that won't publish its own numbers. Neither side shows you the full math, which is the disclosure gap again.

Article H · Gaming

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: "The Hidden Cost of Computer Gaming", plus the study's summary (Mills and others, 2019).

  • Gaming in the US uses about 34 terawatt-hours a year, about 2.4 percent of all home electricity, with about 24 million tonnes of CO2.
  • That's about $5 billion a year in electricity, and the same emissions as over 5 million cars.
  • One gaming system can use anywhere from 5 to 1,200 kWh a year, depending on the hardware and the game.
  • Cloud gaming, where the graphics run in a data center and stream to you, uses markedly more energy than playing on your own console or PC. The researchers called it one of their biggest surprises.
  • A 2026 study, not yet peer reviewed, estimates cloud gaming (Xbox Cloud Gaming, PlayStation Plus Premium, GeForce Now) at about 75 grams of CO2 an hour, not counting your own screen or console. The companies don't publish their own numbers (Hazas and others, 2026).

Who's saying it: a federal research lab, with the study funded by the California Energy Commission. Note: Berkeley Lab gives no per-hour number for cloud gaming; the 2026 estimate is the only one.

Putting it all together

You've now read what AI costs and what your everyday tech costs. Here's how they stack up, using only numbers from the articles in sections 1 and 2. One warning before you read it: these numbers don't happen in the same place. Google's AI number counts only its data center. The Netflix number counts the data center, the network, and your screen, and the data center is the smallest part: about 5 percent, against 23 percent for the network and 72 percent for the screen you watch on (IEA). The TikTok and YouTube numbers count your phone and the network. So streaming's electricity is spread across millions of homes and the wires between them, while AI's is concentrated in a few large buildings, on the grids of the towns that host them. (Your phone and the network do a little work for an AI prompt too; the AI numbers here don't count it.) Different boundaries, so treat these as rough. Your calculator in section 3 lets you plug in your own day.

The comparison isn't quite even, because the two happen in different places. The AI energy is all in the data center. Most of the streaming energy is in your own home: about 72 percent is your screen, 23 percent the network, and only 5 percent the data center (Article F). Training isn't counted either.

So it cuts both ways. A day of short text questions to a chatbot is small next to an evening of TikTok or Netflix. A handful of AI videos is not: one five-second clip uses about as much as half a day of Netflix. One more difference: a text prompt is a quick burst, while streaming is a steady draw for as long as the show runs. That's why these tables count AI per request and streaming per hour. Two things even it out, though. Your screen stays on the whole time you're chatting with AI, and none of that is counted here. And at the data center, billions of quick prompts from millions of people add up to a load that runs around the clock, and that steady load is what the town next door feels.

And these tables leave two big things out. First, training: every AI number here counts only answering a request, not the one-time cost of training the model in the first place, which for a large model runs to thousands of megawatt-hours (Chapter 5, section 2.3). TikTok's and YouTube's recommendation systems get trained too, so the same gap applies to them. Second, the buildings: none of this counts the data centers, chips, and power lines that get built because billions of people do all of the above.

The takeaway

None of these habits made headlines like AI did, and all of them run in data centers. Streaming, social media, and gaming each have a real footprint, sometimes a big one, and they had it before chatbots arrived. Notice what's different, though: these numbers are older, they mostly count your screen and the network, and some are yearly totals for everyone rather than a cost per hour. And notice where things are heading: AI is about 20 percent of data center energy today and expected to be about 40 percent by 2030 (Article E). The old habits set the floor; AI is what's raising it fast. And training counts too: the recommenders that pick your next TikTok or YouTube video were trained, and keep being updated, on billions of swipes, the same way a chatbot is trained on text (Chapter 5, section 2.3). Turning all of this into something you can compare takes assumptions. In the next section, watch who makes them.

At home: Build a technology impact calculator with Claude

Don't want to use AI? Do this instead

Same 40 minutes, no chatbot. Instead of building a calculator, take stock of your own technology use.

  1. List your digital day. Open your phone's screen-time report, or estimate. Look at your top five apps and roughly how long you spent on each yesterday.
  2. Find the data center work in it, AI and not. Every app on your list uses a data center, and not all of that work is AI. Playing a video, backing up your photos, storing your email, and loading a web page aren't AI, but they still use energy: the building stores the files and sends them to you. Other work is AI, and AI isn't only chatbots. TikTok's For You feed, YouTube's recommendations, Netflix's rows, Spotify's playlists, your spam filter, and your maps app are all predictive AI running in data centers (Chapter 1's three kinds of AI; Chapter 3's recommendation systems). For each app, mark two things: what it does in a data center that isn't AI, like streaming or storage, and what it does that is AI, and which kind.
  3. Put rough numbers on it. For streaming, YouTube, and TikTok, multiply your time by the numbers in section 2's articles. Then compare your day to the round-trip flight from round 3 below.
  4. Think it through. Data centers had a footprint long before the AI boom: US data centers already used about 58 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2014, running cloud storage, streaming, and social feeds (Chapter 5, section 2.1), and the UN report in Article E says only about 20 percent of data center energy is AI today. AI has sped the growth up: US data center electricity more than doubled from 2017 to 2023, largely because of servers built for AI (section 2.2). Which of your habits were already in a data center before chatbots, and what has AI changed?

Now you turn those article details into a tool. You'll ask Claude to build an interactive calculator, an artifact, one piece at a time: AI first, then your everyday habits, then a comparison with ordinary life, then the sources. After each step you check its work. About 40 minutes.

At home: Under the hood, how your calculator works, Claude included

You've used eight articles, a United Nations flight calculator, and Claude. Here's what was inside each piece of your calculator. About 5 minutes.

1 The numbers

Every number in your calculator is somebody's choice about where to draw the line. Google counted the water that cools its own chips; the Post also counted the water at the power plant. The IEA's streaming hour includes your screen and your Wi-Fi. The TikTok minute was measured in 2021 on one phone, a Samsung Galaxy S7 from 2016. The flight estimate comes from Google Flights, and flight numbers rarely include the extra warming from contrails. Same word, "footprint," different boundaries. That's section 2.4's disclosure gap, and now you've seen that it isn't only an AI problem. The only difference is that the AI numbers are newer and fewer.

2 The arithmetic

The calculator does one thing over and over: cost per use, times how many, times how often. That's section 2.3's point: small, times billions. A per-use number is easy to multiply. A yearly total for everyone, like gaming's 34 terawatt-hours, has to be divided by a guess about how much everyone used it before it turns into a per-hour number. Every one of those guesses is an assumption, and the more assumptions a bar rests on, the less its tidy height means.

3 Claude

Claude is a large language model, a full-size version of the small one you built in Lab 2 & 3. It wrote your calculator's code the same way it writes a sentence, by predicting what comes next. That's why it can build a working page in a minute. It's also why it can drop in a number nobody gave it, the way a sentence can drift. Three instructions in your prompts did the guarding: use only my numbers, mark every assumption, and list anything not from my sources. That's the same move as Lab 4's "based only on this data." The sources section is what makes a confident-looking chart checkable.

4 The building

Every row in your calculator touches the same kind of place: a building full of servers, drawing power from a local grid, cooled with air or water (section 2.1). How much of each row happens there differs. For an AI prompt, nearly all the counted energy is in the building. For an hour of streaming, the building is about 5 percent, and most of the energy is your own screen. Either way, the building's electric meter doesn't know whether a watt went to TikTok, a Netflix show, a cloud game, or a chatbot. Hold onto that. It's the whole reason for the next section.

The search comparison (section 1)
Luccioni, Gamazaychikov, Alves da Costa and Strubell, "Misinformation by Omission," arXiv 2506.15572, June 2025: the 3 Wh and "ten times a Google search" claim traced to John Hennessy's 2023 Reuters remark, de Vries (Joule, October 2023), and Google's 2009 blog post (0.0003 kWh per search); 53 percent of 100 news articles checked in April 2025 repeated it. Aral, Li and Zuo, "The Rise of AI Search," MIT, arXiv 2602.13415, February 2026, preprint: 67 percent of US test queries returned an AI Overview in 2025, 42 percent in 2024. No Google per-search figure for AI Overviews was found in a search on 15 October 2026; Google's 0.24 Wh covers Gemini Apps text prompts only. Re-check each term.
TikTok, disputed (section 2, added 15 October 2026)
Fortune, Sydney Lake, December 13, 2024 (linked), on Greenly's study: 2.921 gCO2e per minute of TikTok, 45.8 minutes a day (Statista), about 48,830 g a year (about 122 miles at the EPA's 400 g per mile), and about 50 Mt CO2e a year globally versus Greece's estimated 51 Mt (IEA). US Energy Information Administration FAQ, "How much carbon dioxide is produced per kilowatthour of U.S. electricity generation?" E-waste comparisons (section 6, added 15 October 2026)

Everyday-tech articles (section 2)
The comparison tables in section 2 are arithmetic on the article figures, rounded: TikTok 2.63 g and YouTube 0.46 g per minute (Greenspector via Global Citizen), streaming 36 g and 77 Wh per hour (IEA, 2019), Gemini 0.03 g and 0.24 Wh per median prompt (Google), an AI image about 2,282 J and a five-second AI video about 3.4 MJ (MIT Technology Review); 1 Wh is 3,600 J. The boundaries differ, which the section says. Whole-building figures: Seth Borenstein, Associated Press, via PBS News, 4 June 2026, on a United Nations University report (448 TWh in 2025; about 189 million metric tons of CO2, comparable to Argentina; about 1.2 trillion gallons of water to produce that electricity; about 20 percent of data center energy due to AI, about 40 percent expected by 2030; a typical chatbot query about 200 times a spam-filter classification; 30 percent fewer words for about 25 percent less energy). Streaming: George Kamiya, IEA commentary (first published by Carbon Brief, February 2020, revised December 2020): about 0.077 kWh and 36 gCO2 per hour in 2019, revised down from 82 g; the Shift Project's 1.6 kg claim and its roughly eight-fold correction. TikTok: Global Citizen, Joe McCarthy, 7 March 2022, reporting Compare the Market's calculator built on Greenspector's 2021 measurements on a Galaxy S7 (TikTok 2.63, Reddit 2.45, Pinterest 1.3, YouTube 0.46 gCO2e per minute; 145 minutes a day for a year at nearly 140 kg). Gaming: Berkeley Lab news release and Mills, Bourassa, Rainer, Mai, Shehabi, and Mills, "Toward Greener Gaming" (2019): 34 TWh a year, 2.4 percent of residential electricity, 24 Mt CO2, $5 billion; 5 to 1,200 kWh a year by system; cloud gaming markedly higher; funded by the California Energy Commission.
Comparisons (section 3)
ICAO Carbon Emissions Calculator, the UN's tool for flights; it counts engine CO2 only, with no contrail or radiative-forcing multiplier (ICAO FAQ). Students look up their own figure. EPA, "Greenhouse Gas Emissions from a Typical Passenger Vehicle" (about 400 g CO2 per mile). Our World in Data, from Poore and Nemecek, Science, 2018 (beef from beef herds 99.48 kg CO2e per kg, a global mean; a quarter pound is about 113 g). FAO, "Pathways towards lower emissions" (2023): livestock agrifood systems 6.2 Gt CO2e, about 12 percent of anthropogenic emissions, reference year 2015; other estimates run higher, roughly 12 to 17 percent. IEA, Energy and AI (April 2025): data centers about 180 Mt CO2 today, about 0.5 percent of combustion emissions. Carbon Brief, "Q&A: Why cement emissions matter for climate change" (2018), citing Chatham House: cement about 2.8 Gt CO2 in 2015, about 8 percent of the global total, more than any country but China and the US. GCSAA Golf Course Environmental Profile, 2022 report: about 1.68 million acre-feet applied in 2020, down 29 percent since 2005, 21 percent recycled water; one acre-foot is 325,851 gallons. Golf's figure is water applied; Berkeley Lab's data center figure is water consumed. The two are not the same measure, which the lab asks students to notice.
