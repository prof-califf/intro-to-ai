The Footprint Machine

Chapter 5 said the cost of AI lives in the multiplication, the defaults, and the numbers nobody publishes. Today you work all three. First you add up your own digital day. Then you set the defaults for a product with a hundred million users. Then you read a company's sustainability report the way an analyst would, and ask a chatbot what it just cost.

Your day · the default · the report · the machine

Take-home this week

Wednesday is the midterm, so this lab is on your own time, alone or with a partner. Skip the in-class check-in. Where a step says "the room," jot your own answer instead; those answers can go in your writeup. The writeup is due Friday, October 23, at 11:59 p.m.

From Chapter 5 · what this lab runs on

The sliders are section 2.3's two costs, training and inference, at four scales. The uncertainty band is section 2.5's disclosure gap made visible: the honest answer is a range because nobody who knows the number publishes it. Right-sizing (2.6) is the lever you'll find you actually control. When you interrogate a live AI about its own footprint, you're running Question 4 on a system talking about itself.

6

At home: Inside the machine, where your watt-hours went

Every number your sliders produced came from Chapter 5's two costs (2.3). Training is a one-time bill paid before you ever typed a prompt; inference is the per-query bill you paid today, and your slider multiplied a per-prompt estimate by how many prompts you sent. That estimate is a range because of the disclosure gap (2.5): the companies know the number and don't publish it, so the lab uses published outside estimates and shows you the band instead of a false point. The water term comes from the cooling panel in the chapter's data-center diagram: chips turn electricity into heat, and evaporative towers turn heat into water vapor.

The scale slider is the real lesson. Your own day rounds to nothing; the campus is small; the country is a power plant; the world is a buildout. Right-sizing (2.6), the default-model choice in Part 2, is the one lever that moves the country-sized number, and it's a product decision, not a personal one. When you asked the machine about its own footprint in Part 4 and it answered with a number, that number came from the same outside estimates, or from nowhere. Ask it where.

7

At home: Who should pay for the power?

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · sides assigned, speak for one, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

The neighbor's bill is the chapter's externality. Argue how to allocate it.

  • Side A: the data center pays its own way. Large new loads should fund the grid capacity they require and be barred from rate structures that spread the cost, as some states are now writing into law. Cost: the projects go to the state next door, with the jobs and the tax base.
  • Side B: it's infrastructure; share the cost and demand disclosure. Treat AI compute like roads or ports, socialize the buildout, and in exchange require the operators to publish energy, water, and emissions per query. Cost: the households paying now get no say, and "in exchange" has a poor record.

Then the smaller, closer question: should your university, which buys AI tools for you, publish the footprint of what it bought? Who at the school would have to ask the vendor, and would the vendor answer?

8

At home: The room · 25 minutes

Discussion questions
  1. Fair scrutiny? For many of us, an evening of streaming outweighs a month of chatbot questions. So why is AI the one getting the scrutiny? Is that fair, or is something about AI different enough to justify it?
  2. Who sets the default? The company, the user, or a regulator? What does each choice cost, and who pays it?
  3. The green toggle. Would you actually flip it? Would most people? What would it take?
  4. The county. A data center is proposed for Whatcom County. Yes, no, or yes with conditions? Who should pay for the grid upgrades?
  5. One number. If you could force every AI company to publish one number, what would it be, and why that one?

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

Sources & what's simulated

Streaming: 77 Wh per hour including devices, networks, and data centers, with data centers about 5 percent (about 3.9 Wh) and 36 gCO2 per hour, from George Kamiya, "The carbon footprint of streaming video: fact-checking the headlines," IEA, December 2020 (2019 estimates); short-video scrolling uses the same commentary's phone-on-WiFi scenario, 37 Wh per hour, and ignores recommendation computing. Grams use the 0.47 gCO2 per Wh implied by the IEA's 2019 figures. AI text prompts: 0.05, 0.3, and 1.2 Wh for small, medium, and large models, the same constants as Lab 1 and consistent with Chapter 5 (Google's 0.24 Wh median, Epoch AI's 0.3 Wh estimate). Reasoning: a 30x multiplier, the AI Energy Score average reported by Ebert, Gamazaychikov, Hacker, and Luccioni (2026). Images (2,282 J, about 0.63 Wh) and five-second videos (3.4 MJ, about 944 Wh) are from MIT Technology Review, May 2025. The uncertainty bands (one-third to three times for AI figures, half to double for streaming) are illustrative, not measured. Parla, Helio AI, their user counts, request mixes, automatic-summary counts, electricity price ($0.08 per kWh), quality scores, and emissions are fictional; Helio's scope pattern is modeled on Microsoft's 2024 report as described in Chapter 5. The household comparison uses about 10,800 kWh per US home per year, as in Chapter 5.
