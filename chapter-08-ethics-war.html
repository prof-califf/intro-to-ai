Ethics & War

We hold this week's discussion on the Monday of Veterans Day week, and we hold two things at once: respect for the people who serve, and hard questions about the tools they're being handed. This chapter is about ethics, so it starts with the old rules of war and asks what AI does to each one, using true stories: a Soviet officer who didn't trust his computer, an air-defense system that trusted its own too much, and two tech companies that drew lines and paid for them. Then the case: Ukraine, where jamming pushed drones toward finishing their own strikes, and where "a human in the loop" quietly changed what it means.

1

Holding two things

This is the chapter where our five questions carry the most weight they'll ever carry, because "what happens when it fails?" stops being a metaphor. It's also a chapter where easy positions are cheap on every side. "Tech companies should never touch defense" is easy until you ask who defends a country that has been invaded, and with what. "Whatever keeps us safe" is easy until you ask who defines us, and safe, and who checks the answer.

So we'll do the harder thing. War already has ethics, worked out over centuries by soldiers, lawyers, and philosophers: fight only combatants, keep a person responsible for every use of force, don't let a tool decide what its user should. Each section below takes one of those old ideas, tells a true story about what happened when a machine got involved, and asks what AI changes. One rule for the week, stated once: every sentence in this chapter should survive being read aloud to a veteran in the room.

2

The concepts: the old rules of war, and what AI does to each

2.1 The chain, and where AI already sits in it

"Military AI" is a phrase that makes people picture a robot with a rifle. Almost none of it looks like that. Militaries describe a strike as a chain of steps, usually six: find a target, fix its location, track it, target it (match it to a weapon and a plan), engage, and assess whether it worked. The chain is decades older than AI, and it's useful here because it shows where AI actually went first.

It went in from the left, into the unglamorous steps. Sorting satellite images to find things worth looking at is a perception task, Chapter 7's step two on a bigger map. Matching radio signals, tracking a vehicle across hours of drone footage, flagging a pattern in shipping manifests: predictive AI, in uniform. Then generative AI arrived in the middle: language models that summarize a thousand intelligence reports into a page, translate intercepted messages, and draft the recommendation a person signs. The engage step, the weapon itself, is where embodied AI enters. It's the step everyone argues about, and until recently it was the step with the least AI in it. Section 3 is about what happens when that changes.

One note on the term. The military calls this sequence the kill chain. That is their word, not mine, and it is public doctrine rather than anything dramatic. I use it because it is the real name for the thing, and because a chapter about whether a machine should be allowed to finish that sequence is the wrong place to reach for a softer one.

That's the kill chain: find, fix, track, target, engage, assess. The common mistake is to think military AI means the last step. Most of it lives in the first four, and it's the same predictive AI as the rest of this book. The real argument is about how far right it moves, and how fast.

2.2 Distinction: telling who is who, the oldest rule

The oldest rule in the ethics of war is distinction: fight combatants, and protect everyone else. It's written into the laws of armed conflict, and its partners are proportionality (expected harm to civilians can't be excessive compared with the military gain) and precaution (do what's feasible to check before you act). Every soldier is trained on them. Distinction sounds like a moral rule, and it is. Look closer and it's also a classification problem: is that a military truck or a farmer's truck?

That's where AI comes in, because classification is what neural networks do. In Chapter 7 a network learned to label road scenes from examples someone had labeled. A target-recognition model works the same way: shown thousands of labeled images of vehicles, it learns which shapes, sizes, and heat signatures go with "military," and it gives every new vehicle a confidence score. Then someone sets a threshold: above this score, flag it.

Here's the part people miss. Military and civilian vehicles overlap. A pickup with a tarp can look like a pickup with a weapon mount. So no threshold gets both mistakes to zero. Set it high and the model misses real threats, which can cost soldiers' lives. Set it low and it flags civilians, which can cost civilians' lives. Chapter 6 had the same trade with loans and jobs. The difference here is what's on each side of the line, and the fact that the people who pay for a wrong flag were never asked where to put it.

So distinction is a moral rule and an engineering setting at the same time. It's easy to treat it as a matter of good intentions. Once a model helps decide who is who, it becomes a threshold someone sets in advance, with a known rate of wrong answers on each side.

2.3 Meaningful human control, and the officer who didn't trust his screen

Just after midnight on September 26, 1983, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov was the duty officer at a Soviet early-warning center outside Moscow. The satellite system sounded an alarm: the United States had launched a missile. Then more, until the screen showed five. His training said to report an attack up the chain, where it could have triggered a counterstrike. Petrov judged it a false alarm instead. A real first strike, he reasoned, wouldn't be five missiles, and the system was new. He was right. The satellites had mistaken sunlight glinting off high clouds for missile launches. He wasn't rewarded; he was reprimanded over his logbook, and the story stayed secret for years (NPR, AP via VOA).

Petrov is why the international debate about AI weapons centers on a phrase everyone endorses: meaningful human control. U.S. policy says autonomous weapons must allow "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force." Nobody has bindingly defined either phrase. So what would "meaningful" have to mean? Petrov's night suggests three things. Understanding: he knew enough about the system to doubt it. Time: he had minutes, not seconds. Authority: his judgment could stop the report from going up. Take away any one and the night ends differently.

Now picture a soldier approving machine-proposed targets every few seconds. Understanding: a label on a screen, not a reason. Time: seconds. Authority: maybe, on paper. Is that person in the loop, or a rubber stamp with a pulse? Chapter 7's handoff problem returns here wearing armor: the middle of the spectrum, a person supervising a fast, mostly-right machine, is hardest exactly when seconds matter.

That's the whole fight over meaningful human control: nearly every government endorses it, and none has bindingly defined it. People tend to treat "a human is in the loop" as the answer. It's only the claim. Understanding, time, and authority are the test.

2.4 Automation bias: when the human trusts the machine

Petrov is the story everyone tells. The 2003 Patriot incidents are the story to tell next to it, because they show the opposite failure. During the invasion of Iraq, U.S. Patriot air-defense batteries were protecting coalition troops from Iraqi missiles, and mostly succeeding. Then, on March 23, a Patriot shot down a British Tornado jet returning from a mission, killing its two crew. On April 2, another shot down a U.S. Navy F/A-18, killing its pilot. In both cases the system classified a friendly aircraft as an incoming missile, and the crews, with seconds to decide, accepted what the system told them (MIT Technology Review).

The Defense Science Board, the Pentagon's own advisory panel, investigated. It found that Patriot ran largely on automatic, and that its operators had been trained to trust the software. An Army expert who led the fixes afterward put a name on it: unwarranted trust in automation (CNAS). The people in the loop weren't careless. They were doing what the system and their training taught them to do. That is automation bias: the tendency to accept a machine's answer because it's usually right, especially when there's no time to check.

Hold the two stories together. Petrov had time and doubt, and the human saved the day. The Patriot crews had seconds and trust, and the human approved the machine's mistake. Same arrangement, a person between a computer and a weapon, with opposite outcomes. The lesson isn't "humans good" or "machines bad." It's that a human in the loop only helps if the loop is built to let them doubt. After 2003, the Army changed how Patriot crews trained and how much they could leave on automatic. Notice that it took deaths to do it.

The lesson people miss about automation bias is that putting a person in the loop doesn't fix a machine's errors on its own. If the person has been trained to trust the machine, they approve the errors too, and now the error has a signature on it.

2.5 Dual use, and who draws the line

Dual use means a technology that serves civilian and military ends at once. GPS was a military system before it found your pizza. Language models are dual use in the most complete way: the same model that drafts a marketing email can draft an intelligence summary, and nothing about the model changes when the customer does. Over time, dual use works like a ratchet inside a company. A logistics contract builds a team, a cleared data pipeline, and a relationship with the customer. When an intelligence contract arrives, all of that is in place; then a targeting aid. Each step is a small move, and a ratchet only turns one way.

So who decides where a company stops? Two true stories, eight years apart, give two answers.

Google, 2018. Google had a contract on Project Maven, a Pentagon program using AI to analyze drone footage. Thousands of employees signed a letter asking the company to get out, and about a dozen resigned. In June, Google said it would let the contract lapse when it ended (Military Times). The line was drawn by employees, and the company paid with a contract and some goodwill in Washington.

Anthropic, 2026. Disclosure first, because it matters here: Anthropic makes Claude, the model that helped draft this textbook, so read this paragraph knowing that. Anthropic had a Pentagon contract worth up to $200 million, and its model ran on classified military networks. Its terms kept two exceptions: no domestic mass surveillance and no fully autonomous weapons. The Pentagon wanted use for "all lawful purposes," arguing that a contractor shouldn't control how the military uses what it buys, the same as a company that sells jets. Anthropic refused. In late February, the President directed federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, and the Defense Department labeled the company a supply-chain risk, a designation usually aimed at firms tied to foreign adversaries. Rival labs signed military deals of their own. In court, the results have been split: a federal judge in California ruled in August that one version of the designation was unlawful, and in September a federal appeals court in Washington upheld the other, 2 to 1 (CNN, Nextgov). As of late October 2026, the fight isn't over.

Both stories ask the same question, and it isn't "is defense work good or bad?" It's who gets to draw the line: the employees, the company, the customer, or the law? The Pentagon's argument is serious: in a democracy, elected officials and the military they command decide how force is used, not a vendor's terms of service. So is the other side's: a company knows what its product can't reliably do, and some uses are beyond what today's technology can do safely. Hold both, because you'll have to pick one in the lab.

The risk in dual use usually isn't a company lying about what it sells. It's a company telling the truth at each step and arriving somewhere it never decided to go, unless someone's job is to stop it.

2.6 The inversion, and the accountability gap

Now run our third question, what happens when it fails, and who finds out?, across two chapters. A robotaxi hits something. A bystander films it, a regulator opens a file, the company issues a recall, and the public learns within days. A wrong strike, shaped by a model, is investigated, if at all, by the side that made it. The report is classified. The people who could tell the public what happened are, by design, the people who can't.

That's the inversion: the systems with the gravest failure modes are the ones with the least visible failures. Every other chapter in this book relied on someone finding out. Here, finding out is the hard part. And when someone does, a second problem appears: the accountability gap. When a person makes a wrong call, the military has centuries of rules for responsibility. When a model's output shaped the call, who answers: the operator who approved it, the commander who fielded it, the engineers who trained it, the company that sold it? After the Patriot incidents, one board recommended reprimanding an officer, a finding later criticized as blaming one person for problems in training and design. That's the gap in one sentence: when the system is the problem, the easiest person to hold responsible is the nearest one.

You'd expect the most dangerous systems to get the most scrutiny. With the inversion, classification and secrecy often make the opposite true, which is why "who finds out" is the question to ask before any other.

2.7 Oversight, or the look of it

So who's checking? A lot of AI governance runs on principles, pledges, and summits, and some of it is real work. Some of it is governance theater: the appearance of oversight performed in place of its substance. The test is three questions, and it works on far more than AI. Behind this principle, this pledge, this summit: is there an enforcement mechanism? A budget? A deadline? If every answer is no, we're watching theater. Run it on "meaningful human control" and notice that the phrase, so far, fails all three. The critical skill isn't cynicism. It's checking, every time.

The answer to governance theater isn't to stop trusting anyone. It's to ask the three questions, enforcement, budget, deadline, and believe the answers.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: which concept is doing the work? Decide, then click to check.
1. A company announces an AI ethics board. It has no budget, meets twice a year, and can advise but not block a product.

Governance theater. No enforcement, no budget, no deadline. Advisory boards can be substance, but only if one of the three answers is yes.

2. An analyst gets 200 machine-flagged locations a shift and must clear each in under ten seconds.

Meaningful human control, failing on time and probably understanding. The person is in the loop on paper and a rubber stamp in practice.

3. A hospital's AI flags a scan as normal. The radiologist, who has seen it be right thousands of times, signs off without looking closely.

Automation bias. It's not a war story, which is the point: the Patriot lesson applies anywhere a person approves a usually-right machine under time pressure.

4. A vehicle-recognition model is set to flag anything above 60 percent confidence. Raising it to 90 would miss more real threats; lowering it to 40 would flag more farm trucks.

Distinction as a threshold. No setting removes both errors; the choice is which error you'd rather make, and who pays for it.

5. A translation model sold for customer service is now used to translate intercepted messages.

Dual use. Nothing about the model changed; the customer did. The ratchet question is what the next contract asks for, and whose job it is to say no.

3

The case: Ukraine's drones, and the moving human

Background A war fought with cheap drones

Since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine has fought one of the first wars in which small, cheap drones do much of the work. The most common is the FPV drone, short for first-person view: a racing-style quadcopter, often carrying an explosive, flown by a pilot wearing goggles that show the drone's camera feed. Ukraine planned to buy 4.5 million of them in 2025 alone, and in 2024 it created a separate branch of its armed forces just for unmanned systems (AP).

Then the other side adapted. An FPV drone depends on a radio link: video down to the pilot, commands up to the drone. Jammers flood those frequencies with noise, and the link drops, usually in the last stretch near the target, where defenses are thickest. A drone that loses its pilot a few hundred meters out either falls or drifts. Both sides went looking for a fix, and they found two.

One fix keeps the human: fiber-optic drones that unspool a thin cable behind them, so there's no radio link to jam. The pilot flies all the way to the end. The other fix is AI. A small onboard computer, sometimes a module that costs about $50, watches the camera feed. The pilot flies the drone toward the target and draws a box around it. That's the lock. From then on, if the link drops, the onboard AI keeps tracking whatever is inside the box and steers the drone the rest of the way. Ukrainian makers have announced mass production of FPV drones with this terminal guidance; one module maker says its system takes over in the last 500 meters, and its founder claims it can raise strike success rates as much as four times (Kyiv Post, IEEE Spectrum). The United States noticed: in 2025 the Pentagon funded 33,000 AI guidance kits for Ukraine's drones (Reuters, via Espreso), and by 2026 U.S. officials were saying openly that they were learning drone warfare from Ukraine (Ukrainska Pravda).

This is the chapter's case because it's where section 2's questions stopped being hypothetical. A country defending itself needed its drones to work under jamming, and the fastest fix moved the machine one step further right on the kill chain.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

An embodied system with a small neural network inside. Run Chapter 1's AI systems model on it. Data: video from combat flights, used to train the onboard model to keep tracking a target through smoke, motion, and partial views. Model: a compact vision network, the same family as Chapter 7's perception step, small enough to run on a chip that rides on the drone. Output: steering commands, many times a second, for the last few hundred meters. Feedback loop: every flight's video can become training data for the next version, which is why both sides improve week to week. Humans: the pilot who chooses the target and draws the lock, the commanders who send them, and the engineers who decided what the model will and won't follow. Notice what it's not. It's not a robot choosing its own targets across a battlefield. A person picks the target. The machine finishes the job.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

It exists because jamming made unguided drones fail, and drone pilots kept losing strikes in the last few hundred meters. Who benefits: Ukraine's soldiers, who can hit targets from farther back and stay out of range, and a country outnumbered by its invader, for whom a cheap module that makes a cheap drone work is a big deal. Precision can also be a benefit to civilians: a drone that holds its lock on a vehicle is less likely to wander into something else than one that falls out of the sky. Who doesn't: anyone in the frame after the lock who wasn't there when the pilot chose. And, over time, everyone, if the techniques spread to forces with fewer scruples about who they point them at.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

Here's the human-in-the-loop lesson, and it's subtle. Jamming didn't take the person out of the loop. It moved the person's last decision earlier, to the moment of the lock. Between the lock and impact, the machine is on its own. If a civilian car pulls into the frame, or the vehicle turns out to be the wrong one, or the tracker slides from the target onto something next to it, there may be no one left who can call it off. Run Petrov's three conditions on the pilot. Understanding: good, at the moment of the lock. Authority: real, at the moment of the lock. Time: everything after the lock happens without them. That's still a human decision, but it's a decision about a future the person can't see.

And who finds out? Mostly the people who fly the missions and review the footage, inside a military at war. That's the inversion from section 2.6, in a case where the users have every reason to want the system to be accurate, and no one outside to check that it is.

Business implications What this means for a company selling into a war

The companies in this case are small: Ukrainian startups, Western investors, and a few American defense-tech firms. Three lessons carry to any company whose product could end up in this chain. First, the feature is the ethics. Whether the module keeps following a target after the lock, or aborts when the picture changes too much, is a product decision, made by engineers and managers, with a cost either way: abort too easily and the strike fails, too rarely and the machine finishes what the human might have stopped. Second, battlefield speed changes how you ship. Ukraine's makers update weekly; the faster the cycle, the less time for the testing section 2.4 shows was missing in 2003. Third, the customer decides the use. Once a module is sold, the seller rarely controls where it goes next, which is exactly what Anthropic's fight was about.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

Most of what we know comes from the companies selling the modules, the governments buying them, and reporters with limited access to a war zone. "Up to four times the success rate" is a founder's claim. "More precise means fewer civilian deaths" is a reasonable argument that nobody outside can currently check. Meanwhile the technology is spreading: the U.S. is studying Ukraine's lessons and budgeting for drones at scale, and the same techniques are available to other armies, including ones you'd least want to have them. A treaty on autonomous weapons has been discussed at the United Nations for years without becoming binding, which is section 2.7's theater test, applied.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep terminal guidance where a person chooses the target and the machine only holds the lock, in a war of national defense. The cost: the human's last decision comes before the last thing that happens, and some of what happens after the lock will be wrong.
  • Fix it by building the doubt in: modules that abort when the picture changes too much, a lock that expires after a set time, a record of every strike's final seconds kept for review. The cost: more strikes that fail, in a war where failed strikes cost soldiers.
  • Drop systems that pick their own targets with no person choosing, and push for a binding international rule. The cost: an adversary may build them anyway, which is the argument for the rule, not against the line.
How to make it good

Keep a person choosing every target, and say plainly that the lock is the human's last decision, not their last moment of responsibility. Fix the gap after the lock with designed doubt: aborts, time limits, and a record someone outside the unit can audit. Drop target selection by machine, and back a binding rule so that line holds for everyone. The cost is real: more failed strikes, slower updates, and a limit the other side may not accept. That cost is the reason the line has to be written down, rather than left to each new module.

Thinking critically The five critical AI questions, on this case, by you

Thinking critically · for Monday's discussion · read the IEEE Spectrum piece on Ukraine's drones first (linked above) · one question each, answered by you, not by the chapter
  1. What is it, and what does it do? Draw the timeline of one terminal-guided strike from launch to impact. Mark where the pilot decides, where the machine decides, and where the jamming starts. Which of Petrov's three conditions is the first to go?
  2. Why does it exist, and who benefits? Make the strongest case, in three sentences, that a country defending itself is right to use terminal guidance. Then name the person that case leaves out.
  3. What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Describe one way the tracker could fail after the lock that nobody outside the unit would ever learn about. What would it take for someone outside to find out?
  4. Who's saying that, and how would we know? "Four times the success rate" comes from a company founder. What evidence would you want before believing it, and who could collect it in a war?
  5. Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Should a terminal-guidance module abort if the picture inside the box changes after the lock? Pick a rule, then say who pays for it: the soldier whose strike fails, or the person who walks into the frame. Keep your answer; the lab asks for it again.
The takeaway from the case

A human in the loop is a place, not a guarantee. In Ukraine, jamming moved that place to the moment of the lock, and everything after it belongs to the machine. Petrov and the Patriot crews show the two ways that can go. The question the case leaves you with isn't whether drones should exist. It's where the last human decision should sit, and who answers for what happens after it.

Decide: the desk

This is the one chapter where deciding is a task with a form. This week's lab runs at home, because Veterans Day takes Wednesday. It puts you at a desk at an AI company and hands you real categories of defense work to sort: accept, accept with conditions, or decline, with a price on each line you draw. That's the same choice Google faced in 2018 and Anthropic faced this year, with the same discovery waiting: the hard part isn't having values, it's finding where they actually sit and what it costs to hold them. Refusal is legitimate. So is engagement. Undefended is the only wrong answer. Read your answer aloud with a veteran in the room.

Key terms

The kill chain

Find, fix, track, target, engage, assess: the steps militaries use to describe a strike, and the map of where AI already sits.

Distinction

The rule that force is aimed only at combatants. With a classifier involved, it becomes a threshold that trades missed threats for wrongly flagged civilians.

Proportionality and precaution

Distinction's partners: civilian harm can't be excessive for the military gain, and you must do what's feasible to check first.

Meaningful human control

The universally endorsed, never-defined standard. Three conditions make it real: understanding, time, and authority.

Automation bias

Trusting a usually-right machine without checking, especially under time pressure. The Patriot crews in 2003.

Dual use

Technology serving civilian and military ends at once, and the ratchet by which the first quietly enables the second.

The inversion

The gravest failures are the least visible, because secrecy and stakes rise together.

The accountability gap

When a model shaped a wrong call, responsibility spreads across operator, commander, engineer, and vendor, and tends to land on the nearest person.

Governance theater

Oversight performed instead of practiced. The test: enforcement, budget, deadline.

Terminal guidance and the lock

The pilot boxes a target; if the link drops, onboard AI steers the rest of the way. The lock is the human's last decision.
