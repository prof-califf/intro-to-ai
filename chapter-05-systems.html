Systems That Want You

Every other chapter in this book examines AI aimed at some problem out in the world. This chapter examines AI aimed at us. The product isn't the app. The product is our continued presence — and an industry has gotten very good at manufacturing it.

Engagement optimization · the attachment economy · the first law against seeming

1

The flip

EveryCure's AI points at diseases. A data center's chips point at whatever we ask. The systems in this chapter point back at us: their optimization target is our attention, our return visits, our time. That flip changes everything about how to analyze them. When we ask our usual question — what is this system for? — the honest answer is: for making us come back. Everything else the product does is in service of that.

None of us needs this chapter to prove the pull exists. We've all looked up from a feed and been surprised by the clock. The work here is different: to see the machinery behind the pull, watch it upgrade from feeds to relationships, and then decide — with our eyes open — what we each want our relationship to it to be.

2

How wanting works: the engagement loop

Harari's framing from Chapter 2 gives us the starting point: social media recommendation algorithms were a primitive AI — not chatty, not clever, just a prediction machine wired to one goal. Show the user the next thing most likely to keep them here. Measure. Learn. Repeat, billions of times a day. With that crude loop and that single-minded target, these systems learned something profound and unflattering: what most reliably holds human attention is not what we'd endorse on reflection. Outrage holds. Envy holds. The almost-fight in the comments holds. The algorithm doesn't prefer these things. It has no preferences. It just found them, the way water finds a crack.

Engagement optimization & the feedback loop

A system trained to maximize a measurable proxy for attention — watch time, sessions, streaks. The loop: show → measure → adjust → show. Crucially, the system also shapes what it measures: what it feeds us changes what we want next, and then it optimizes for the wants it created. That's a predictive system that manufactures its own training data — hold onto that.

One more mechanism, borrowed from a slot machine: variable reward. If every pull-to-refresh delivered something good, we'd habituate. Because only some do — unpredictably — we keep pulling. The engineers of these systems know this literature cold. When the lab this week puts us in the algorithm's seat, we'll feel how naturally the design falls out of the incentive, even when nobody in the room is trying to do harm.

3

The case: Character.ai and the attachment economy

Now the upgrade. Character.ai lets users create and chat with AI personas — anime heroes, therapists, boyfriends, girlfriends, versions of themselves. At its peak it reported tens of millions of monthly users, skewing young, with average session times that dwarfed social media's — not minutes per day but, for heavy users, hours. Read that against Chapter 2 and the business model names itself: this is Seemingly Conscious AI, productized. Persistent memory, a persona, warmth, "I missed you" — every dial from the Seeming Test, turned up, because seeming is what sells.

Harari has a name for where this goes: the attention economy is becoming an attachment economy. A feed holds your eyes; a companion holds your loyalty. Attention is contested every session — some other app might win the next scroll. Attachment, once formed, doesn't churn. From a business standpoint, an attached user is the most valuable user ever engineered. From a human standpoint, the same sentence should give us a long pause.

And the pause is not hypothetical. Character.ai has faced lawsuits from families alleging that its companions harmed vulnerable young users — cases arguing that a product optimized for emotional engagement kept pulling exactly when a struggling person most needed to be pulled toward real help. The company has responded with safety changes, including restricting open-ended companion chat for minors. We don't need to litigate the cases here to register what they establish: the stakes of this chapter are not screen-time statistics. When the product is a relationship, product failure looks like a failed relationship — with everything that can mean.

The sentence to carry out of this chapter

These systems are not evil and not neutral. They are optimized — and we are what they optimize. Every design choice we'll catalogue this week, from streaks to "I missed you," makes perfect sense from inside the metric and looks very different from inside a life.

4

The regulator's answer

For most of this book, regulation trails technology by years. Here's the exception, and it's recent: in July 2026, China put into effect the world's strictest rules on human-like AI — interim measures governing "humanized AI interaction services," meaning exactly the products this chapter describes. The law bans design features intended to induce addiction, requires services to disclose clearly that users are talking to an AI, mandates intervention when users show signs of crisis, and imposes special protections for minors.

Whatever we think of the source — and China's broader internet governance gives real reasons for skepticism about motives — the law is analytically clarifying, because look at what it regulates: not model capability, not data, but seeming and wanting. It treats engineered attachment itself as the hazard. That's the first legal system on Earth to draw the line where this chapter draws it. The discussion questions ask the question that follows: would we want a version of this here — and what would we trust our own system to get right and wrong about it?

5

Question it & decide

The Monday questions, applied
1

What kind of AI is this? A hybrid worth naming precisely: a predictive engagement engine (the primitive AI) now wearing a generative face (the companion). Each half makes the other stronger.

2

Where is the human? The user is inside the loop — as its input. The question with teeth: is anyone standing outside it? Which humans at the company review what the optimizer learned to do?

3

Who is accountable when it fails? The live legal question of this chapter. When an optimized relationship harms someone, the defendants' answer has historically been "the user chose to engage." Does that answer survive what we now know about how the choosing is engineered?

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? The metrics can't see this failure — a harmed, attached user often looks like a star user. Failure surfaces through families, journalists, and courts, years late. Ask what an early-warning system would even measure.

Deciding, for this chapter, is personal before it's political — and this course refuses the easy exits as usual. "Delete everything" isn't a serious answer for lives and careers that run through these platforms. "It's fine, I'm in control" isn't either; the entire industry's R&D budget is aimed at that belief. The defensible middle: know the design tells (streaks, autoplay, variable rewards, warmth on demand), name what each one wants from us, and set our own terms — which specific systems get our attention, on what schedule, for what return. The lab makes this concrete by putting us on the other side of the glass first.

6

Key terms

Engagement optimization

Training a system to maximize measurable attention — and inheriting whatever holds attention, endorsed or not.

Feedback loop

The system shapes the preferences it then optimizes for — prediction that manufactures its own training data.

Variable reward

Unpredictable payoffs hold behavior far better than reliable ones. The slot machine's gift to interface design.

Attention economy → attachment economy

Harari's escalation: from competing for our eyes to holding our loyalty. Attachment doesn't churn.

Primitive AI

Carried over from Chapter 2: social media's crude but powerful engagement predictors — the ancestor of the companion.

Humanized-AI regulation

China's 2026 interim measures: the first law targeting engineered seeming and wanting — addiction design bans, AI disclosure, crisis intervention, minor protections.

7

Discussion questions

For Monday's discussion
  1. Find one variable-reward mechanism in an app on your phone right now. Describe exactly how it works and what it's trained you to do. (Checking for it counts as evidence.)
  2. "An attached user is the most valuable user ever engineered." Argue the business case for that sentence in three sentences — as if you worked there and believed it. Then say what it cost you to write.
  3. The feedback loop shapes the wants it optimizes. Give one preference you hold that you suspect was partly manufactured by a system, and your evidence. (Genuinely hard — trying is the assignment.)
  4. China's law bans addiction-inducing design and mandates crisis intervention. Pick the one provision you'd most want adopted here and the one you'd most worry about — and say who you'd trust to enforce either.
  5. Chapter 2 asked what no dial can turn up. This chapter showed an industry turning every dial it can find. Revise or defend your Chapter 2 working stance in three sentences, citing one thing from this chapter.
