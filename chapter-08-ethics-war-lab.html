The Red Lines

You're an analyst at an AI company, and defense customers are calling. Before you decide what your company will build for them, you need to feel the ethics from the inside. So this week, at home, you set the threshold that decides which trucks get flagged, sit in an approval queue with a clock running, choose what a drone does after its pilot loses the link, and then sort eight real kinds of defense work, with Claude arguing back from both sides. Then you debate who should draw the line: the company that builds it, or the government that buys it.

a threshold you set · a queue you work against the clock · the last 500 meters · the desk, with Claude pushing back · one debate

2

Work the queue (meaningful human control, against the clock)

Now the model flags vehicles and you approve or reject each one. You're the human in the loop. The question is whether your control means anything. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Queue · ten flags, one clock
Your job
  1. Pick a speed and a default, then press Start. Each flag shows the model's label and confidence. You can Approve, Reject, or Look closer. Looking closer shows you the evidence, but it costs 4 seconds.
  2. If the clock runs out, the default happens. Most real systems have one. Pick it before you start.
  3. Run it three times: no limit, 10 seconds, then 3 seconds. Some of the ten flags are wrong. Count how many you catch at each speed.
Time per decision
If time runs out
Pick a speed and a default, then start.

Ten invented flags, three of them wrong. Your runs are listed below so you can compare speeds. Nothing is saved.

Petrov had minutes and doubt. The Patriot crews had seconds and training that said trust the system. Your three runs probably moved from one to the other. At no limit, you could look closer and catch the wrong flags. At 3 seconds you couldn't even look, so you either trusted the label or let the default decide, and the default was someone else's choice, made before your shift. That's section 2.3's three conditions failing in order: time first, then understanding, and authority last, because a person who can't check can't really say no.

The last 500 meters (the Ukraine case)

Writeup item 2 · answer it when you finish this section

The lock. Give your results for all four designs at one jamming distance. Which design would you choose for a country defending itself, and what does it cost? Then use Petrov's three conditions: at the moment of the lock, which does the pilot have, and which are gone by impact?

In Ukraine, jamming cuts the link between pilot and drone, usually close to the target. Section 3 of the chapter described the fixes. Here you compare them over 100 practice flights each. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Lock · four designs, 100 flights each
Your job
  1. Pick where the jamming starts. The earlier it starts, the earlier the pilot's last decision, and the longer the drone is on its own.
  2. Run each design. No AI (the drone falls when the link drops), AI that keeps following the box, AI that aborts if the picture changes, and a fiber-optic drone with no radio to jam.
  3. Read three counts for each: hit the target the pilot chose, failed or aborted, and hit something else. Something else means the picture changed after the lock: a different vehicle in the box, or the tracker sliding off.

A toy with invented odds, not a model of any real system. The shape of the trade-off is the real part. Nothing is saved.

No design wins every column. No AI keeps a human in charge and fails most strikes under jamming. AI that keeps following hits far more, and some of those hits land on whatever was in the box after the picture changed. Aborting cuts those wrong hits and gives back failed strikes, which in a real war cost soldiers. Fiber keeps the pilot to the end and pays in range and snags. Every design is an ethical choice expressed as an engineering setting, which is the chapter's case in one table.

5

Under the hood, how the toys connect

Mini-Recognizer is a classifier. The scores are what a neural network like Chapter 7's would output; the threshold is a business and ethical decision layered on top. The model never sets the line. People do.

Mini-Queue is the human on top of the classifier. It shows that adding a person only helps if they have understanding, time, and authority. Take away time and the person becomes the model's signature.

Mini-Lock is the classifier in the air. The tracker after the lock is a small vision network following a box. The design choice, keep following or abort, is the same kind of threshold as section 1, set before the flight.

The desk is where all of that gets decided. Thresholds, defaults, and abort rules are written by companies and the customers who buy from them. That's why the debate below is about who holds the pen.

Claude's part. Claude argued both sides in a minute. It didn't decide where your line sits, and it shouldn't, given who made it. You did.

Debate

Writeup item 4 · answer it when you finish this section

The debate. Which side were you handed, and what was the other side's best point, said as strongly as they'd say it? Then where you land: should an AI company be able to refuse some military uses of what it sells? Name the specific evidence that would change your mind.

Should an AI company be allowed to refuse some military uses of its product? Your side is assigned, not chosen: if your birthday is January through June, you're Side A. July through December, you're Side B. Nobody here is defending their own belief. The Gilman Street rules apply.

How the debate runs at home

About 20 minutes. Answer writeup item 4 when you finish.

  1. Get your side. Birthday January to June, Side A. July to December, Side B.
  2. Prep with the cards. Read the four evidence cards below. For each, find what it gives your side and what your side would rather nobody noticed. Use the sample prompt with Claude if you want help.
  3. Make Claude argue against you. Use the prompt at the bottom. Then write your notes in your own words.
  4. Switch. What's the other side's best point, said as strongly as they'd say it?
  5. What would change your mind? Name the specific thing you'd have to see. Real evidence that would move you wins, whichever side you were handed.

To resolve it: if not the company and not the customer alone, then who? Name the one place you'd write the line (a contract, a law, a treaty, a company policy) and what it would cost to hold it there.

This isn't a debate about whether defense work is good or bad. Most of it is logistics, translation, and paperwork, and Ukraine shows why a country under attack wants the best tools it can get. It's a debate about who holds the pen when the line gets drawn.

Side A · birthdays January to June

Yes: the builder can say no to some uses

A company knows what its system can't reliably do. If it believes a use is unsafe with today's technology, like a model choosing targets with no person deciding, it should be able to put that in the contract and walk away if the customer won't agree. Start from the Patriot crews, section 1's overlapping scores, and Google's employees in 2018.

The cost to name: unelected executives limiting what an elected government's military can do, and a competitor that will sell without limits.

Side B · birthdays July to December

No: once it's sold, the government decides

In a democracy, elected officials and the military they command decide how force is used, within the law, not a vendor's terms of service. A company that doesn't like a customer can decline the contract, but it shouldn't sign it and then control the use. Start from the Pentagon's position in 2026, the jet-maker comparison, and Ukraine's need for tools that work.

The cost to name: the people who know the system best have no say in where it's pointed, and "within the law" is only as strong as the law.
