Meaningful human control · condition 1 of 3 from section 2.3

Understanding

You saw a score for every vehicle, and never a reason. A truck sits at 62 because of patterns the model picked up from thousands of labeled pictures, and nobody can read those patterns out (Chapter 7, section 2.2). The person downstream sees even less than you did: a flag.

Remember this: What would a person approving these flags need to see, beyond the score, to understand why the model flagged a vehicle?

For the desk

Two of your eight requests run on a model like this one: satellite image analysis and target identification support. Once you sell it, the customer sets the threshold, not you. You could make a condition of it: the customer writes down where the line sits and how often it was wrong in testing. That costs you a customer who'd rather not put it in writing. Not asking means the model's mistakes carry the customer's line and your company's name.

At home: Work the queue (meaningful human control, against the clock)

Now the model flags vehicles and you approve or reject each one. You're the human in the loop. The question is whether your control means anything. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Queue · ten flags, one clock
Your job
  1. Pick a speed and a default, then press Start. Each flag shows the model's label and confidence. You can Approve, Reject, or Look closer. Looking closer shows you the evidence, but it costs 4 seconds.
  2. If the clock runs out, the default happens. Most real systems have one. Pick it before you start.
  3. Run it three times: no limit, 10 seconds, then 3 seconds. Some of the ten flags are wrong. Count how many you catch at each speed.
Time per decision
If time runs out
Pick a speed and a default, then start.

Ten invented flags, three of them wrong. Your runs are listed below so you can compare speeds. Nothing is saved.

Petrov had minutes and doubt. The Patriot crews had seconds and training that said trust the system. Your three runs probably moved from one to the other. At no limit, you could look closer and catch the wrong flags. At 3 seconds you couldn't even look, so you either trusted the label or let the default decide, and the default was someone else's choice, made before your shift. That's section 2.3's three conditions failing in order: time first, then understanding, and authority last, because a person who can't check can't really say no.

Is this real?

The flags are invented. The pattern isn't. In 2003, U.S. Patriot crews with seconds to decide accepted the system's wrong labels twice, and three allied crew members died. The Pentagon's own advisory board found the operators had been trained to trust the software (Chapter 8, section 2.4).

Meaningful human control · condition 2 of 3 from section 2.3

Time

At no limit you could look closer, and looking closer is where understanding comes from. Every second the clock took away was a second you couldn't spend checking. Below some speed, the look-closer button might as well not exist, and the default decides instead. Someone chose that default before your shift began.

Remember this: At what speed did looking closer stop being possible for you? That's the point where your control stopped meaning anything.

For the desk

Target identification support says the human approves each strike "at a tempo of seconds per decision." You just worked that tempo. A condition you could ask for: a minimum review time, or a default that holds a flag instead of approving it. It costs the customer speed, and the customer may go to a competitor that doesn't ask.

No design wins every column. No AI keeps a human in charge and fails most strikes under jamming. Meaningful human control · condition 3 of 3 from section 2.3

Authority

Up to the lock, the pilot can call it off. After the lock, under jamming, nobody can, because nobody can reach the drone. Authority you can't use isn't authority. What's left is the abort rule, and that was written before takeoff by whoever designed the module.

Remember this: Who should have the authority to set the abort rule: the engineer, the company, the commander, or a law? Pick one and say why.

For the desk

Autonomous engagement is on your desk too: defensive systems where no per-engagement human decision is physically possible. Mini-Lock showed you what happens after the last human decision. Before you sort that card, decide whether a design rule, like aborting when the picture changes or keeping a record of the final seconds, makes it acceptable with conditions, or whether the line is somewhere you won't sell at all. Either answer costs something, and section 4 asks you to name it.

At home: Under the hood, how the toys connect

Mini-Recognizer is a classifier. The scores are what a neural network like Chapter 7's would output; the threshold is a business and ethical decision layered on top. The model never sets the line. People do.

Mini-Queue is the human on top of the classifier. It shows that adding a person only helps if they have understanding, time, and authority. Take away time and the person becomes the model's signature.

Mini-Lock is the classifier in the air. The tracker after the lock is a small vision network following a box. The design choice, keep following or abort, is the same kind of threshold as section 1, set before the flight.

The desk is where all of that gets decided. Thresholds, defaults, and abort rules are written by companies and the customers who buy from them. And the abort rule is where the debate below lives: what the drone does after the lock, when no person can call it off.

This week’s case: terminal guidance in Ukraine

Jammers cut the radio link between a drone and its pilot, usually in the last stretch near the target. Ukraine’s fix: the pilot flies toward the target and draws a box around it, the lock, and a small onboard AI holds that lock for the last few hundred meters if the link drops. One module maker says its system takes over in the last 500 meters (Kyiv Post), and in 2025 the Pentagon funded 33,000 AI guidance kits for Ukraine’s drones (Reuters, via Espreso).

Both sides can use it. Side A: a person still chooses every target, and the machine only finishes what the person started. Side B: jamming moved the human’s last decision earlier, and anything that changes after the lock, the machine handles alone (Chapter 8, the case).

Before drones: the human in the loop, two nights

Before you argue about the drone, look at what happened the last times a person stood between a computer and a weapon. These two stories pull in opposite directions, and both sides will use them.

  1. WhenWhat happenedSource
  2. 1983

    The officer who doubted his screen

    Just after midnight on September 26, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov’s early-warning system showed five U.S. missiles launched at the Soviet Union. His training said to report an attack. He judged it a false alarm, and he was right: the satellites had mistaken sunlight on high clouds for launches. He had understanding, minutes to think, and the authority to stop the report.

    The catch: he was reprimanded, not rewarded, and the story stayed secret for years. The system worked because one person broke from what it told him.

    NPR (WBEZ). (2017, September 18). Stanislav Petrov, the man who saved the world, dies at 77. Open source AP, via VOA. Open source

  3. 2003

    The crews who trusted theirs

    During the invasion of Iraq, U.S. Patriot batteries shot down a British Tornado, killing its two crew, and a U.S. Navy F/A-18, killing its pilot. Both times the system labeled a friendly aircraft an incoming missile, and crews with seconds to decide accepted it.

    The catch: a person was in the loop both times. The humans approved the machine’s mistake.

    MIT Technology Review. (2005, June 1). Preventing fratricide. Open source

  4. 2005 and after

    The fix

    The Pentagon’s Defense Science Board found that Patriot ran largely on automatic and that operators had been trained to trust the software. An Army expert who led the fixes called it unwarranted trust in automation. The Army changed how crews trained and how much they could leave on automatic.

    The catch: it took deaths to make the change.

    Hawley, J. K. (2017). Patriot wars. Center for a New American Security. Open source

Notice the pattern. Petrov had time and doubt, and the human saved the day. The Patriot crews had seconds and trust, and the human signed off on the error. A person in the loop only helps if the loop lets them doubt. Side B leans on Petrov. Side A points at Patriot.

The case for letting it finish: a country that needs it to work

Why terminal guidance exists, in the words of the people building and buying it.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. 2022 to 2025

    A war of cheap drones

    Since Russia’s full-scale invasion, small FPV drones flown by pilots in goggles do much of Ukraine’s fighting. Ukraine planned to buy 4.5 million of them in 2025 alone and created a separate branch of its armed forces for unmanned systems.

    AP, via WPXI. (2026). Open source

  3. The problem

    Jamming breaks the last stretch

    Jammers flood the radio link, usually near the target where defenses are thickest. A drone that loses its pilot a few hundred meters out either falls or drifts, and the strike fails.

    The catch for Side B: a drone that falls doesn’t land nowhere. One that holds its lock may be less likely to wander into something else.

    Chapter 8, the case

  4. 2025

    The fix goes to scale

    Ukrainian makers announced mass production of drones with terminal guidance. A module, sometimes costing about $50, holds the lock for the last stretch. One founder says it can raise strike success as much as four times. The Pentagon funded 33,000 AI guidance kits for Ukraine.

    The catch: “up to four times” is the founder’s claim, and nobody outside the war can check it.

    Kyiv Post. Open source IEEE Spectrum. Autonomous drone warfare. Open source Reuters, via Espreso. (2025, July). Open source

  5. May 2026

    The U.S. takes notes

    U.S. officials said openly that they were learning drone warfare from Ukraine.

    The catch: what one army learns, others learn too, including armies with fewer scruples about where they point it.

    Ukrainska Pravda. (2026, May 30). Open source

Notice the pattern. Every part of this record is about a strike that fails without AI, and nearly every success number comes from someone selling or buying the module. Side A leans on the need. Side B asks who checked the numbers.

The case for keeping a person to the end: the line nobody has drawn

The alternatives, and the rules people are trying to write.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. 2023

    The U.S. rule

    U.S. policy says autonomous weapons must allow “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force.”

    The catch: it doesn’t define “appropriate,” so both sides can claim it.

    U.S. Department of Defense. (2023). Directive 3000.09, Autonomy in weapon systems. Quoted in Chapter 8, section 2.3

  3. 2023

    The world asks for a treaty

    The UN Secretary-General and the president of the International Committee of the Red Cross called on countries to negotiate binding prohibitions and restrictions on autonomous weapons, and to finish by 2026.

    The catch: as of this lab, no binding treaty exists.

    ICRC. (2023, October 5). Joint call by the UN Secretary-General and the ICRC President. Open source

  4. Ongoing

    The other fix: a cable

    Fiber-optic drones unspool a thin cable behind them, so there’s no radio link to jam. The pilot flies all the way to impact.

    The catch: they’re slower, shorter-ranged, and the cable can snag. Keeping the human costs soldiers something.

    Chapter 8, the case

  5. August 2026

    “A moral red line”

    The two leaders renewed their call, warning that the world is close to crossing a moral red line: machines targeting humans on their own. Countries meet in Geneva in November 2026 to decide whether to start formal treaty negotiations.

    The catch: their red line is machines choosing targets. In terminal guidance, a person still chooses. Side A will say the line is already being held.

    UN News. (2026, August). UN chief, Red Cross renew call for rules on lethal autonomous weapons. Open source American Society of International Law. (2026). Open source

Notice the pattern. Everyone endorses human judgment, and nobody has bindingly defined it. The alternatives that keep a person in all cost something on the battlefield. Side B leans on the line. Side A asks who pays for holding it.

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your runs, your sort, and your choices, because a model that wasn't there can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
