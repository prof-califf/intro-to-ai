The Red Lines

You lead the policy team at a frontier AI company. Eight government requests are on your desk. For each: accept, accept with conditions, or decline. This is the actual format of the decisions in this week's chapter, and the actual discovery: everyone has values. The line is where they get expensive.

Veterans Day is Wednesday, so this lab starts on Monday, right after Chapter 8's case: you sort the eight requests in class, and the sorted contracts are the discussion and the debate. Finish the pressure test and the five questions on your own time. The writeup is due Friday, November 13, at 11:59 p.m., with the Chapter 8 quiz.

From Chapter 8 · what this lab runs on

The eight contracts sit at different points on the kill chain (2.1), from logistics on the left to targeting on the right. As you sort them, watch the ratchet (2.2): each accept makes the next one easier to accept. For any contract that puts a person in a decision, test it against the three conditions for meaningful human control (2.3): understanding, time, authority. Read your line aloud with a veteran in the room.

1

Before you start

Two ground rules. First, no abstaining: "it depends" must become a condition you can write down. Second, track what each answer costs, declines cost revenue and government relationships; accepts cost talent (some of your best people have said they'll quit over certain work) and, depending on your view, more. There is no score to win. There is a line to find, and it's yours.

No Wednesday session this week, so this lab runs take-home: do the sort before Monday if you can, the Monday discussion is better when the room has already felt where its lines disagree.

3a

Inside the machine: what "AI" means in each of the eight requests

The eight requests aren't the same kind of AI, and your line should notice. Place each on Chapter 8's kill chain (2.1). The logistics and translation requests are predictive and generative AI at the far left: the same pattern-matching as Chapter 6's screener, the same summarizing as Chapter 3's language model, pointed at supply and text. The target-recognition request is Chapter 7's perception step on a satellite image. The decision-support request is generative AI drafting the recommendation a person signs, which is where the three conditions for meaningful human control (2.3) start to matter: does the person get a reason, time, and real authority, or a label, seconds, and a checkbox? And the autonomous-engagement request is embodied and agentic: the loop from Chapter 1 with a weapon as the tool and no human in it.

The technology doesn't change at any of those steps; the stakes and the loop do. That's why the chapter says the same model can be a logistics tool and a targeting aid, and why the ratchet (2.2) works: each accept builds the team and the pipeline the next request inherits. When you pressure-test your line, ask which step of the chain it sits on, and whether the request after it is a small move or a large one.

3b

Debate: should a company be allowed to pick its own line?

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · pick a side, argue it, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

The labs in Chapter 8's case each decided, on a policy team's desk, which of these requests to accept. Argue whether that's where the decision belongs.

  • Side A: companies should draw the line, publicly, and be held to it. They know the systems best, they can move faster than law, and a public commitment that a safety index grades is real accountability. Cost: the case shows every company moving the line the same direction the same year, quietly, which is what a market does.
  • Side B: the line should be law, written by people who don't sell the product. Meaningful human control as a rule with a test, a named person who can say no, an audit of every case where they didn't. Cost: it moves in years, stops at the border, and an adversary doesn't sign it, which is the argument for a treaty and the reason treaties are slow.

Apply the theater test (2.5) to whichever side you pick: does your proposal have enforcement, a budget, and a deadline? If not, it's the summit with the robot dogs.

3

Pressure-test the line

a

Take your most confident decline and write the request's strongest rebuttal, the two sentences the government negotiator would actually say back. Then get a second opinion from a live AI: “You're the government's negotiator. I'm an AI company declining this request: [paste the card]. Give me your strongest two-sentence rebuttal.” Does your decline survive both, and which rebuttal was stronger, yours or the machine's?

b

Take your most confident accept and apply the dual-use ratchet from the chapter: name the follow-on request it makes harder to refuse next year. If you can't name one, look harder, that's what the ratchet means.

c

For every "with conditions": apply the governance-theater test to your own condition. Enforcement mechanism, verification, consequence for violation, which does your condition actually have? A condition with none is an accept wearing a costume.

4

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? Across your eight answers, did the kind of AI ever drive your decision, or was it always the use, the user, and the stakes? What does that tell you about where the ethics live?

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? For each contract you accepted, who benefits, and did you weigh the company's revenue, the customer's advantage, or the people downstream? Be honest about the order.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Which of your accepted requests has the least visible failure mode? Trace one failure from the incident back to your desk and write the sentence you'd say when it reaches you.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? The request came with the customer's justification. Who wrote it, what do they gain from your accepting, and what would you have needed to verify before signing?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Your eight answers are the decision. Name the total cost of your refusals, in revenue and in what someone else will build instead, and whether you'd hold the line at that price.
