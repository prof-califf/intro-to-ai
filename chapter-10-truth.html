Truth: The Plausibility Machines

The AI systems in this book share one design fact: none of them was built to tell the truth. They were built to be plausible, and plausible is cheaper than true, faster than true, and harder and harder to tell apart from true. This chapter is about what happens when the difference stops being visible, and who pays to check.

1

Built for plausible, not true

If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it this one: a language model is never lying, and it is never telling the truth. It is doing something else, and checking is our job.

That sounds like a riddle, but it describes a mechanism. A language model writes the most likely next words. Most of the time the likely words are also the true ones, which is why these tools are useful. Sometimes they aren't, and the model has no way to tell the two cases apart. Once you see that, most of what is confusing about AI and truth gets simpler.

So this chapter asks a plain question. If the tools that now write so much of what we read were built to sound right rather than to be right, who does the checking, and who pays for it?

The point of this chapter, in one sentence: AI made believable content almost free to produce, checking it still costs time and money, and most of what we can do about truth comes down to making checking cheaper.

What this chapter teaches. Five ideas, then a case to use them on.

  • First, what a language model optimizes for: why confident fabrication is the machine working as designed.
  • Second, the verification asymmetry: a fake takes seconds to make and hours to check.
  • Third, deepfakes and the liar's dividend: fake media of real people, and what the possibility of fakes does to real evidence.
  • Fourth, grounding and provenance: the two main ways to make checking cheap again.
  • Fifth, the other edge: language models get bad press for hallucinations, and fairly, but a chatbot is graded on being right in a way a feed never was.

Then the case: in 2023, a lawyer asked ChatGPT for court cases, filed what it gave him, and found out in front of a federal judge that the cases did not exist. We'll run the five critical AI questions on it, and ask how to make it good.

2

The concepts: the plausibility machine, and the economics it creates

2.1 What a language model optimizes for

A large language model is trained to do one thing: produce the most plausible continuation of a text, the next piece that best fits the pattern of everything it has read. Chapter 2 showed you this with a matchbox, and Chapter 3 showed you where it sits inside a product. Here is what that means for truth.

Truth and plausibility usually line up. If the training text said something thousands of times, the likely answer and the true answer are the same, which is why these systems are so often right. But the model has no internal flag that marks the difference. When it states a real fact, and when it invents one with full confidence, it is doing the same thing.

Watch it happen on a real example. A lawyer asks for court cases that support his argument. The model writes a case name, because case names follow requests like that. It puts an airline in the name, because the request was about an airline. It adds numbers shaped like a legal citation, because that is what follows a case name, and then a court and a year that fit. Each piece is the most plausible next piece. The finished line looks exactly like a real citation. At no step did the model check a database, because it has no step for that.

That's a hallucination: confident fabrication by a generative model, such as an invented fact, quote, or source, delivered in the same fluent voice as everything accurate. People picture it as a malfunction that will be patched out. It's the model working as designed, in the cases where plausible and true come apart. The systems have gotten meaningfully better at staying close to the truth, and the grounding tools in section 2.4 help a lot. They have not changed what the model is built to do.

2.2 The verification asymmetry

Now the economics. A phantom citation takes seconds to generate and hours of a professional's time to expose. The same pattern shows up wherever making content gets cheap, and elections are where the stakes make it visible first.

Here's what happened in New Hampshire. Two days before the 2024 presidential primary, thousands of voters got a robocall in Joe Biden's voice telling them not to vote. It was an AI clone. The political consultant who commissioned it testified that he paid a magician $150 to make the recording. The fallout ran for more than a year: a state investigation, a $6 million federal fine finalized in September 2024, and a criminal trial on 22 counts. In June 2025, a New Hampshire jury acquitted him of all of them. He argued the primary was an unsanctioned straw poll and that the call never used Biden's name.

Add it up. A hundred and fifty dollars of generation tied up investigators, regulators, and courts, and a criminal law written before voice clones existed didn't reach him. For a century, audio proved itself: if you heard someone say it, they said it. That default is gone.

That's the verification asymmetry: making content costs seconds and cents, while checking it costs minutes, expertise, and institutional time. Anyone who benefits from confusion can now produce it faster than anyone else can clean it up. It's tempting to blame gullible people. The real problem is cost, so the real fixes are the ones that make checking cheaper. Any system where attacking costs pennies and defending costs hours has a stability problem, and the information we all depend on is now that kind of system.

2.3 Deepfakes, and the liar's dividend

Start with the fakes themselves. A deepfake is audio, an image, or video of a real person, made by AI, showing them saying or doing something they never said or did. The New Hampshire robocall in 2.2 was one. The name comes from deep learning, the kind of AI that makes them.

Making one works like everything else in this book: data in, pattern out. A voice-cloning tool takes a sample of someone speaking, learns the patterns of that voice, and then says whatever new text you type, in that voice. Many services need only a few seconds of audio, such as a clip from TikTok or YouTube (NBC News). In 2025, Consumer Reports tested six leading voice-cloning products. For four of them, its researchers could easily clone a voice from public audio, with nothing to check that the speaker had agreed (Consumer Reports). Fake video works the same way, with photos and footage in place of a voice sample.

Businesses are already paying for it. In early 2024, a finance worker in the Hong Kong office of Arup, the British engineering firm behind the Sydney Opera House, joined a video call with what looked like the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. He had doubts, but the people on the call looked and sounded like colleagues he knew. He made 15 transfers worth about $25 million. Every person on the call was a deepfake (Fortune, reporting CNN and Hong Kong police).

That's a deepfake: AI-made audio, images, or video of a real person doing something they never did. People assume they could spot one. The Arup worker had doubts and still couldn't, and detection software often struggles to tell a real voice from a cloned one (NBC News). The defense that works is a check outside the fake: a call back to a number you already know, a family password, a second person who has to sign off.

Now the deeper damage. It doesn't come from the fakes themselves. It comes from what the possibility of fakes does to real evidence. Legal scholars call this the liar's dividend. Once everyone knows audio can be faked, anyone caught on real audio can say "that's a deepfake." Some people will believe them, and everyone else now has to do work to prove the recording is real. The dishonest person collects the benefit without making anything at all.

Follow that to the end and you reach the real stakes of this chapter. The danger isn't only a world where people believe false things. It's a world where people can't be made to believe true ones, because every inconvenient fact has an easy way out. A society can survive some lies. It cannot govern itself without a shared way to settle what happened.

That's the liar's dividend: the bonus fakery pays the dishonest, because real evidence becomes deniable. We tend to count the harm from fakes by the fakes people believe. Count it by the true things people dismiss, and the number is larger.

2.4 Making verification cheap again

If the problem is a cost gap, the fixes have to close it from the checking side. Two matter most, and you'll use both.

Grounding is the fix for text. Connect the language model to a real database, and require every claim it makes to point to a document that exists. The model still predicts plausible text, but now that text has to link to something, and a phantom has nothing to link to. This is how legal research tools now work. It's why the Avianca mistake is harder to make today than it was in 2023, though not impossible: the model can still misread a real document, and the reader still has to click.

Provenance is the fix for photos, audio, and video. A camera signs what it captures with a cryptographic credential. Every edit adds to the record, and a viewer can check the chain from start to finish. The leading standard is called C2PA. Camera makers, software companies, and newsrooms back it, and adoption is still partial.

Notice what provenance does and doesn't do. It doesn't detect fakes. A photo with no credential isn't proven fake; it's just unproven. What content credentials do is make real things provable, which is the side of the asymmetry worth fixing. Hearing used to be believing. Now the credential is.

2.5 The other edge: AI built for truth, and the feed it's competing with

So far this chapter has treated generative AI as a truth problem. Now compare it with the technology it is replacing for many people: the feed from Chapter 3. The picture changes.

A feed is paid in attention. Without anyone intending it, the feed learned that outrage holds people, and nobody at the company is judged on whether a post was true. A chatbot is judged on whether its answer was right. Users notice when it's wrong, competitors advertise on it, and the labs say they train their models for honesty, which is a claim to hold them to rather than take on faith. Neither system is built for truth by nature. But one of them has truth in its objective, imperfectly, and the other doesn't have it at all.

Here's the comparison people usually skip. Language models get a lot of bad press for hallucinations, and that's fair; this chapter's case is one reason why. But hold them next to the feed. A feed's ranking system was never asked whether a post was true. It was built to keep you engaged, and false news is engaging. An MIT study of about 126,000 news stories on Twitter from 2006 to 2017 found that false news spread farther and faster than the truth in every category, and that falsehoods were 70 percent more likely to be retweeted (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral, Science, 2018). That study measured people sharing, not one company's algorithm. But Facebook's own researchers found its algorithm adding to the problem. After a 2018 change that rewarded comments and reshares, they warned that "misinformation, toxicity, and violent content are inordinately prevalent among reshares" (Wall Street Journal, the Facebook Files, 2021). Nobody designed the feed to spread false things. It spread them because they kept people scrolling, and nothing in its goal pushed back.

Chatbots get measured against the truth, and on most measures they've improved. OpenAI reports that with web search turned on, its GPT-5 thinking model gave an answer with at least one major factual error 4.8 percent of the time, against 22 percent for its earlier o3 model (GPT-5 system card, as summarized by Implicator). That's the company grading itself, and the progress isn't steady. OpenAI's own tests found that its 2025 o3 and o4-mini models made things up more often than the model before them: 33 and 48 percent of the time on questions about people, against 16 percent for o1 (TechCrunch). Later in 2025, OpenAI's researchers argued that the industry's own tests had been rewarding models for guessing instead of saying "I don't know," and called for scoring that rewards admitting uncertainty (OpenAI). Notice what that means: a lab publicly treating confident wrong answers as a failure to fix. No feed ever ran that test on itself.

Users grade chatbots too, and they grade harshly. Researchers at Wharton found that people lose confidence in an algorithm faster than in a person after seeing both make the same mistake (Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey, 2015). That's hard on the companies, and it's good for truth. A feed that spreads a false post loses nothing. A chatbot caught inventing something loses trust, and Air Canada in Chapter 2 and the lawyers in this chapter both paid for theirs.

So the fair version is this. A language model is not a truth machine; underneath, it still predicts plausible text, and it still hallucinates. But it's the first mass information technology that gets graded on truth, by its makers, by researchers, and by angry users, and on most of those grades it has been getting better. The feed never took the test.

The strongest evidence that the objective matters comes from research on conspiracy theories. In 2024, three researchers, Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook, and David Rand, put 2,190 conspiracy believers into three rounds of conversation with OpenAI's GPT-4. The model was asked to answer each person's own reasons with evidence. Belief in the person's chosen conspiracy fell by about 20 percent, the drop held two months later, and it worked even on people with deeply entrenched views. Decades of human fact-checking had rarely moved believers at all. A model that could answer their specific argument did.

A 2025 follow-up with 1,224 people used a different lab's model on antisemitic conspiracies and found the same thing. The mechanism is the one from section 2.1, pointed the other way: the model can write the specific, tailored counterargument a believer has never actually heard.

Two caveats, and both are course material. First, in June 2026, Science posted an editorial expression of concern on the 2024 paper. That means the journal is looking into something. It's not a retraction and it's not an all-clear, and the replication stands on its own. Check where it lands, and run Question 4 on whatever you find. Second, a 2026 study found the same models persuade people into conspiracies about as well as out of them. The ability runs both ways. What the system is built to optimize for decides the direction, and someone makes that choice.

Nobody has tested a feed against a chatbot head to head for truth, and the evidence that does exist points both ways. When journalists from 22 public broadcasters checked more than 3,000 answers to news questions from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and Perplexity in 2025, 45 percent had at least one significant problem (EBU and BBC). And the feed isn't simply blind to truth. When researchers switched Facebook and Instagram users from the ranking algorithm to a plain newest-first feed during the 2020 election, the amount of untrustworthy content they saw went up (Guess and others, Science, 2023). Critics later pointed out that Facebook changed its algorithm partway through the experiment (a 2024 critique). So "the feed lies and the chatbot doesn't" is too simple. The difference is in what each one is for.

And that difference lives in us. People open a feed to be entertained. They open a chatbot to be told what's true. A false video that holds your attention has done its job; a chatbot answer that turns out false has broken the deal. That expectation is real: just over a third of UK adults say they trust AI to summarize the news accurately, and almost half of people under 35 do (BBC). It's the strongest pressure anyone has on AI companies to get things right, because a tool people expect to be right loses them when it isn't. But the same expectation lowers our guard. The lawyers in this chapter's case expected the truth, so they didn't check.

One more thing to watch, because the objective can change. In February 2026, OpenAI began showing ads in ChatGPT to free and budget-plan users. The company says ads don't influence its answers. Axios's read was that advertising splits a company's incentives between serving the user and pleasing advertisers, and that the gap can grow (Axios). A chatbot paid for attention could start to drift the way the feed did. That's a claim to check over the next few years, not a verdict.

So the honest version of this chapter's claim is narrower than "AI is a truth problem." Generation got cheap, and that created the asymmetry and the dividend, which are real and getting worse. At the same time, the most capable tool anyone has had for tailored, evidence-based correction just arrived, and whether it gets used that way depends on who's paying for it and what for.

That's the principle for this section: the objective decides. A model that can write the perfect tailored counterargument can write the perfect tailored lie, and which one you get depends on what it's built to optimize for and who's paying. So when the question is AI and truth, look past the technology to the system in front of you: what is it judged on, and by whom? And keep checking. The expectation that a chatbot will be right is the best pressure we have on the companies, and it only works while people notice when it isn't.

3

The case: the phantom citations

Background A serving cart, a brief, and six cases that didn't exist

In 2022, a passenger sued the airline Avianca in federal court in New York. He said a metal serving cart had struck his knee on a flight. It was a routine case.

In 2023, his lawyers filed a brief citing several court cases that supported their argument. The cases had convincing names, plausible citations, and quotable passages. Avianca's lawyers couldn't find them. Neither could the judge, because they did not exist. One of the lawyers had asked ChatGPT for supporting cases, and it produced them, complete, confident, and fictional. When he asked it directly whether the cases were real, it said they were. When the judge ordered copies, what came back was fabricated too.

On June 22, 2023, the court dismissed the case and fined the lawyers $5,000. Mata v. Avianca became the first famous collision between a tool built to be plausible and an institution built on checking.

Here's what makes it a case for this book rather than a funny story: it keeps happening. Courts and researchers now track AI-fabricated citations in legal filings, and the running counts are in the hundreds, even after every lawyer in America heard about the first one. The lesson isn't that some lawyers are lazy. The problem is built in. The fabrication arrives already formatted to look reliable, so a phantom citation looks exactly like a real one. The fluency that makes the tool useful is the same fluency that hides its failures. The phantom docket in section 2.2 lets you try catching them yourself, with the clock running.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

Generative AI in its plainest form: a general chatbot, OpenAI's ChatGPT, asked to do a lookup job it was never built for. The five parts:

  • Data: the internet's text, including a great deal of legal writing, which is why the phantoms looked so convincing.
  • Model: a language model that predicts plausible text and has no lookup step.
  • Output: a brief's worth of citations, real and fake in the same voice.
  • Feedback loop: none that mattered here, though every fabricated citation now in a court record is a document the next model may read.
  • Humans: a lawyer who treated a writer as a librarian, opposing counsel who was paid to check, and a judge.

Beyond this one case, the truth problem takes three systems working together: the model that fabricates, the platforms that distribute, and the recommendation systems from Chapter 3 that decide what spreads.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

The tool exists to produce plausible text cheaply, at any volume. Who benefits: anyone who needs a lot of words fast, and anyone for whom plausible is enough, such as a lawyer in a hurry, a campaign, or a scammer with a voice sample. Who doesn't: the client whose case was dismissed because of his lawyers' shortcut; the court, which spent its time on a brief that should never have been filed; and, once the counts reached the hundreds, every honest lawyer whose real citations now get a second look. Generation is cheap and checking is expensive. That gap is the business model, and its costs land on whoever does the checking.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

It fails quietly. There's no crash and no recall, just a rising cost of knowing anything. Who finds out is the asymmetry question again: whoever can afford to check. In Avianca, opposing counsel was paid to check, which is why this one was caught and why it became a case at all. Most of life has no such opponent. The fabricated statistic in a term paper, the invented quote in a marketing deck, the phantom source in a news summary: those get found out later, or never.

Business implications What this means for anyone who ships words

Every business now produces text with these tools, and nobody wants to be the next Avianca lawyer. Three lessons.

First, know which task you're asking for. Drafting, summarizing, and rephrasing are what the model does well. Looking up facts is not, unless the tool is grounded. A company that confuses the two has Chapter 3's "narrator, not decider" problem, with legal exposure attached.

Second, the check has to be part of the workflow, not a good intention. "Someone reviews it" fails the same way "on the loop" fails in Chapter 7. "Every citation links to its source before it leaves" holds.

Third, the signature rule: whatever your company publishes with AI, a named person signs it, and that person has checked anything the company could be held to.

The costs are the minutes the lawyer didn't spend, multiplied across a company, plus grounded tools that cost more than a free chatbot. The cost of skipping them was a dismissed case, a sanction, and a name that now appears in every textbook on the subject, including this one.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

"The AI confirmed the cases were real" is what the lawyer said the AI said. A chatbot's answer about its own output is a claim from a source with no way to check itself. What would have settled it: the court docket, which took ten minutes. That's the whole lesson, and it scales up. A society that can't afford the ten minutes, or has been talked out of believing what the ten minutes show, is the liar's dividend at full size.

The structural answers are the ones in section 2.4, plus the institutions that do expensive checking for a living: courts, journalism, and science. All three are underfunded compared with what generation now costs. Deciding to pay for that checking is a political choice, which is Chapter 11's subject.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it for drafts, summaries, and finding the argument you forgot. The cost: the discipline to remember which kind of task you're doing.
  • Fix it: ground it, so every claim links to a source; sign what you produce; check anything you'd be held to. The cost: the ten minutes the lawyer didn't spend, and paid tools instead of free ones.
  • Drop it wherever nobody is there to check. The cost: when checking is rare, knowing what's true becomes something only some people can afford.

How to make it good

The lawyers' mistake wasn't using the tool. It was using it as a source. Keep AI for what it does well in legal work: summarizing a long record, drafting a first pass, finding the argument you forgot. Fix the workflow so every citation it produces is checked against the real database before it leaves the office, which is exactly what grounded legal research tools now do. The cost is the minutes that checking takes, and that's the point. Drop asking a general chatbot to find cases at all. That costs nothing, because it was never able to do it. And keep section 2.5 in view: the same machine that wrote the phantoms is, pointed the other way and paid for being right, the best correction tool anyone has built. The lawyers had the good version available. They asked it the wrong kind of question.

4

Decide: the reflex, and the referees

4.1 Your own check

Deciding works at two levels. The first is personal: adopt a habit of checking and practice it until it's a reflex. Question 4 has been rehearsing it all quarter. Asking "how would we know?" was always the point; this chapter just showed you the stakes.

In practice the habit is short. Check the source, not the model's word about the source (section 2.1). Click the link before you repeat the claim (section 2.4). Read a missing content credential as unproven, not fake. And for a voice you can't be sure of, a family password is a check that costs nothing.

4.2 The checking we pay for together

The second level is structural, because personal care doesn't fix an asymmetry. Making checking cheaper does. That means provenance standards that let cameras sign what they capture, grounded tools that make claims clickable, platform accountability for what gets amplified, and institutions funded to do the expensive checking. It also means deciding, as citizens, that we'll pay for referees: the courts, newsrooms, and scientists who check for a living.

The mature position refuses both easy exits. Not "trust nothing," because that's the liar's dividend winning. Not "it'll sort itself out," because the asymmetry says it won't. See it, question it, decide.

4.3 Three takeaways

The model doesn't check

A language model writes the plausible answer. When plausible and true come apart, it sounds exactly as sure. Its answer about its own output comes from the same process, so asking it is not a check.

Checking is the expensive side

A fake costs seconds; checking costs hours. The fixes that work make checking cheaper: grounding for text, provenance for media, and people paid to look.

The objective decides

A feed is paid for attention; a chatbot is judged on being right. The same model can talk a person out of a conspiracy or into one. Ask what a system is judged on, and by whom, and keep noticing when it's wrong.

5

Key terms

Plausibility optimization

What language models actually do: produce the most likely continuation. Truth often comes along, but it isn't the target.

Hallucination

Confident fabrication: the model working as designed where plausible and true come apart. Not a bug that can be patched out on its own.

Deepfake

AI-made audio, image, or video of a real person doing something they never did. Cheap to make from a few seconds of audio, hard to spot, and the end of media that proves itself.

Verification asymmetry

Generation costs seconds; checking costs hours. The economics underneath every case in this chapter.

The liar's dividend

The bonus fakery pays the dishonest: real evidence becomes deniable. The deepest damage runs through true things, not false ones.

The objective decides

A feed is paid for attention; a chatbot is judged on being right. Same raw material, opposite failures, and the difference is a design choice.

Algorithm aversion

People lose trust in an algorithm faster than in a person after the same mistake. Hard on AI companies, and one reason a chatbot has to care about being right.

Grounding

Connecting a model to a real database so every claim has to point to a document that exists. The fix for phantom citations.

Provenance

Content credentials: cameras and tools signing what they produce so a viewer can check where a file came from. The leading attempt to make real media provable.
