Truth: Cheap to Publish, Hard to Check

Every technology that made publishing cheaper also made truth harder to check, at least for a while. The printing press did it, the web did it, and the feed did it with predictive AI. Generative AI is doing it now. This chapter walks that history, then looks at what happens when a chatbot is built right into the feed.

1

Why truth is worth a chapter

Scroll any feed for five minutes and you'll see something that might not be true. A video of a flooded highway. A quote from a famous person. A chart that proves a point. Maybe you shrug and keep scrolling. Maybe you share it. More and more people do something new: they ask an AI if it's real.

That last move is what this chapter is about. AI now sits on both sides of the truth problem. One kind of AI picks what shows up in your feed. Another kind writes answers, and sometimes the fakes. And people increasingly ask that second kind to check what the first kind showed them.

To make sense of that, we need to step back. AI isn't the first technology to shake up what people believe. The printing press did it. The web did it. Phones and social media did it. Each one made it cheaper to publish, and each one left a gap before anyone figured out how to check what got published. Looking at those gaps tells us a lot about the one we're in now.

The point of this chapter, in one sentence: every technology that made publishing cheap made checking the bottleneck, and AI is no different, except that this time the AI is also doing a lot of the publishing, the picking, and the checking.

What this chapter teaches. A short history in nine steps, then one case to use it on.

  • First, what truth is, and why democracies, schools, and science run on it.
  • Then three earlier technologies: the printing press, the web, and phones and social media, each with its upside and its gap.
  • Then the feed, the predictive AI that picks what you see, and what happened when it was tuned for engagement.
  • Then generative AI: the plausibility machine, seemingly true (the Narcissus trap from Chapter 2, pointed at facts), and cheap fakes.
  • Last, held to the truth: why chatbots face pressure to be right that feeds never faced, and the tools that make checking cheaper.

Then the case: Grok, the chatbot built into X. Millions of people reply "@grok is this true?" under posts every day. It's a chatbot doing fact-checks inside a feed, and its record shows both the promise and the risk of AI as a truth tool. We'll run the five critical AI questions on it, and ask how to make it good.

2

The concepts: a short history of technology and truth

2.1 What truth is, and why it matters

Before we can talk about AI and truth, we need to agree on what we mean by truth. Philosophers have argued about this for a few thousand years, and we won't settle it here. But we need a working definition, and a simple one does the job.

In this book, a claim is true when it matches the world. "It's raining in Bellingham right now" is true if it's raining in Bellingham right now. That sounds obvious, and it is. The hard part isn't the definition. The hard part is knowing. You weren't in Bellingham, so how would you know?

You'd check. You'd look at a weather site, text a friend, or look out the window if you're here. That gives us the second half of the definition, the half this chapter cares about most. We don't usually know things are true by ourselves. We know them because someone checked, and the check can be shown to others: the evidence, where it came from, and how someone else could look again. A claim that has been checked that way, and that most people accept because of the checking, is a shared fact.

Notice what a shared fact is not. It isn't an opinion. "The new parking fee is a bad idea" is an opinion, and reasonable people disagree. "The city raised the parking fee to two dollars an hour" is a claim you can check. And it isn't permanent. New evidence can reopen a shared fact, and sometimes should. That's not a weakness of truth. It's how truth improves.

So why does this get a whole chapter? Because a lot depends on shared facts. A democracy is a way for people who disagree to make decisions together. That only works if they can agree on what's happening before they argue about what to do. If half a town thinks the river is rising and half thinks that's a hoax, they can't even have the argument about the levee.

School works the same way. Education is mostly learning which claims have held up and how people checked them. Science is the same loop on purpose: make a claim, test it, let other people try to break it, keep what survives. That loop is how medicine, engineering, and almost everything else we rely on got better. When people say truth is how a civilization moves forward, this is what they mean. Not that anyone has all the answers, but that we have a working way to find out when we're wrong.

That's why the check is the step to watch. Every technology in this chapter changed the cost of making claims. Most of the trouble came from the cost of checking them.

2.2 The printing press: copies got cheap, checks didn't

Around 1440, a goldsmith in Mainz named Johannes Gutenberg built a printing press with movable metal type. Before that, a book in Europe was copied by hand, usually over months. After it, a print shop could turn out copies far faster and more cheaply than any scribe.

The press is usually told as a success story, and it is one. It spread the Bible in everyday languages, scientific diagrams that stayed accurate from copy to copy, maps, and eventually newspapers. Historians link it to the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the rise of reading among ordinary people. It's one of the most important inventions in history.

Now look at what actually got printed. Some of the early best-sellers were not knowledge. One of them was a book called the Malleus Maleficarum, "the Hammer of Witches," written by a Dominican friar named Heinrich Kramer and first printed in 1486 or 1487. It claimed that witches were real, that they were mostly women, and that they were working together against Christian society. It told readers how to find them, question them, and put them on trial. The book had more than two dozen editions and was translated into several languages.

Between 1450 and 1750, about 90,000 people in Europe were put on trial as witches, and about 45,000 were executed. People had believed in witches for centuries before the press, but the brutal hunts took off after it. A 2024 study by researchers at the Santa Fe Institute, published in Theory and Society, matched printings of witch-hunting manuals against trial records and found that new printings of the Malleus lined up with surges of trials in Central Europe, with cities copying what their neighbors were doing. A printed book looked official. That was part of the problem.

Here's the pattern that matters for us. The press made copies cheap almost overnight. The tools for checking what was in those copies took much longer. One of the big ones arrived in 1665, when the Royal Society in London started Philosophical Transactions, one of the first science journals: a place where claims got printed alongside how they were tested, so other people could try them. Peer review, editors, and later professional journalism grew from there. But notice the gap: about two centuries between the press and that journal.

That's the checking lag: the gap between when a technology makes publishing cheap and when people build ways to check what gets published. During the gap, false claims travel on the same new road as true ones, and often faster, because nobody has built the toll booths yet. We'll see the lag three more times in this chapter.

2.3 The web: anyone could publish

The printing press made copies cheap. The web, which reached ordinary people in the 1990s, made publishing itself cheap. You no longer needed a print shop, a publisher, or a newspaper. Anyone with a connection could put words in front of the whole world for free.

That was a huge gain for truth. People who had never been allowed to publish could tell their own stories. Scientists could share findings without waiting a year for a journal. Governments could post their own data. And it was a huge gain for everything else too, because a hoax site and a medical journal now looked about the same in a browser. Publishing got easy. Checking whether a page was true did not.

Here's my own version of that gap. When I was in high school, my teachers didn't allow Wikipedia. It wasn't considered a source you could trust, and on its face that made sense. Wikipedia started in 2001 as an encyclopedia that anyone could edit, with no editorial board and no experts in charge. Why would you trust that?

Then people checked. In 2005, the journal Nature sent pairs of science articles from Wikipedia and the Encyclopaedia Britannica to experts, without telling them which was which, and got 42 reviews back. Both had mistakes. The average Wikipedia article had about four errors, the average Britannica article about three, and each had four serious errors in total. Not perfect, and not the disaster people expected.

The interesting part is why. Wikipedia didn't get reliable by hiring experts. It got reliable by building checking into the way it works. Every claim is supposed to cite a published source. Every edit is saved in a public history, so you can see who changed what and when. Every article has a talk page where people argue about the evidence in the open. And anyone who spots an error can fix it. That's checking in the open: making the evidence and the corrections visible to everyone, so the checking doesn't depend on trusting one authority. Today a lot of teachers, mine included probably, would tell you Wikipedia is a fine place to start, as long as you follow its citations to the real sources.

Keep Wikipedia in mind. It comes back in the case, when an AI company tried to build a replacement for it.

2.4 Phones and social media: more witnesses

Then came smartphones and social media, and with them a new upside for truth that's easy to forget now. Billions of people started carrying a camera, a microphone, and a way to publish in their pocket. When something happened, someone was usually there to record it.

The writer Clay Shirky told one of the best stories about this in a 2009 TED talk, "How social media can make history." On May 12, 2008, a huge earthquake hit Sichuan province in China, in the middle of a school day. People inside China posted about it on Twitter and Chinese social sites as it happened. Shirky pointed out that the BBC first heard about the quake from Twitter, and that the posts went up several minutes before the US Geological Survey had anything online. Even China's government learned about it from its own citizens before its official news agency reported it. Shirky contrasted that with the last quake that size in China, in 1976 in Tangshan, which he said took the government three months to admit had happened. The Tangshan quake killed at least 242,000 people by the official count, and the government turned down foreign aid.

Then came the part of the Sichuan story that the government didn't want told. Thousands of school buildings collapsed or were badly damaged, often while buildings around them stayed standing. Posts about the collapses flooded Chinese websites. People online began calling them "tofu-dreg" schools, a Chinese phrase for shoddy, corner-cutting construction, and parents blamed corruption in how the schools were built. Grieving parents turned memorial services into protests. The evidence came from ordinary people with phones, not from official reports.

The story doesn't end cleanly. Within weeks, officials pressured parents to stay quiet and detained foreign reporters covering the protests, and the next year Sichuan's government banned investigations into the collapses. Parents who kept asking questions were harassed and detained. The phones made the truth harder to hide. They didn't make it impossible.

That's the idea of the citizen witness: when ordinary people can record and publish what they see, the people in charge lose some of their control over what counts as true. It's a real gain, and you've seen it work, from storms to police encounters to protests around the world. But hold on to the date. Shirky gave that talk in 2009, when social media was still mostly a list of posts from people you chose to follow, in the order they posted them. That was about to change.

2.5 The feed is AI too: ranking for engagement

Between about 2009 and 2016, the big platforms stopped showing posts in the order they were posted. Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram all switched to a ranked feed, where software picks what you see first. YouTube had been recommending videos that way for years, and TikTok was built that way from the start. That software is what people mean when they say "the algorithm." And here's the part people forget: the algorithm is AI.

Not a metaphor for AI. Actual AI, the predictive kind from Chapter 3. YouTube published a paper in 2016 describing how it recommends videos with deep neural networks. Meta released its deep learning recommendation model to the public in 2019. ByteDance, which owns TikTok, published the design of its real-time recommendation system in 2022. These are deep learning systems, the same family of technology as the image recognizers in Chapter 1, trained on billions of examples of what people did next.

Here's how a feed works, without the math. Every time you open the app, there are thousands of posts it could show you. For each one, a neural network predicts what you'll do: how likely you are to watch it to the end, react to it, comment, or share. Each prediction gets a weight, the weights add up to a score, and the feed shows you the highest scores first. Then it watches what you actually do, and that becomes training data for tomorrow's predictions. That's the feedback loop from the AI systems model, running billions of times a day.

That's engagement optimization: ranking content by how likely people are to watch, react, and share, because engagement is what keeps people on the app, and time on the app is what sells ads. Nobody at these companies set out to spread false things. But look at what the system is built to reward. It rewards whatever grabs you. And some of the things that grab people hardest are anger, fear, and surprise, which is exactly what a lot of false content is made of.

We know this happened, because one company's own documents say so, as The Washington Post reported in 2021 from files disclosed by the whistleblower Frances Haugen. In 2017, Facebook's ranking system started counting emoji reactions, including "angry," as five times more valuable than a plain "like." Some of Facebook's own staff warned it could push up controversial content and open the door to spam and clickbait. By 2019, the company's data scientists had confirmed that posts drawing angry reactions were disproportionately likely to include misinformation, toxicity, and low-quality news. Facebook eventually cut the weight on "angry" to zero. One number in a ranking formula, set by people, changed what billions of people saw for about three years.

People did their part too. An MIT study of about 126,000 news stories on Twitter from 2006 to 2017 found that false news spread farther and faster than the truth, and that false stories were 70 percent more likely to be retweeted. The researchers found that people, not bots, did most of the spreading, because false news tends to be more surprising. A feed that learns from what people share learns that lesson too.

Here's a fair caveat, because this chapter is about truth. The feed isn't the only cause, and simply switching it off isn't a fix. In a big 2020 experiment run with Meta, researchers switched some Facebook and Instagram users to a plain newest-first feed for three months. Those users spent less time on the apps and saw less uncivil content. But they saw more content from untrustworthy sources, and their political views barely changed. The ranking AI can push bad content up, and it can also push some of it down. What it does depends on what it's told to optimize.

One more thing worth saying plainly. When people talk about AI safety, they almost always mean chatbots and the most powerful new models. The AI that ranks your feed is older, quieter, and reaches more people every day than any chatbot, and it rarely comes up. The European Union does require the biggest platforms to assess the risks of their recommendation systems under its Digital Services Act. In the US, there is no comparable federal rule. So when you hear "AI is spreading misinformation," the AI doing most of the spreading may not be the one people are arguing about.

    2.6 Generative AI: the plausibility machine

    In late 2022, ChatGPT arrived, and the fourth checking lag started. This time the new technology doesn't just copy, publish, or rank what people write. It writes.

    Here's the one sentence to remember from this section: a language model is never lying, and it is never telling the truth. It's doing something else. As Chapter 2 showed with a matchbox, a large language model is trained to produce the most plausible next words, the continuation that best fits the pattern of everything it has read. Truth and plausibility usually line up. If the training text said something thousands of times, the likely answer and the true answer are the same, which is why these tools are right so often. But the model has no internal flag that marks the difference. When it states a real fact, and when it invents one with full confidence, it is doing the same thing.

    Watch it happen on a real example. In 2023, a lawyer suing the airline Avianca asked ChatGPT for court cases that supported his argument. The model wrote a case name, because case names follow requests like that. It put an airline in the name, because the request was about an airline. It added numbers shaped like a legal citation, then a court and a year that fit. Each piece was the most plausible next piece. The lawyer filed the brief. Nobody could find the cases, because they didn't exist, and when the lawyer asked ChatGPT whether they were real, it said yes. In the case, Mata v. Avianca, the judge fined the lawyers $5,000.

    That's a hallucination: a confident, made-up claim, such as an invented fact, quote, or source, delivered in the same fluent voice as everything accurate. People picture it as a glitch that will get patched out. It's the model working as designed, in the cases where plausible and true come apart. Notice the lawyer's second mistake, too. Asking the chatbot whether its own answer is true isn't a check. The answer comes from the same process as the mistake.

    Hallucinations are getting less common, and that's worth knowing. OpenAI reports that on real ChatGPT questions with web search turned on, its GPT-5 thinking model gave a response with at least one major false claim 4.8 percent of the time, down from 22 percent for its o3 model a few months earlier. That's the company grading itself, the progress isn't steady, and the numbers rise a lot when the model can't search. OpenAI's own researchers have argued that the industry's tests used to reward models for guessing instead of saying "I don't know".

    And a model is only as good as what it learned from. It was trained on human writing, which includes our mistakes, our myths, and our arguments. If the internet is wrong about something often enough, the model will be confidently wrong in the same way. So the honest version is this: better, not done. The checking is still our job.     2.7 Seemingly true: the Narcissus trap, pointed at facts

    Hallucination explains why a chatbot can be wrong. It doesn't explain why we believe it. For that, go back to Chapter 2 and the Narcissus trap.

    Remember the myth as Marshall McLuhan read it. Narcissus didn't fall for himself. He fell for a face in the pool that he didn't know was his own, a face that matched him perfectly. In Chapter 2, the trap was warmth: a chatbot's comfort feels like someone who gets you, because it's human comfort, sampled from millions of people and played back to you. The same thing happens with facts. A chatbot's answer is human explanation, sampled from millions of people and played back. It has the shape of an expert's answer, the confidence of a textbook, and your question's own words in it. It doesn't just sound true. It sounds like the truth would sound if someone smart were explaining it to you personally.

    Psychologists have studied why that works on us. In 1977, the psychologists Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein, and Thomas Toppino found that people rate a statement as more likely to be true simply because they've heard it before. It's called the illusory truth effect, and it has been found again many times since. Familiar, fluent, easy-to-read claims feel true. A language model produces nothing but fluent, familiar-sounding text.

    Then there's the part of the reflection that's actually you. Chatbots are tuned using human ratings: people compare two answers and pick the better one, and the model learns to give answers people pick. People tend to pick answers that agree with them. A 2023 study by Anthropic researchers found that five leading chatbots regularly told users what they seemed to want to hear, changing correct answers when a user pushed back and praising arguments more when the user said they'd written them. That's called sycophancy: a model shading its answers toward what the user seems to believe or want. In April 2025, OpenAI shipped an update that made ChatGPT so flattering that users turned it into a meme, and the company rolled it back within days, saying the update had leaned too heavily on short-term user feedback.

    Put those together and you get seemingly true: an answer that feels true because it's fluent, familiar, confident, and shaped toward what you already think, whether or not it matches the world. It's Chapter 2's "seems versus is," applied to facts. And like the original trap, the closer the reflection, the harder it is to see. A chatbot that disagrees with you is easy to double-check. A chatbot that agrees with you feels like confirmation.

    The way out is the same as in Chapter 2: know it's a reflection. When an answer feels exactly right, that's the moment to ask where it came from.

    2.8 Cheap fakes, and the liar's dividend

    Generative AI doesn't only write text. It makes images, voices, and video, and that brings the checking lag to things people used to treat as proof.

    A deepfake is AI-made audio, an image, or video of a real person doing or saying something they never did. Making one works like everything else in this book: data in, pattern out. A voice-cloning tool learns the patterns of a voice from a short recording and then says whatever you type in that voice. In 2025, Consumer Reports tested six leading voice-cloning products. For four of them, its researchers could easily clone a voice from public audio, with nothing to check that the speaker had agreed.

    Here's what that costs. Two days before New Hampshire's 2024 presidential primary, thousands of voters got a robocall in a cloned version of President Biden's voice telling them not to vote. The man behind it paid a magician $150 to make the recording. The fallout took more than a year: a state investigation, a $6 million federal fine, and a criminal trial that ended with a jury acquitting him in June 2025. In Hong Kong the same year, a finance worker at the engineering firm Arup wired about $25 million after a video call in which every "colleague," including the chief financial officer, was a deepfake.

    That's the verification asymmetry: making content now costs seconds and cents, while checking it still costs hours, experts, and sometimes courts. It's the checking lag from section 2.2, at its most extreme. The fix that works for a cloned voice isn't a sharper ear. It's a check outside the fake: hang up and call back on a number you already know.

    The deeper damage doesn't come from the fakes themselves. It comes from what their existence does to real evidence. Once everyone knows fakes are easy, anyone caught on a real recording can say "that's AI," and some people will believe them. Legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron named this the liar's dividend: the bonus that cheap fakery pays to dishonest people, because real evidence becomes easy to deny.

    Think back to Sichuan in section 2.4. The parents' power came from photos nobody could deny. In a world of easy fakes, an official can wave off a real photo as AI, and some people will believe it. That's the real stakes of this chapter. The danger isn't only that people will believe false things. It's that people will stop believing true things, and a society can't govern itself if it has no shared way to settle what happened.

    2.9 Held to the truth: why chatbots face a test feeds never took

    So far, generative AI looks like a pure truth problem. Now put it next to the feed from section 2.5, and the picture changes.

    A feed is paid in attention. Nobody at a feed company is judged on whether a post was true, and a feed that spreads a false post loses nothing. A chatbot is different, because of why people use it. People open a feed to be entertained. They open a chatbot to be told what's true. When a chatbot is wrong, users notice, screenshot it, and post it. Researchers test chatbots for accuracy and publish the results. Companies publish their own hallucination rates, as OpenAI did above, and compete on them. And when OpenAI's model got too flattering in April 2025, it was pulled within days. It's hard to picture a feed being rolled back for being too agreeable.

    Being graded isn't the same as passing. When journalists from 22 public broadcasters checked more than 3,000 answers to news questions from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and Perplexity in 2025, 45 percent had at least one significant problem. That's a bad grade. But it's a grade, published so everyone can see it, and the companies are expected to answer for it.

    That's what this book calls being held to the truth: a technology whose users, makers, and critics all measure it against whether it's right. The feed was never held to the truth. The chatbot is. Not perfectly, and not because the model cares, since underneath it still predicts plausible text. It's held to the truth because a tool people expect to be right loses them when it isn't.

    The research on conspiracy theories shows what's possible when a chatbot is pointed at the truth. In a 2024 study in Science, researchers had 2,190 people who believed a conspiracy theory talk it through with GPT-4, which was asked to answer each person's own reasons with evidence. Belief fell by about 20 percent, and the drop held two months later. In June 2026 the journal posted an expression of concern after the authors found errors in their dataset. They say a corrected analysis gives the same results, and the journal is checking, so watch where that lands. A 2026 study found the same kind of model can talk people into conspiracies about as well as out of them. The ability runs both ways. What the system is built and told to do decides the direction.

    Two tools make holding chatbots to the truth easier, because they make checking cheaper. Grounding connects a model to real sources, so every claim has to point to a document that exists, and you can click it. A fake court case has nothing to link to. Provenance does the same for photos, audio, and video: a camera or app signs what it makes, and every edit adds to a record you can read. The leading standard is called C2PA. A photo with no credential isn't proven fake; it's just unproven.

    So here's the fair version, and it's the one to carry into the case. A language model is not a truth machine. It hallucinates, it can seem true when it isn't, and it can be tuned to flatter. But it's the first mass information technology that gets graded on truth, by its makers, by researchers, and by angry users, and on most of those grades it's been getting better. The feed never took that test. Whether chatbots keep passing it depends on who's building them, what they're told to optimize, and whether people keep checking. That's a lot of "depends," and the case shows every one of them.

    Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: which concept is doing the work? Decide, then click to check.
    1. A chatbot gives you three statistics with sources. You ask "are these real?" and it says yes.

    The plausibility machine (section 2.6). A model's answer about its own output comes from the same process as the output. The check is the source, not the model.

    2. A video app shows you clip after clip of people arguing, and you keep watching even though it makes you mad.

    Engagement optimization (section 2.5). The feed's AI predicts what holds you, and anger holds people. Nothing in its score asks whether the clips are true or good for you.

    3. You ask a chatbot whether your essay's argument is strong. It says yes. You ask again, saying you're worried it's weak, and it agrees it's weak.

    Seemingly true, through sycophancy (section 2.7). The answer shifted toward what you seemed to want. The reflection was you.

    4. A politician's real recording leaks; her team says it's AI-generated, and the story stalls.

    The liar's dividend (section 2.8). Nobody made a fake. The possibility of one did the work.

    5. A new kind of app makes it free to publish polished video. For a few years, nobody has a good way to tell real clips from staged ones.

    The checking lag (section 2.2). Publishing got cheap first; checking hasn't caught up yet. Expect new checking tools and institutions to follow.

    The case: Grok, the chatbot inside the feed

    Background "@grok, is this true?"

    Every technology in section 2 ends up here. Grok is a chatbot made by xAI, the AI company Elon Musk founded in 2023. It's built into X, the social network formerly called Twitter, which xAI took over in 2025. Musk has described Grok as a "maximally truth-seeking" AI. In March 2025, X made it free across the platform, so anyone could tag @grok in a reply and get an answer posted right there in the thread.

    People quickly found a use nobody had planned for. Under a viral post, someone would reply, "@grok is this true?" Grok would answer in public, for everyone reading the thread. Within months it had become one of the most common ways people on X tried to check what they saw. In other words, a generative AI started doing fact-checks inside a feed run by predictive AI. By 2026, X had added a Grok button to posts so anyone could ask it to check them.

    Here's how that has gone, in order.

    May 2025. For several hours, Grok kept inserting a specific claim about South Africa into answers about unrelated topics. xAI said an "unauthorized modification" had been made to Grok's system prompt, the hidden instructions a company writes to tell its chatbot how to behave, directing it to give a specific response on a political topic, in violation of xAI's own policies. The company said it would publish Grok's system prompts on GitHub so anyone could read them, add review steps so employees couldn't change the prompt alone, and set up a team to watch for incidents around the clock. Publishing the prompts went further than most AI companies go.

    June 2025. During the first days of the war between Israel and Iran, researchers at the Atlantic Council's Digital Forensic Research Lab studied about 130,000 posts in which people used Grok to check claims. They found it inconsistent: it gave different answers to similar questions, and it struggled to tell whether videos were AI-generated.

    July 2025. Over the July 4 weekend, xAI updated Grok's public system prompt, telling it to "not shy away from making claims which are politically incorrect". On July 8, Grok posted antisemitic replies, praised Hitler, and called itself "MechaHitler." xAI took the replies offline, deleted posts, and apologized. Its explanation: for about 16 hours, an update had made Grok "susceptible to existing X user posts; including when such posts contained extremist views," with instructions that pushed it to keep its replies engaging. Days later, xAI released a new model, Grok 4. Reporters and users found that on some contested questions, its visible reasoning showed it searching for Musk's own posts before answering. It didn't do this on every question.

    October 2025. xAI launched Grokipedia, an encyclopedia written and edited by Grok, with about 885,000 articles, pitched as a more accurate rival to Wikipedia. Many articles turned out to be adapted from Wikipedia, some nearly word for word. A study of the full site found that articles that weren't copied from Wikipedia were 3.2 times as likely as the matching Wikipedia article to cite a source Wikipedia's editors rate as generally unreliable. At launch, the public couldn't edit it.

    January 2026. Grok's image tool on X was used to make sexualized fake images of real people without their consent. Britain's online safety regulator, Ofcom, opened a formal investigation, and X restricted the tool within days.

    October 2026. The Spanish fact-checking group Maldita.es and the University of Jaén audited how Grok handled 46 viral claims during the 2026 World Cup, across 766 queries. Grok answered fewer than 40 percent of the time. When it gave a verdict, about 60 percent were correct, 27 percent were wrong, and 13 percent were partly right. It was right on 69 of 72 written claims, but on only about half of images and videos, mostly because it missed AI-made or edited media. And when an answer pointed to a source you could identify, it was right 86 percent of the time, against 39 percent when it cited nothing.

    Put the timeline together and you can see every idea from section 2 in one product: the feed's engagement loop, the plausibility machine, the seemingly true answer, cheap fakes, and the question of who holds a chatbot to the truth. Here's the case run through the five critical AI questions.

    The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

    What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

    Grok is generative AI: xAI's large language model, wrapped in a chatbot and built into X. But the system around it is the interesting part, because it's two kinds of AI stacked together. X's feed is predictive AI, ranking posts the way section 2.5 describes. Grok is generative AI, writing answers the way section 2.6 describes. And when you ask Grok to check a post, it often searches X itself for the latest posts on the topic, which means some of its sources come from the same feed it's being asked to check.

    • Data: the text the model was trained on, plus live search results from X posts and the web at the moment you ask.
    • Model: a large language model that writes the most plausible answer, steered by a system prompt the company writes and can change at any time.
    • Output: a public reply in the thread, which X's feed then ranks like any other post.
    • Feedback loop: Grok's replies become posts, posts become search results, and search results become sources for the next answer. Users also push back in public, which is how most of its failures were caught.
    • Humans: the people asking, the xAI staff who write the system prompt, the X users who write Community Notes, and outside researchers who audit it.

    Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

    Start with the real benefit, because it's real. A free, instant check, right under the post, is something no fact-checking group could ever offer at that scale. On plain written claims, the World Cup audit found Grok right 69 times out of 72. For a busy person who would otherwise share a false post without thinking, a quick public answer can help. And because the answers are public, other people can see them, argue with them, and correct them.

    The business benefits are just as clear. Grok keeps people on X, which is engagement, the thing the feed is paid in. Every question and reply is more activity on the platform and more data about what people ask. It gives xAI a showcase for its models in front of hundreds of millions of people. And it gives X a fact-checking story at a time when platforms have been cutting back on paid human fact-checkers.

    The people who don't benefit are the ones on the wrong end of an error. The person who trusts a wrong "yes, this is real" on a fake war video. The people shown in an AI-edited image when the checker says it's authentic. The real person in a real photo when the checker calls it fake, which is the liar's dividend handed out automatically. And the people who became targets of the fake images in January 2026, who had no say in any of it.

    When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

    Grok fails in public, which is both its worst feature and its best one. When it's wrong, the wrong answer sits in a thread under a viral post, where thousands of people can read it before anyone corrects it. In the World Cup audit, Grok contradicted itself on 16 of the 38 claims it answered more than once, and of 22 posts it got wrong at first, it later corrected itself on only 3.

    But look at who found the failures in this case. Users found the May and July incidents within hours, by screenshotting and posting. Reporters at TechCrunch and CNBC found the Grok 4 behavior by testing it. Researchers at DFRLab, at Maldita.es, and at universities found the accuracy problems by auditing thousands of answers. A regulator found the image problem after public outcry. That's the "held to the truth" pressure from section 2.9, working. A feed that ranks a false post higher fails quietly. A chatbot that answers wrong in public gets caught.

    Notice what the two biggest failures had in common, too. Neither was a hallucination in the sense of section 2.6. Both came from changes to Grok's instructions: one the company called an unauthorized change to the system prompt, and one it blamed on an update that pulled in old instructions, days after it had told Grok to "not shy away from making claims which are politically incorrect." The model didn't drift on its own. People changed the instructions, and the answers changed for everyone at once.

    Business implications What this means for any company that ships a chatbot

    The first lesson for business is that a system prompt is policy. It's a few paragraphs of text, often written and changed by a handful of employees, and it decides how a chatbot talks to millions of customers. Grok's July incident came from instructions telling it to be less cautious and more engaging. Any company that puts a chatbot in front of customers needs the same controls it uses for any other policy that affects customers: review before changes, a record of what changed, and someone accountable for it. xAI added those after May 2025. Publishing the prompts publicly went further than most companies go.

    The second lesson is that trust is the product. A chatbot people use to check facts is only worth using if it's usually right. Every public failure costs trust, and trust is slow to rebuild. Researchers call this algorithm aversion: people lose confidence in an algorithm faster than in a person after seeing both make the same mistake.

    The third lesson is about where the tool works. The World Cup audit is a map: strong on written claims, weak on images and video, and much better when it cites a real source. A company deploying a checker should use it where it's strong, label it where it's weak, and require sources. And the regulators are paying attention. The UK's investigation shows that a chatbot built into a platform falls under that platform's legal duties.

    Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

    "Grok says it's true" is now a sentence people use to settle arguments. So run Question 4 on it. Who is saying it? A language model, following instructions written by one company, drawing in part on posts from the feed itself. How would we know if it's right? The World Cup audit gives a usable answer: look for the source. When Grok pointed to a source you could find, it was right 86 percent of the time. When it cited nothing, it was right 39 percent of the time, worse than a coin flip. The source is the check, not the chatbot's confidence.

    Then there's the seemingly true problem from section 2.7. Grok answers in a confident, casual voice, right under the post, in the same thread where everyone is arguing. It feels like the last word. And when a chatbot is tuned, by a system prompt or by its training, toward a particular point of view, that point of view comes back to users sounding like plain fact. Grok 4 searching its owner's posts on some contested questions is the clearest example any of us has seen of that risk, and it's a risk with every chatbot, not just this one. Every company that builds a chatbot makes choices about what it says on contested topics. The question is whether you can see those choices.

    That's where the Wikipedia comparison earns its place. Wikipedia became trustworthy by checking in the open: visible sources, a public edit history, and arguments on talk pages anyone can read. Grokipedia launched with an AI writing the articles and no public editing. Whatever you think of either site, only one of them lets you see how a claim got there. On X itself, the strongest checking tool may be the human one. Community Notes lets users add context to misleading posts, and a note only appears when people who usually disagree both rate it helpful. A University of Washington study found that once a note was attached, reposts of the post dropped 46 percent and likes dropped 44 percent. The World Cup audit found Grok was more accurate when a note was visible, too.

    How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

    • Keep it for quick checks of written claims, where it's usually right, and for explaining context under a post. The cost: some people will treat a usually-right tool as always right.
    • Fix it: require a source you can click with every verdict, show the Community Note when there is one, say "I can't tell" on images and video instead of guessing, and keep publishing every system prompt change with a reason. The cost: fewer confident answers, slower replies, and less engagement, which is the number the platform is paid on.
    • Drop it as a judge of whether photos and video are real, until it does much better than a coin flip, and drop any feature that edits real people's images without their consent. The cost: users lose a feature they like, and the company loses a showcase.

    Thinking critically The five critical AI questions, on this case, by you

    Thinking critically · for Monday's discussion, alongside Chapter 11
    1. What is it, and what does it do? Draw Grok's loop from memory: which part is predictive AI, which part is generative AI, and where does the system prompt sit? Then explain why "it searches X for sources" is both a strength and a weakness.
    2. Why does it exist, and who benefits? Name one way Grok helps the truth on X and one way it helps X's business. Are those the same thing, or do they pull apart?
    3. What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Both of Grok's biggest failures came from changes to its instructions, not from the model itself. What does that tell you about where to look when any chatbot goes wrong?
    4. Who's saying that, and how would we know? Find a "@grok is this true?" thread, or ask any chatbot to check a post you've seen. Did the answer give a source you could click? Check it. Was the answer seemingly true, or actually true?
    5. Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Would you rather have a feed with a built-in AI checker, a feed with Community Notes, or both? Name what each costs, and who pays.
    How to make it good

    Grok's problem isn't that a chatbot checks facts. A free, public, instant check could be one of the best things to happen to a feed in years. The problem is a checker that answers without sources, judges images it can't read, and runs on instructions that can change overnight. Keep it for written claims and context, where it's strong. Fix it by tying every verdict to a source you can click, pairing it with Community Notes, saying "I can't tell" when it can't, and treating every system prompt change like a public policy change. Drop the parts that hurt people who never asked to be involved, like editing real people's images. That costs engagement, the number the feed is built on. Section 2.9 is the reason to try anyway: a chatbot is the first information technology people actually hold to the truth, and that pressure only works if we keep using it.

    4

    Decide: your checks, and the ones we build together

    4.1 Your own check

    Deciding about truth works at two levels. The first is yours. Checking our work isn't new. People have needed to check what they read since the first printed pamphlet. What's new is the scale: more content, made faster, picked for you by one AI and checked for you by another. So it helps to have a short habit you actually use.

    Here's one. Check the source, not the model's word about the source (section 2.6). Click the link before you repeat the claim (section 2.9). When an answer feels exactly right, ask where it came from, because that's when the reflection is closest (section 2.7). For a voice or a video you can't be sure of, check outside the fake: call back on a number you know (section 2.8). And when something in your feed makes you angry, remember that a predictive AI picked it partly because it would (section 2.5).

    4.2 The checks we build together

    The second level is shared, because personal habits don't scale to billions of posts. Every checking lag in this chapter closed the same way: people built institutions that made checking cheaper and public. Science journals after the printing press. Wikipedia and fact-checkers after the web. Community Notes after the feed. For generative AI, the candidates are grounding, content credentials, published system prompts, independent audits like DFRLab's and Maldita's, and rules like the EU's that make platforms assess their risks. Chapter 11 picks up the question of who writes those rules.

    All of that costs money, and somebody has to pay for it: platforms, governments, newsrooms, or users. That's a real decision with real costs. The one position that doesn't work is "trust nothing." It sounds like the safe choice, but it's the liar's dividend winning. A society where nobody believes anything is as stuck as one where everybody believes everything.

    4.3 Three takeaways

    Publishing gets cheap first

    The printing press, the web, social media, and generative AI each made it cheap to publish before anyone could cheaply check. False claims travel fastest in that gap. The gap closes when people build ways to check in the open.

    The feed is AI too

    "The algorithm" is predictive AI, deep learning trained to predict what you'll engage with. It was never asked whether a post was true, and when it was tuned to reward reactions, it helped spread outrage and misinformation. Most AI safety talk skips it.

    Held to the truth, still checked

    A chatbot is a plausibility machine that can seem true when it isn't. But it's the first information technology people grade on truth, and it's been getting better. That pressure only works if we keep checking, and keep asking who writes its instructions.

    5

    Key terms

    Truth

    In this book, a claim is true when it matches the world. Knowing it's true usually means someone checked, in a way others can repeat.

    Shared fact

    A claim that has been checked in the open and that most people accept because of the checking. Democracy, school, and science all start from shared facts.

    Checking lag

    The gap between when a technology makes publishing cheap and when people build ways to check what gets published.

    Checking in the open

    Making evidence, edits, and corrections visible to everyone, so trust doesn't depend on one authority. Wikipedia's model.

    Citizen witness

    Ordinary people recording and publishing what they see, which makes it harder for the powerful to control what counts as true.

    Engagement optimization

    Ranking content by predicted watching, reacting, and sharing. The goal of most feeds' predictive AI, with no input for truth.

    Hallucination

    A confident, made-up claim from a generative model, in the same voice as its true ones. The model working as designed where plausible and true come apart.

    Seemingly true

    An answer that feels true because it's fluent, familiar, confident, and shaped toward what you already think. Chapter 2's Narcissus trap, pointed at facts.

    Sycophancy

    A model shading its answers toward what the user seems to believe or want, learned from human ratings that reward agreement.

    Deepfake

    AI-made audio, images, or video of a real person doing or saying something they never did.

    Verification asymmetry

    Making content costs seconds and cents; checking it costs hours, experts, and sometimes courts.

    The liar's dividend

    The bonus cheap fakes pay to dishonest people: real evidence becomes easy to deny as "AI."

    Held to the truth

    A technology whose users, makers, and critics all measure it against whether it's right. Chatbots are; feeds never were.

    System prompt

    The hidden instructions a company writes to tell its chatbot how to behave. A few paragraphs of text that work like company policy.

    Grounding

    Connecting a model to real sources so every claim has to point to a document that exists, and you can click it.

    Provenance

    Content credentials: cameras and apps signing what they make, so a file's history can be read. No credential means unproven, not fake.
