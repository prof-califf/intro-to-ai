Truth: The Plausibility Machines

The systems we've studied all quarter share one design fact this chapter finally faces head-on: none of them was built to tell the truth. They were built to be plausible, and plausible is cheaper than true, faster than true, and increasingly hard to tell from true. What does a society do when the difference stops being visible?

Hallucination is not a bug · the phantom citations · the liar's dividend

Links print with full web addresses; the try-it answers print open.
Learning objectives
  1. Explain what a language model optimizes for, and why confident fabrication is not a separable bug.
  2. State the verification asymmetry and give an example of it in time and money.
  3. Explain the liar's dividend, and why the deepest damage runs through true things.
  4. Describe the two main ways to make verification cheap again, grounding and provenance, and what each does and doesn't do.
  5. Compare a feed and a chatbot on what each is optimized for, and say why the same model can debunk a conspiracy or sell one.
  6. Run the five critical AI questions on Mata v. Avianca and build a personal verification reflex from it.
1

Built for plausible, not true

If this book has one sentence to tattoo somewhere before the exam, this chapter's candidate: the machine is never lying, and it is never telling the truth. It is always doing something else, and checking is our job.

That sounds like a riddle. It's a description of a mechanism, and once you see the mechanism, most of what's confusing about AI and truth stops being confusing. This chapter shows you the mechanism, the economics it creates, and the surprising other edge: compared with the feeds it's replacing, a chatbot is the first mass technology with truth anywhere in its objective, and the evidence that it can move people toward the facts is real. Then the case, where a plausibility machine met an institution built on verification, and lost.

2

The concepts: the plausibility machine, and the economics it creates

2.1 What a language model optimizes for

A large language model is trained to produce the most plausible continuation of a text: the next piece that best fits the pattern of everything it has read. Chapter 2 showed you this with a matchbox; Chapter 3 showed you where it sits in a product. Here's the consequence. Truth and plausibility usually overlap, which is why these systems are so often right. But the system has no internal flag that distinguishes them. When it states a fact, and when it invents one with perfect confidence, it is doing the same operation.

Watch it happen on a real example. Ask for court cases supporting an argument, and the model produces a case name, because case names follow prompts like that; an airline in the name, because the prompt was about an airline; a citation shaped like a reporter citation, because that's what follows a case name; a court and a year that fit. Each piece is the most plausible next piece. The finished line looks exactly like a real citation, and at no step did the model check a database, because it has no step for that.

This is why hallucination, the industry's word for confident fabrication, is not a bug being patched out. It's the machine working as designed, in the cases where plausible and true come apart. The systems have gotten meaningfully better at staying aligned with truth, and the grounding tools in section 2.4 help a lot. They have not changed what the model fundamentally optimizes.

Hallucination

Confident fabrication by a generative model: invented facts, quotes, sources, delivered in the same fluent voice as everything accurate. What people get wrong: they picture it as a malfunction. It's the plausibility engine running in territory where plausible and true diverge, and the fluency is the same either way.

2.2 The verification asymmetry

Now the economics. A phantom citation took seconds to generate and hours of professional time to expose. The same shape shows up wherever generation gets cheap. Two days before the 2024 New Hampshire presidential primary, thousands of voters got a robocall in Joe Biden's voice telling them not to vote. It was an AI clone, commissioned by a political consultant who testified that he paid a magician $150 to create the recording. The consequences ran for more than a year: a state investigation, a $6 million federal fine finalized in September 2024, and a criminal trial on 22 counts, at which a New Hampshire jury acquitted him of all of them in June 2025. He argued the primary was an unsanctioned straw poll and the call never used Biden's name. A hundred and fifty dollars of generation consumed investigators, regulators, and courts, and the criminal law, written before voice clones existed, didn't reach him.

That's the chapter's central fact: generation is cheap and verification is expensive, and the gap is widening. For a century, audio was self-authenticating: hearing was believing. That default is gone, and elections are just where the stakes make it visible first. Any system where attacking costs pennies and defending costs hours has a stability problem, and "the information environment" is now such a system.

The verification asymmetry

Fabricating content costs seconds and cents; checking it costs minutes, expertise, and institutional time. Whoever benefits from noise can now produce it faster than anyone can clean it up. What people get wrong: they treat this as a problem of gullible people. It's a problem of cost, and the fixes are things that make checking cheap.

2.3 The liar's dividend

The deepest damage doesn't come from the fakes. It comes from what the possibility of fakes does to real evidence. Legal scholars call it the liar's dividend: once everyone knows audio can be faked, anyone caught on real audio can say "deepfake," and some people will believe them, and everyone else has to do work to prove otherwise. The technology pays dishonest people a dividend without their generating anything at all.

Follow that to its end state and you get the real stakes of this chapter: not a world where people believe false things, but a world where people can't be made to believe true ones, where every inconvenient fact has a plausible exit. A society can survive some lies. It cannot govern itself without a shared way to settle what happened.

The liar's dividend

The bonus fakery pays the dishonest: real evidence becomes deniable. What people get wrong: they count the harm in fakes believed. Count it in truths dismissed, and the number is larger.

2.4 Making verification cheap again

If the problem is a cost gap, the fixes are things that close it from the checking side. Two matter most, and you'll use both.

Grounding, for text. Connect the language model to a real database and require every claim it makes to point at a document that exists. The model still predicts plausible text, but plausible now has to link to something, and a phantom has nowhere to link. This is how legal research tools now work, and it's why the Avianca mistake is harder to make today than it was in 2023, though not impossible: the model can still misread a real document, and the reader still has to click.

Provenance, for photos, audio, and video. A camera signs what it captures with a cryptographic credential; every edit adds to the record; a viewer can check the chain. The leading standard, called C2PA, is backed by camera makers, software companies, and newsrooms, and its adoption is partial. Notice what it does and doesn't do: a photo with no credential isn't proven fake, it's just unproven. Hearing was believing; now the credential is.

Provenance

Cryptographic content credentials: cameras and tools signing what they produce, so that a viewer can check where a file came from and what changed. What people get wrong: they expect it to detect fakes. It doesn't. It makes real things provable, which is the side of the asymmetry worth fixing.

2.5 The other edge: AI built for truth, and the feed it's competing with

Everything so far has treated generative AI as a truth problem. Hold that against the technology it's actually displacing for many people, the feed from Chapter 3, and the picture changes. A feed is paid by attention. It learned, without anyone intending it, that outrage holds people, and nobody at the company is judged on whether a post was true. A chatbot is judged on whether its answer was right. Users notice when it's wrong, competitors advertise on it, and the labs train their models for honesty, by their own account, which is a claim we hold them to rather than take on faith. Neither system is built for truth by nature. But one of them has truth in the objective, imperfectly, and the other doesn't have it at all.

Now the evidence, because this shouldn't rest on a diagram. In 2024, three researchers, Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook, and David Rand, put 2,190 conspiracy believers into three rounds of conversation with GPT-4, which was asked to respond to each person's own reasons with evidence. Belief in the person's chosen conspiracy fell by about 20 percent, the effect held two months later, and it worked even on people with deeply entrenched views. Decades of human fact-checking had rarely moved believers at all; a model that could answer their specific argument did. A 2025 follow-up with 1,224 people used a different lab's model on antisemitic conspiracies and found the same thing. The mechanism is the plausibility machine again, pointed the other way: it can produce the specific, tailored counterargument a believer has never actually heard.

Two caveats, and both are course material. First, Science posted an editorial expression of concern on the 2024 paper in June 2026. That's a journal saying it's looking into something; it's not a retraction and it's not an all-clear, and the replication stands on its own. Check where it lands, and run Question 4 on whatever you find. Second, a 2026 study found the same models persuade people into conspiracies about as effectively as out of them. The capability is symmetric. The objective decides the direction, which is exactly the point of the diagram: what a system is built to optimize for is the whole difference between a debunking tool and a propaganda machine, and it's a choice someone makes.

So the honest version of this chapter's claim is narrower than "AI is a truth problem." Generation got cheap, and that created the asymmetry and the dividend, which are real and getting worse. And, at the same time, the most capable tool anyone has ever had for tailored, evidence-based correction just arrived, and whether it's used that way depends on who's paying for it and what for. Both edges. The chapter's job is to make sure you can see both.

The objective decides

A model that can produce the perfect tailored counterargument can produce the perfect tailored lie; which one you get depends on what it's optimized for and who's paying. What people get wrong: they ask whether AI is good or bad for truth. Ask what the system in front of you is judged on, and by whom.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: which concept is doing the work? Decide, then click to check.
1. A chatbot gives you three statistics with sources. You ask "are these real?" and it says yes.

Section 2.1. A model's assurance about its own output is the same operation as the output. The check is the source, not the model.

2. A politician's real recording leaks; her team says it's AI-generated, and the story stalls.

The liar's dividend. Nobody made a fake. The possibility of one did the work.

3. Your research tool shows a citation as a link; you click and the document opens.

Grounding. The model could only cite what's in the database, and you just did the ten-minute check in ten seconds.

4. A viral photo has no content credential.

Provenance, correctly read: unproven, not fake. The absence of a credential is a reason to check, not a verdict.

5. A scammer clones a grandson's voice from a video and calls his grandmother.

The verification asymmetry. Seconds to make, and the person who has to verify is the one least equipped to. The fix is a family password, which is cheap verification.

3

The case: the phantom citations

Background A serving cart, a brief, and six cases that didn't exist

In 2022, a passenger sued the airline Avianca in federal court in New York, saying a metal serving cart had struck his knee on a flight. A routine case. In 2023, his lawyers filed a brief citing multiple court cases supporting their argument: cases with convincing names, plausible citations, quotable passages. Avianca's lawyers couldn't find them. Neither could the judge. Because they did not exist. One of the lawyers had asked ChatGPT for supporting cases; it produced them, complete and confident and fictional. Asked directly whether the cases were real, it assured him they were. When the judge ordered copies, what came back was fabricated too. On June 22, 2023, the court dismissed the case and fined the lawyers $5,000, and Mata v. Avianca became the first famous collision between a plausibility machine and an institution built on verification.

Here's what makes it a chapter case rather than a funny story: it keeps happening. Courts and researchers now track AI-fabricated citations in legal filings, and the running counts are in the hundreds, after every lawyer in America heard about the first one. The lesson isn't that some lawyers are lazy. It's structural: the fabrication arrives pre-formatted in the genre of reliability. A phantom citation looks exactly like a real one; the fluency that makes the tool useful is the same fluency that makes its failures invisible. Monday's discussion hands you two of the actual phantom citations from the actual case and asks how you'd catch them, with a clock running, for a reason section 2.2 explained.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

Generative AI in its purest form: a general chatbot asked to do a retrieval task it was never built for. The five parts: data, the internet's text, including a great deal of legal writing, which is why the phantoms were so well-formed; model, a plausibility engine with no lookup step; output, a brief's worth of citations, real and fake in the same voice; feedback loop, none that mattered here, though every fabricated citation now in a court record is a document the next model may read; humans, a lawyer who treated a writer as a librarian, opposing counsel who was paid to check, and a judge. Beyond the one case, the truth problem is three chapters working together: the model that fabricates, the platforms that distribute, and the recommendation systems from Chapter 3 that decide what spreads.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

The tool exists to produce plausible text cheaply, at any volume. Who benefits: anyone who needs a lot of words fast, and anyone for whom plausible is enough: a lawyer in a hurry, a campaign, a scammer with a voice sample. Who doesn't: the client whose case was dismissed for his lawyers' shortcut; the court, which spent its time on a brief that should never have been filed; and, once the counts reached the hundreds, every honest lawyer whose real citations now get a second look. Generation is cheap; verification is expensive; that gap is the business model, and the costs land on the people doing the checking.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

It fails as epistemic pollution: no crash, no recall, just a rising cost of knowing anything. Who finds out is the asymmetry question: whoever can afford verification. In Avianca, opposing counsel was paid to check, which is why this one was caught, and why it became a case at all. Most of life has no such adversary. The fabricated statistic in a term paper, the invented quote in a marketing deck, the phantom source in a news summary: those get found out later, or never.

Business implications What this means for anyone who ships words

Every business now produces text with these tools, and the Avianca lawyers are the case study nobody wants to be. Three lessons. First, know which task you're asking for. Drafting, summarizing, and rephrasing are what the model does; retrieving facts is not, unless it's grounded, and a company that confuses the two has a Chapter 3 "narrator, not decider" problem with legal exposure. Second, the check has to be in the workflow, not in the good intentions. "Someone reviews it" fails the way "on the loop" fails in Chapter 7; "every citation links to its source before it leaves" holds. Third, the signature rule: whatever your company publishes with AI, a named person signs, and that person checked anything the company could be held to. The costs are the minutes the lawyer didn't spend, multiplied across a company, plus grounded tools that cost more than a free chatbot. The alternative cost was a dismissed case, a sanction, and a name that now appears in every textbook on the subject, including this one.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

"The AI confirmed the cases were real" is what the lawyer said the AI said. A chatbot's assurance about its own output is a claim from a source with no way to check itself. What would settle it: the docket, which took ten minutes. That's the whole lesson, and it scales up. A society that can't afford the ten minutes, or has been talked out of believing what the ten minutes show, is the liar's dividend at full size. The structural answers are the ones in section 2.4, plus the institutions that do expensive checking for a living: courts, journalism, science. All three are under-funded relative to what generation now costs, and deciding to pay for referees is a political choice, which is Chapter 11's subject.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep the tool for drafts, summaries, and the argument you forgot. The cost: the discipline to remember which task you're in.
  • Fix it: ground it, so every claim links to a source; sign what you produce; check anything you'd be held to. The cost is the ten minutes the lawyer didn't spend, and paid tools instead of free ones.
  • Drop it where the checker doesn't exist, and say what knowing costs when it becomes a luxury good.
How to make it good

The lawyers' mistake wasn't using the tool. It was using it as a source. Keep AI for what it's good at in legal work: summarizing a long record, drafting a first pass, finding the argument you forgot. Fix the workflow so that every citation it produces is checked against the real database before it leaves the office, which is exactly what grounded legal research tools now do; the cost is the minutes that checking takes, which is the point. Drop asking a general chatbot to find cases at all. The cost is nothing, because it was never able to. And keep section 2.5 in view: the same machine that wrote the phantoms is, pointed the other way and paid for being right, the best correction tool anyone has built. The lawyers had the good version available. They asked it the wrong kind of question.

4

Decide: the reflex, and the referees

Deciding operates at two levels. Personal: adopt a verification protocol and make it a reflex. Question 4 has been rehearsing it all quarter. Asking how we'd know was always the point; this chapter just showed you the stakes. Structural: personal vigilance doesn't fix an asymmetry; cheapening verification does. That means provenance standards that let cameras sign what they capture, grounded tools that make claims clickable, platform accountability for what gets amplified, institutions funded to do the expensive checking, and it means deciding, as citizens, that we'll pay for referees.

The mature position, one last time, refuses both exits: not "trust nothing" (that's the liar's dividend winning) and not "it'll sort itself out" (the asymmetry says it won't). See it. Question it. Decide. That was never advice about AI. It was advice about being a citizen of what comes next.

5

Key terms

Plausibility optimization

What language models actually do: produce the most likely continuation. Truth is a frequent correlate, not the target.

Hallucination

Confident fabrication, the plausibility engine running where plausible and true diverge. Not a separable bug.

Deepfake

AI-fabricated audio, image, or video of a real person. The end of self-authenticating media.

Verification asymmetry

Generation costs seconds; checking costs hours. The economics underneath every case in this chapter.

The liar's dividend

The bonus fakery pays the dishonest: real evidence becomes deniable. The deepest damage runs through true things, not false ones.

The objective decides

A feed is paid for attention; a chatbot is judged on being right. Same raw material, opposite failure modes, and the difference is a design choice.

Grounding

Connecting a model to a real database so every claim has to point at a document that exists. The fix for phantom citations.

Provenance

Cryptographic content credentials, cameras and tools signing what they produce, the leading attempt to make real media provable.
