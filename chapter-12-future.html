The Future, and What We Learned to See

Eleven chapters, eleven kinds of AI, and the same two things kept happening. AI held up a mirror to flaws that were already there, and it magnified whatever its builders put into it. Which one you got was never up to the AI. Someone decided. This chapter puts the whole course on one page, then uses it to read the futures people are selling.

1

What eleven chapters added up to

On the first day of this course, a researcher had just quit one of the world's leading AI companies, warning that the industry was racing toward systems it had no plan to control. We didn't ask you to decide whether AI is good or bad. We asked what you'd need to see. Then we spent eleven chapters looking.

We looked at a feed, a drug-finder, a data center, a résumé screener, a robotaxi, a weapon, a payroll dataset, a chatbot that invents court cases, and a camera network in our own town. Different technologies, different industries, different people affected. But the same two things kept happening, chapter after chapter, and they are the most useful thing this course has to give you.

The mirror and the magnifier

AI is a mirror: it shows us flaws that were already in how things worked, often for the first time in a form we can count. AI is also a magnifier: it scales whatever its builders put into it, good or bad, to everyone at once, and it can add flaws of its own. Which one you get is not a property of the AI. Someone decides, in how the system is built, checked, and governed. What people usually get wrong is asking only "is the AI good?" The better question is "what did it show us, what did it multiply, and who chose?"

2

The pattern: a mirror and a magnifier

2.1 The case that shows both at once: bias

Start with the clearest example in the book. In Chapter 6, Amazon built a résumé screener and trained it on years of its own hiring decisions. The model learned to downgrade women. It didn't invent that preference. It learned it from who the company had hired. The bias was in the human decisions all along. The AI just made it impossible to miss.

That's the mirror. And the mirror does something humans rarely do: it makes bias countable. You can't easily send the same résumé to one hiring manager two hundred times and change one detail. You can do it to a model in an afternoon, which is exactly what you did in Lab 6. It's also how iTutorGroup got caught: one applicant, two birthdates, one interview. The Berkeley mortgage study went further. It found that lending algorithms discriminated less than human loan officers on some loans. AI exposed human bias, and in that comparison, it beat it.

Now the magnifier. One biased manager affects the people they hire. One biased model affects every applicant, the same way, every time, at a scale no manager ever could. And it launders the bias into a clean score that looks like arithmetic, so the prejudice is harder to see, not easier. The same Berkeley study that found algorithms less biased also found the remaining gaps still cost Black and Latino borrowers more than $450 million a year.

Hold both halves, because each one alone is a trap. "AI is biased" ignores that the humans it replaced were biased too, sometimes worse. "Humans are biased too" turns into an excuse to stop checking. Chapter 6's answer is still the right one: better than before is not the finish line. Auditing is.

2.2 The same pattern, everywhere we looked

Once you see it in hiring, you see it in every chapter. Here's the course on one page. Each card names what AI exposed about how things already worked, what AI itself added, and who made the call.

Ch 6 · Hiring and lending

Mirror: Loan officers charged equally risky borrowers different rates, and past hiring favored men. The bias was in the human decisions all along.

Magnifier: One model scores every applicant the same way, and its bias looks like arithmetic.

Who decided: The company that trained the screener on its own history, and the lender that bought the model.

Ch 7 · Transportation

Mirror: About 36,640 people died on U.S. roads in 2025, and we treated it as the cost of driving.

Magnifier: The long tail of rare situations, and safety numbers counted by the company that runs the cars.

Who decided: Waymo, and the cities and states that issue the permits.

Ch 4 · Medicine

Mirror: Research follows money and patients, so drugs that could treat rare diseases sat on pharmacy shelves for decades, untested for them.

Magnifier: A prediction is only as thick as the data, so the neglected diseases get the weakest predictions.

Who decided: Every Cure's researchers, and the doctors who test what the graph suggests.

Ch 9 · The economy

Mirror: Many first jobs were codified work that doubled as training, and nobody called it that.

Magnifier: An automating build can cut the pipeline that turns beginners into experts.

Who decided: Whoever designs the job: the manager who decides whether AI replaces the junior or helps them.

Ch 3 · Systems that know you

Mirror: A business built on attention was always optimizing for your return, not your taste.

Magnifier: A feedback loop that shapes what you want, and paid placement you can't see from inside the app.

Who decided: Spotify, and a terms-of-service clause that priced a listener's claim out of reach.

Ch 10 · Truth

Mirror: Professionals who didn't check their sources existed long before chatbots.

Magnifier: Plausible text at no cost, so a phantom citation takes seconds to make and hours to expose.

Who decided: The lawyers who filed without checking, and the opposing counsel who looked.

Ch 11 · Rules

Mirror: Data sharing between agencies that nobody had ever governed.

Magnifier: A system that grew past what its buyer understood, like a police chief who didn't know he had an AI search tool.

Who decided: The departments that turned sharing on, and the vendor that built the search.

Two chapters are missing on purpose. Chapter 2 is about people, some of them young and lonely, who trusted a chatbot that was built to seem like a friend. Chapter 8 is about war, and the people who serve in it. The pattern still applies to both, but a neat card would make it sound simpler than it is. Some stakes deserve more than a card.

2.3 "Compared to what?" is the first question, not the last

The mirror gives you a powerful first question. When someone says an AI system is dangerous, ask: compared to what? Waymo's driverless cars have to be judged against the human drivers on the same roads, not against perfection. A lending model has to be judged against the loan officers it replaced. Plenty of bad arguments about AI fall apart right here, because the status quo they defend was never as good as they assume.

But "compared to what?" can't be the last question, because the magnifier is still running. A system can beat the old way and still do harm at a new scale. It can be safer on average and fail in ways nobody has seen before. It can be less biased than the humans and still cost people hundreds of millions of dollars. So ask "compared to what?" first, and then ask the five questions anyway.

2.4 Someone decides

Look back at the cards. Every one ends with a name: a company, an agency, a manager, a lawyer. Not one ends with "the AI decided." That's not a writing rule. It's the finding. Whether a system mostly holds up a mirror or mostly magnifies harm depends on choices people made: what data it learned from, where a person checks its work, who can see when it fails, and who answers for it. Those are the five questions. They were never a list of complaints about AI. They were a way of finding the person who decided.

Try it · mirror, magnifier, or both?

Five invented situations. For each one, decide whether the AI is mostly showing a flaw that was already there, mostly multiplying one, or both. Then name who decided.

1. A city uses AI to schedule pothole repairs. It sends crews mostly to neighborhoods where residents report potholes through an app.

Both. The mirror: the reporting system always favored people with the time and the phone to use it, and the AI makes that visible in its schedule. The magnifier: it turns that bias into the automatic plan for the whole city. Who decided: the public works department that chose app reports as the only input.

2. A hospital tests an AI triage tool and finds that, for years, nurses recorded pain scores lower for some groups of patients than others.

Mirror. The AI didn't create the gap; the audit of its training data exposed it. Who decides now: the hospital, which can fix the recording or train the tool on the gap.

3. A company's chatbot answers 10,000 customer emails a day, and every one repeats the same wrong refund policy.

Magnifier. One wrong sentence in the instructions, copied ten thousand times a day. Who decided: whoever wrote the policy into the system and didn't check a sample of its answers.

4. A grading assistant flags that two teaching assistants gave the same essays very different scores.

Mirror. The inconsistency was already there; the AI made it countable. Who decides: the instructor, who can recalibrate the graders or ignore the flag.

5. A loan app approves more applicants than the human officers it replaced, but still approves fewer from one neighborhood.

Both, and it's the Berkeley mortgage study in miniature. Better than before, and still not the finish line. Who decided: the lender that built the app and chose whether to audit it.

3

Reading the futures, context by context

Now point the pattern forward. Everyone sells a future for AI: the companies building it, the critics, the investors, your relatives at Thanksgiving. This course gives you a way to read them.

3.1 Three kinds of claims about the future

A prediction names a date and a number: "AI will do half of all coding by 2028." Treat it as a guess to check. A scenario names the chain of events that would have to happen: "if agents become reliable enough, small firms could do what mid-size firms do today." That's more useful, because you can watch each link. An instrument is a measurement that updates on a schedule, like a dashboard, an index, or a map, so you can watch a future arrive or fail to arrive in public. And a slogan, like "AI is going to change everything," is none of these. It asks you to feel, not look.

Every context in this course has its own instruments. Here are three, read through the mirror and the magnifier.

3.2 Three contexts, two possible futures each

Work. The mirror future: AI exposes that much of a first job was codified routine, and companies redesign entry-level roles around judgment and learning, like the call-center study where the newest workers gained the most. The magnifier future: companies use AI to do the junior work and stop hiring juniors, and in ten years there are no experienced people to promote. Which one arrives is a design choice made job by job. The instrument: the Canaries Dashboard, monthly, which shows whether young workers in AI-exposed jobs keep falling behind (Chapter 9).

Hiring. The mirror future: screeners get audited the way you audited one in Lab 6, and companies discover and fix biases their human recruiters had for decades. The magnifier future: unaudited screeners spread, and one vendor's blind spot rejects the same kind of applicant at thousands of companies at once. The instrument: whether employers test and publish their screeners' selection rates, and what courts and agencies do with the cases still in front of them (Chapter 6).

Medicine. The mirror future: AI keeps exposing how much research follows money, and groups like Every Cure point it at the neglected diseases on purpose. The magnifier future: the same tools get pointed only where the data and the money are already thickest, and the gap widens. The instrument: drug approvals that trace back to AI-found candidates, counted every year, and which diseases they're for (Chapter 4).

3.3 The biggest future: the one we started with

The researcher who quit on Day 1 wasn't worried about one context. He was worried about all of them at once: AI systems good enough to improve themselves, faster than anyone can check. Three serious, published camps disagree about whether that happens. Read each with the same habits: name the mechanism, find the instrument, ask who's saying it.

Future one: the fast takeoff. This is the Day 1 researcher's future, and it has a detailed written version: AI 2027, a scenario published in 2025 by a group of forecasters that walks month by month through agents automating AI research itself, recursive self-improvement kicking in, and a world transformed within a few years, with two endings: one where alignment holds and one where it doesn't. Jacob Coxon's resignation this September is this future with a human face: three years of pretraining research at OpenAI and Anthropic, then a public exit warning that both companies were "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives," and a senior Anthropic alignment researcher publicly agreeing, putting the odds of AI-caused human extinction within the decade above ten percent, and saying plainly that the company did not yet have a plan to solve alignment for systems smarter than their overseers. The mechanism this future needs: agents good enough to do AI research, then self-improvement. The instrument: watch for AI systems doing measurable, verified shares of frontier research work. The labs themselves report it, and the Safety Index grades whether anyone independent can check.

Future two: normal technology. The strongest published rebuttal comes from two Princeton computer scientists, Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, in an essay called AI as Normal Technology. Their argument is Chapter 9's, sharpened: even transformative technologies diffuse through institutions slowly (electricity took forty years), and AI has to pass through regulation, liability, retraining, and reorganization the same way. Capability isn't deployment. Intelligence isn't power; power comes from being trusted with resources, and trust is granted slowly by institutions built to be slow. On this reading, the takeoff scenario mistakes a lab benchmark for the world. The mechanism this future needs: none. It's what happens by default when nothing extraordinary occurs. The instrument: the Adoption Monitor's intensive margin, and the Canaries Dashboard. If firms reorganize slowly and labor effects stay gradual and uneven, this is the future arriving.

Future three: flourishing. The most optimistic serious case comes from inside the industry. Dario Amodei, Anthropic's chief executive, in a 2024 essay called Machines of Loving Grace. His scenario: if powerful AI arrives and alignment holds, the payoff is a century of scientific progress compressed into a decade, most diseases cured, mental health transformed, poverty reduced at unprecedented rates, with AlphaFold as the down payment. (A disclosure, as this course always makes: this textbook was drafted with the model his company makes. Weigh accordingly, and notice that the same company houses this future's author and the previous future's most alarmed researcher. That's not hypocrisy; that's the disagreement being real.) The mechanism: the same powerful AI as future one, plus successful alignment. The instrument: EveryCure's clinical pipeline and drug approvals traceable to AI-originated candidates, the kind of science-output data that would show the decade compressing.

Notice what the pattern says about all three. The fast-takeoff camp fears a magnifier with no one deciding. The normal-technology camp expects institutions to stay in the loop, slowly. The flourishing camp hopes for the best mirror ever built, pointed at disease and poverty, but only if alignment holds, which is another way of saying only if someone can still decide. They disagree about speed. They agree that the outcome runs through whether people keep the power to check and to refuse.

Set your tripwires · three futures, one instrument each

An instrument is only useful if you decide in advance what would move you. Pick the one thing you would accept as real evidence that each future is arriving, then grade your own picks. There is no right future here, only picks you can be wrong about in public and picks you cannot.

Nothing is saved, so write your three down somewhere before you close the tab. That is the point of them.

4

Decide: someone looks

The course asked one question on Day 1: can we build a good, beneficial hybrid society? Here is the book's answer. Yes, under conditions, and you spent eleven weeks learning to name them.

Look at where it went right, because it did, repeatedly. A doctor read his own lab results, and a drug that had sat on a shelf for decades reached a patient. One applicant changed a birthdate and caught a company breaking the law. Opposing counsel looked up the citations. Researchers pulled public records and found out who was searching a state's camera networks. A dashboard made an invisible hiring shift visible every month. A researcher quit loudly instead of quietly. Every one of those is the mirror working, and every one has the same cause. Someone looked.

And look at where it went wrong. A screener learned a decade of bias and applied it to everyone. A feed optimized for your return. A camera network grew past what its buyers understood. Every one of those is the magnifier working, and every one has the same cause too. Someone decided, and nobody checked.

Which future arrives is not settled, and this book won't pretend to know. But you know what to do in any of them. Ask compared to what. Ask what it showed us and what it multiplied. Find the person who decided. Then keep looking, because a system that was a mirror last year can become a magnifier next year, the moment someone stops checking.

Mirror. Magnifier. Someone decides.

For the rest of your career, you will be the someone: the analyst who checks the numbers, the manager who designs the job, the employee who asks who's accountable. This course was practice. Now go look.

Key terms

Mirror

What AI does when it exposes a flaw that was already in how things worked, often in a form we can count for the first time.

Magnifier

What AI does when it scales whatever its builders put into it, good or bad, to everyone at once, sometimes adding flaws of its own.

Compared to what?

The first question to ask about any AI system: judge it against what it replaced. Never the last question.

Scenario

A possible future built from a chain of events you can check, written in the plural, paired with the signal that would show it arriving.

Instrument

A dated, scheduled measurement (a dashboard, an index, a map) that lets a claim about the future become true or false in public.

Alignment

Making a system's goals and behavior match what its builders intend. Unsolved for systems more capable than the people checking them.
