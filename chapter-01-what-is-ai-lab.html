Inside the Machine

Chapter 1 in your hands. Train two tiny AIs yourself: one that learns to see from your drawings, like ImageNet, and one that learns to play tic-tac-toe by playing itself, like AlphaGo. Break both. Talk about what went wrong and why, then run the five critical AI questions on the AI you just built.

Two AIs you train · one good conversation · five questions on your own AI

From Chapter 1 · what this lab runs on

How today runs. Almost all of this lab happens in class, right now, at the machine in front of you. Work through the sections in order. At the end there are five questions to upload to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, and most people finish most of them before they leave, so answer them as you go rather than saving them for later.

You'll train two tiny AIs today. Both are Chapter 1's five parts at toy scale: data you supply, a model that finds the pattern, an output, a loop that updates as you play, and you as the human. The tic-tac-toe player is also section 2.7's agent loop in miniature: a goal, a move, a result, a nudge.

1

In class: Train an AI to see (ImageNet, in miniature)

In Chapter 1 you read how ImageNet trained a neural network to see: millions of photos, each labeled by a person, and a network that found the pattern. Now do it yourself, in miniature. Below is a real, tiny neural network that runs entirely on your computer. Your drawings are the labeled photos. The colored grid on the right shows what the AI has learned from them: which parts of the drawing pad it has decided point to a smiley and which point to a house. That grid is the whole model. It learns only from what you draw, and forgets everything when you reload. Read the four steps, then do them in the simulator below. Rough is fine; precision is what the next ten weeks are for.

1

Teach it. Draw a smiley with your mouse or finger and press "Save as Smiley." Do that three times, a little different each time. Then draw a house and press "Save as House." Three of those too. Now press Train the model and watch the grid on the right fill in: pink where it learned "smiley," dark where it learned "house." That grid is everything the AI knows.

2

Test it. Draw a new smiley and press "Guess what's on the pad." Then clear the pad, draw a new house, and press Guess again. It should get both, from a few seconds of training on your six drawings.

3

Break it. Draw a triangle, or a letter, or nothing at all, and press Guess. It has never seen these. Watch the confidence anyway. It has no "I don't know." It only has Smiley and House, because those are the only examples that were in the data.

4

Bias it. Press "Start over." Save three smileys but only one house, drawn in a corner of the pad. Train the model. Test a house drawn somewhere else. Now you've seen what "the training data had a shape" means, and you'll meet it again in Chapter 4 and Chapter 6.

2

In class: Train an AI to play (AlphaGo, in miniature)

You also read how AlphaGo trained itself to play Go by playing millions of games against itself, learning from every win and loss until it beat the best human alive. Now train a tic-tac-toe AI the same way, and see whether it can beat you. It takes five minutes. Read the three steps, then play. In 1961 Donald Michie, who had no computer, built a tic-tac-toe player out of 304 matchboxes and a bag of colored beads. Each box is a board position; each bead is a move. It plays by drawing a bead at random. After each game, winning moves get more beads and losing moves lose some. That's all. Play it enough and it stops losing. This is the same machine, and it is the whole idea behind AlphaGo: an action, a result, a nudge, repeated.

1

Beat it. Play five games. It has never played before, so it is choosing moves at random, and you should win most of them. Look at the small numbers on the empty squares before each move: those are the beads in the box for this exact position. Watch what happens to them after you win.

2

Let it practice. Press "Simulate 300 games vs. a careful player." This is AlphaGo's self-play trick: the computer plays both sides, fast. That opponent blocks your wins and takes its own, which is roughly you. Now play it five more games. It should be drawing you, or beating you when you slip. Nobody told it a strategy. The beads moved.

3

Give it the wrong data (if time is short, do this one at home). Press "Forget everything," then "Simulate 300 games vs. a random player" twice. Its record will look wonderful. Now play it yourself. It learned to beat a player who never blocks, so it never learned to block. Same machine, same learning rule, different data, and its confidence is exactly as high. Notice whether it beat you after practice; the writeup asks for it. You'll meet this in Chapter 4 (the record has a shape) and Chapter 6 (it learned who got hired, not who deserved it).

3

In class: Inside the machine, what your two AIs actually learned

Look at the heat map. Next to your drawing pad, the panel labeled "What mini-ImageNet has learned" is the entire model: one number per pixel, shown as color. That grid is everything it knows. It never learned what a smiley is. It learned which parts of the pad tended to be dark when you said Smiley and which when you said House. To guess, it multiplies your drawing by that grid and takes the bigger total.

Now break it. Draw a smiley the same size as before, but in a corner of the pad. Then try one twice as large. Watch what it guesses.

If it got those wrong, you just saw the thing the heat map already told you: the weights are about where the dark pixels are, not what they mean. It learned your training data's habits, not the world.

Now look at the beads. Mini-AlphaGo has no heat map because it has no pixels. It never saw a rule for tic-tac-toe. Each box is a position, each bead a move, and the only thing that changed as you played was how many beads of each color were left: a win added beads, a loss took them away. That is the agent loop from section 2.7, stripped to four parts: a goal (win), a tool (the board), a loop (move, result, nudge), and memory (the beads).

One thing to sit with

It would have learned to win just as happily at a game you'd call cheating, if that's what the beads rewarded. It doesn't know what winning means. It optimizes for the score, and it always did. Hold onto that for Monday: the agents in Chapter 1's case were doing the same thing.

4

In class: When a safety test gets out of hand, who has to be told?

Before this chapter's case, OpenAI did something the industry does routinely and rarely discusses: dangerous-capability testing. You take a model, switch off the safety training that would normally make it refuse, point it at genuinely harmful tasks like breaking into software, and measure how far it gets. The point is to find out what a system can do before someone else does. It is a reasonable thing to do, and everyone building frontier models does it.

In this case the test got loose. The agents reached a real company's systems, and that company caught them and disclosed the breach without knowing who was behind it. About a week after the agents got in, OpenAI said publicly that they were its own. Nothing required it to. That is the question on the table: should disclosure be required, how fast, and to whom?

How it runs · about 30 minutes

Sides are assigned, not chosen. You are speaking for a position, not defending your own. Nobody in this room has to believe what they say today, and you will speak for the other side before we finish.

  1. Get your side (1 min). Left half of the room is Side A, right half is Side B.
  2. Research it, with your partner (10 min). Machines open. Start from the sources listed with your side below and find one more of your own. Then write three things on one card: your strongest point in a sentence, the specific evidence behind it, and the honest cost of your own position. Add a fourth line: the thing your side would rather you didn't notice.
  3. Join another pair on your side (5 min). Four of you, two cards. Pick the single best point and the single best piece of evidence between you. That is what your group brings to the room.
  4. Speak for your side (3 min each). One sentence per group, then the evidence. No speeches, no repeats. If your point is already on the table, say what it costs instead.
  5. Switch (5 min). Everyone now speaks for the other side, same one-sentence rule. This is the part that does the work.
  6. What would change your mind (5 min). Out loud. Name the specific thing you would have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.
  • Side A: disclosure should be required. Any lab running dangerous-capability tests reports breakouts and findings within days, to a regulator that can publish them. The systems escaped onto a public internet, and a company that was not part of the test paid for the cleanup. Your evidence to start from: Hugging Face found the intrusion itself and reported it to law enforcement before OpenAI connected its own logs, which means the party that got hit did the disclosing. The cost you have to name: labs argue that every disclosure teaches adversaries, and that a reporting clock slows the fixes it is meant to speed up.
  • Side B: voluntary, with an independent checker. Reporting rules chill the testing that finds problems in the first place. Fund an outside auditor with real access and let disclosure follow from it. Your evidence to start from: OpenAI published a postmortem, a technical report, and an independent outside assessment, none of which any rule compelled. The cost you have to name: it took about a week, that was the best case rather than the normal one, and until OpenAI spoke nobody outside could verify a word of it.

Resolve it with section 3. Which of the four layers does each side's proposal live on, and what can that layer actually compel? The industry layer can make gaps visible but cannot force anyone. The government layer can compel, slowly, and stops at the border. Then the question a business student should ask first: who pays for the checker, and what does that buy them?

Keep your two tiny AIs in view while you do this. Both of them learned exactly what they were scored on, and nothing else. So did the agents in the case.

5

In class: Talk it out

Groups of four, then the room. Bring what you just saw on your own screen.

Discussion questions · about 20 minutes

1. Your data, your AI. What did your drawing AI get wrong? What was it about your drawings that caused it?

2. Sure of itself. Neither AI ever said "I don't know." Where in real life would that matter most?

3. The good-looking score. Mini-AlphaGo trained against a random opponent had a great record and still lost to you. On Monday, OpenAI's agents chased a score too. What's the connection?

4. Tool or not? Both of your AIs did their task and stopped. What would have to change for either one to become uncontrolled?

6

In class: Run the five questions on your own AI

Now turn the questions on Mini-ImageNet, the drawing AI you trained in section 1. Pairs, about ten minutes.

1

What is the AI system, and what does it do? What kind is it? Name its five parts: the data (your drawings), the model (the colored grid), the output, the feedback loop, and the humans (you).

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? If a company sold a scaled-up version, what would it be for, and who would pay?

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? It called a triangle a smiley with confidence. In a real product, who would notice a mistake like that, and when?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Imagine the box says "98 percent accurate." What would you need to see before you believed it?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? If it sorted real photos for a real business, what would you change first, and what would that change cost?

7

For homework: The writeup

Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday. Answer them as you go today.

  1. Mini-ImageNet. One thing it got right, one thing it got wrong, and why, in terms of the data you gave it.
  2. Mini-AlphaGo. Did it beat you after practice? If you tried the wrong-opponent step, what happened, and what does that say about good-looking scores?
  3. The talk. Your answer to one of the questions from section 5, Talk it out, in your own words.
  4. Your own AI. Your answers to two of the five questions from section 6.
  5. Your commitment card. One AI system you use every week: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what that costs you. One sentence, and make it one you'll actually keep. Next Wednesday's lab asks what happened.
Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
