Opening Positions

Six claims about the world you're already living in, then six quotes about machines that think. Your job: call the claims true or false on instinct, guess the decade on the quotes, and record what your gut says before the evidence arrives. Screenshot both scores before you leave. The finals session asks whether the same claims still surprise you, and the distance between your two answers is the course, measured.

Six claims · six quotes · one baseline reading of your instincts

1

Call them

Work alone for this part. No searching, no talking, no hedging. Read each claim, mark it True or False, and move on. When all six are marked, the reveal button unlocks. Don't press it yet.

Six claims · instinct only · answer every one
The reveal

2

Talk it out

Now pair up with the person next to you. Compare your six answers without changing them. Then join another pair to make a group of four and work through three questions:

Groups of four · six minutes · before the reveal

1. Find the two claims your group disagreed on most. Each side gives one reason, in one sentence. No winning, just the reasons.

2. Pick the one claim your group most wants to be false. Say why in one sentence. Write it down; we'll collect these on the board.

3. For that claim: what would you need to see, not hear, to believe it? A document, a number, a person, a demo? Be specific.

Then press reveal. Read your result, then read the room's.

3

Which decade?

Round two. Six people, each talking about machines that think. Your only job is to guess when they said it. Alone again, then compare with your partner before the reveal.

Six quotes · guess the decade
The reveal

4

The headline · Day 1 discussion

Claim 3 gets its own conversation, because it happened this week. A researcher who spent three years training frontier models at OpenAI and Anthropic publicly quit, saying the companies believe the technology could kill everyone by the end of the decade and are racing anyway. A senior colleague agreed in public. Critics said it was hype dressed as warning. (Chapter 1 has the details and the links.)

Same groups of four · eight minutes · no verdicts allowed

1. Separate his specific claims (what he says the systems will be able to do; what he says the companies lack) from the headline ("could kill us all"). Write both down. Which is easier to argue about? Which is easier to check?

2. Who benefits if he's right? Who benefits if he's wrong? Include his former employer on both lists, and notice that it appears on both.

3. Look back at the decade quiz. Which quote does his sound most like? Does that make you take it more seriously, or less? Say why.

4. Now the four questions from Chapter 1, run on "a system that improves itself": what kind of AI is it, where is the human, who's accountable, who finds out when it fails? Rough answers. Keep them; Chapter 12 asks you to answer again with eleven weeks of evidence.

Report back one sentence per group: the single thing you'd need to see. That list goes on the board and stays there all quarter.

5

Go hunting

Now the live half. The claims above are the world at course-scale; this part is the world at your scale: what people are actually building with AI, this week, for real work.

The hunt · about 10 minutes

Open the Rundown AI Workflow Hub, a live feed of AI workflows real people use for real jobs. Browse until you find two:

One you'd actually use. Not "cool." Use. For your classes, your job, your side hustle, this week.

One that made you hesitate. Something in it felt off; you may not have words for why yet. That hesitation is data. Keep it.

For each pick, jot three things: what it does in one sentence, where the human is in it, and what you'd want to check before trusting it. Then leave a comment on the one that interested you most: a real question or a real observation, the kind you'd want on your own work.

6

Run the four questions, training wheels edition

Chapter 1 gave you four questions we'll ask of every system all quarter. Run them on your own hesitation pick. First attempt, no grade, rough answers welcome:

1

What kind of AI is this? Predictive, generative, embodied, or a chain of several? Best guess.

2

Where is the human? In the workflow you picked: who checks, who approves, who just receives? If the answer is "nobody," you may have found your hesitation's name.

3

Who is accountable when it fails? If this workflow produces something wrong and it ships, whose problem is it?

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Would anyone even notice? How long would it take?

7

The writeup

Due Friday afternoon · post to Canvas
  1. The baseline. Your two screenshots: the six claims and the six quotes. Which claims did you doubt, and which quote fooled you by the most decades? This gets re-read in the finals session; write one sentence to your future self about which claim you most want to understand by then.
  2. The picks. Your two hub workflows, the one you'd use and the one that made you hesitate, each in one sentence.
  3. The hesitation, named. Your best attempt at saying why the second one made you pause. Rough is fine; precision is what the next ten weeks are for.
  4. The four questions. Your training-wheels answers from section 6.
  5. Your move. One question about AI you want answered by December, specific enough that you'll know if it happened. That's your first commitment card, and the course's promise runs the other way too: check the week tags on the reveal. It's probably on the syllabus.
