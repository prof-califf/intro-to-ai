You Are the City Council

A robotaxi company has applied to operate in Bellingham. You sit on the council. Today you weigh the evidence, write the permit, or refuse it, and defend your conditions to the room. Refusal is a legitimate outcome. So is approval. Undefended is the only wrong answer.

Ten evidence cards · one decision · every condition has a cost

From Chapter 7 · what this lab runs on

The evidence cards are section 2.4 in practice: each is tagged by who's saying it, because the safety claim reads differently by source. The conditions you can attach to the permit are the chapter's Business implications, priced: reporting requirements are section 2.7's track-record keeper, school-zone limits are the long tail (2.6), and a fleet cap is the coverage map (2.5) written by a city instead of a company. Refusal is a legitimate outcome; undefended is the only wrong one.

Below are ten evidence cards drawn from the real robotaxi record. Each is tagged with its source type, company data, journalism, independent research, advocacy, or local context, because a council that weighs all sources equally isn't weighing at all. Read each card, then mark it: does it move you toward yes, toward no, or neither? Your running lean shows at the bottom. Mark honestly; you'll be asked to explain any gap between your evidence and your vote.

Now decide. If you approve with conditions, choose them below, but read the fine print on each: every condition has a cost, usually the risk that the operator walks away, delays, or passes the cost to riders. A permit made entirely of conditions is a denial wearing a costume; a permit with none is a yes you'll own alone. 3a

Inside the machine: what the car is doing while you decide

While the council debates, the system it's debating runs the loop from Chapter 7's section 2.1 about ten times a second: sense, perceive, predict, plan, act. The evidence cards you weighed are mostly about steps two and three, because that's where the failures live. A crash statistic is a claim about how often perception and prediction got it right; a school-bus video is one time they didn't, at the long tail (2.6). The coverage map on one of your cards is the operational design domain (2.5): the streets, speeds, and weather the loop has been proven on, and the edge where the car stops and asks a remote assistant instead of guessing.

Now look at your permit conditions again with the loop in mind. Crash reporting to the city is you deciding who keeps the track record (2.7). A speed limit near schools is you shortening the tail on the step that fails there. A fleet cap is a coverage map written by a city instead of a company. None of your conditions touches the model, because a city can't. Every one of them touches what surrounds it, which is the same lesson as Chapter 11's cameras: the rules are where the public's hands are.

3b

Debate: public good, and the people it displaces

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · pick a side, argue it, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

Your permit priced the company's costs. This debate is about the ones the permit form doesn't have a line for.

  • Motion 1: a city should approve robotaxis if the independent safety data holds, because fewer crashes is a public good that outweighs the jobs lost. For: human drivers kill tens of thousands a year; if the numbers are real, refusing is choosing those deaths. Against: "if the numbers are real" is doing all the work, the company keeps the numbers, and the lives saved are statistical while the drivers displaced are people with names.
  • Motion 2: a city that approves robotaxis should require the operator to fund transition support for displaced drivers. For: the operator captures the profit from the drivers' removal, so it should carry the cost; a per-ride fee would do it. Against: every dollar of fee is a reason to launch in the next city instead, and the drivers there don't get support either.

Then the regulation question Chapter 7 hands to Chapter 11: should this be a city decision at all, or a state one? Bellingham can set conditions Olympia can't police, and Olympia can set conditions Bellingham can't enforce. Whoever decides, name what they can and can't compel.

3

Defend it to the room

Wednesday's Q&A half runs on this: councils pair up and cross-examine. Trade decisions with another student and ask each other, in order: Which single card moved you most, and why that one? Which condition would you drop first if the operator threatened to walk? And the chapter's question in local clothes, whose trust matters more for this permit, the riders' or the pedestrians'? Take notes; the writeup asks what the cross-examination changed.

Then bring in one more adversary, a live one. Paste your issued permit into an AI chatbot with this prompt: “You are the robotaxi operator's counsel. My city council just issued this permit. Argue against my two most burdensome conditions, make the strongest case that we'll walk away or that riders lose out.” You just hired the lobbyist every real council faces. Note its single strongest rebuttal, and whether your condition survives it. If the AI's argument moved you where your partner's didn't, that goes in the writeup too: fluent opposition is now cheap, on every side of every hearing.

4

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? You just regulated a system you can't inspect. What did you actually evaluate instead, and is that what regulators of AI are always really evaluating?

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who benefits from your permit as written: the company, the riders, the city, the drivers it replaces? Who paid for each condition you added?

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Under your permit, when the first incident happens on Holly Street: who answers to whom, in what order, and which condition guarantees the public hears about it? If none, was that a choice or an oversight?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? The evidence cards came from the company, the regulator, the press, and a union. Which did you weight most, why, and what would have changed your vote?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Your permit is the decision. Name its total cost, who bears it, and the one condition you'd drop if the company walked.

5

The writeup

Due Friday, 11:59 p.m. · post to Canvas
  1. The evidence. Your lean tally (screenshot), plus the one card you weighted most and the one you deliberately discounted, with the source-type reasoning for both.
  2. The decision. Your issued permit (screenshot), and a defense of your two most important conditions, or of choosing none, or of denial.
  3. The cross-examination. What your partner's hardest question was, and whether anything in your permit changed because of it.
  4. The five questions. Your answers, treating your own permit as the system under review.
  5. Your move. Your entry-ticket instinct vs. your final vote: did the evidence move you, and which specific card did it? One sentence naming what it takes to change your mind, that's Wednesday's commitment card.
