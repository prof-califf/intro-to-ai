The Seattle Pitch

You're a strategy analyst at a robotaxi company, and your boss wants to launch in Seattle. Before you can pitch the city council, you have to understand what you're selling and what it costs the town. So today you open the car up. You train a tiny neural network to see the road, watch a filter scan a camera frame, write the reward function that teaches the car when to turn, and prompt a world model to invent a street it has never driven. Then you dig into Waymo's own safety data, build the pitch math with Claude, crashes avoided on one side and driver work replaced on the other, try to win a city council vote, and debate the question Seattle is fighting over right now: should the safety numbers win, or the drivers' jobs?

1

In class: Teach it to see (a neural network on road scenes)

Why the toys: the council will ask how your car sees and decides. Sections 1 to 4 show you, so you can answer.

A robotaxi's cameras don't see a pedestrian. They see numbers. A neural network turns those numbers into a label, and it learns how by being shown examples someone already labeled. Today that someone is you. About 12 minutes.

In a real car, the early layers of the network find the clues on their own, straight from the pixels (section 2 shows how). Here they're already found for you, as six clues scored 0 to 1: legs, wheels, tall and upright, moving fast, a big metal body, and flutters like it weighs nothing. Your labels decide what the clues mean.

Mini-Perception · teach a network what's on the road
Your job
  1. Label ten scenes as Pedestrian, Cyclist, Vehicle, or Ignore (things the car can drive through, like a bag or a shadow). After each one, the network retrains on everything you've labeled so far.
  2. Watch it guess first. From the second scene on, the network guesses before you answer. Watch the guesses get better, and watch the lines in the diagram thicken as it settles on what matters.
  3. Press Test it on new scenes. Eight scenes it has never seen arrive. Five are ordinary. Three come from the long tail.
  4. Then fix it the way a company would, with more data. Add four rare scenes the data team labeled, and test again.

What is it?

Scene 1 of 10. Label it.

The network, as you've trained it

Six clues in, two hidden layers, four labels out. Each line is a weight, one of the dials from section 2.2. Thicker means it matters more. Dark lines push toward a label; pink lines push away. The six strongest weights show their numbers, and you can hover over any line to see its value. Before you label anything, these lines are random: that’s where training starts, and the network knows nothing yet. Every time you label a scene, watch the lines shift as the network retrains. A real network has billions of these. Real companies rarely start from random, though. They take a model someone else already trained and fine-tune it on their own labels.

Invented scenes, a real network: 6 inputs, hidden layers of 4 and 3, 4 outputs, trained by backpropagation in your browser. Each new label adds 300 training passes on top of what it already learned. Nothing is saved.

Is this real?

The child stepping out from behind a parked SUV is. On January 23, 2026, a Waymo struck a child who ran into the street from behind a double-parked SUV, two blocks from a Santa Monica elementary school at drop-off time. The child had minor injuries. Waymo says its car braked from about 17 mph to under 6 before contact, and that its model shows a fully attentive human would have hit the child at about 14. Federal regulators opened an investigation into how the car behaves around schools (NHTSA, Axios). A partly hidden person is the long tail at its hardest: most of what the camera sees is the SUV.

That's deep learning, small. Labeled examples go in, weights come out, and every new scene gets a label and a confidence. Three things to carry forward.

It learned your labels, not the world. It never knew what a pedestrian is. It learned which clues went with the word you clicked.

It was confidently wrong. Look at the confidence on the three rare scenes before you added data. A network doesn't know what it hasn't seen, so it doesn't know to be unsure. That's why a robotaxi has an operational design domain (section 2.8): the company decides where it's allowed to be confident, because the network can't.

More data shortened the tail, and the tail kept going. Four labeled scenes fixed most of the failures, and the hardest one, a child mostly hidden by an SUV, is still hard, because most of what the camera sees there really is an SUV. Then a person on a horse showed up, and your four labels don't even have a box for it. That's every recall in section 2.6, one at a time.

Responsible AI · question 1 of 4 from section 2.8

Can anyone explain a mistake?

You had something no real engineer has: every weight, on screen. Use it. The network called the child behind the SUV a Vehicle, at high confidence. Now point to the lines in the diagram that made it say that. Hard, isn't it? Your network has fewer than 60 numbers and you still can't explain its mistake in a sentence. Waymo's has billions.

That's why section 2.8 says a responsible company doesn't count on explaining the network. It counts on testing, records, and the ability to replay a failure and fix it, the way the data team's four labels fixed most of yours.

For the pitch

Picture the hearing. A council member asks: "When your car does something strange on a Seattle street and your own engineers can't say why, how does this city find out what happened?" You can't promise an explanation. Nobody can read billions of weights, and you just found you can't read the few dozen on your screen.

What you can offer is a record: save the sensor data from every incident, and let a reviewer the city picks replay it. That costs you storage, a reviewer's time, and the city seeing data you'd rather keep private. It might not win you a single vote. It's still the only honest answer to the question, and in section 6 you'll decide whether to put it on the table.

In class: How the network sees a frame (a convolutional filter)

In section 1 the clues were handed to you. A real car finds its own, straight from the camera's pixels, with a convolutional network: small filters that slide across the whole frame looking for one pattern each. Here's one filter, sliding. About 6 minutes.

Mini-Camera · slide a filter across one frame
Your job
  1. Pick a filter and press the pink Slide the filter button. At each spot, the pink 3 by 3 box multiplies nine pixels by its nine numbers and adds them up (the math shows under the frame). A big total means "my pattern is here," and that spot turns pink on the map.
  2. Read the four layers below the frame: the pixels, the edges both filters found, the shapes made by combining edges, and the network's verdict.
  3. Change the weather to rain, then fog, and slide again. Watch what happens to the edges, then to the verdict.

What’s an edge? A spot where the picture suddenly jumps from dark to light, like where a person’s body meets the background. Edges are how a network starts to find outlines. Each filter hunts for one direction:

Vertical-edge filterCompares the pixels on its left with the pixels on its right. Same on both sides, the total is near zero. Big difference, big total. So it lights up on up-and-down lines: the sides of the body, arms, and legs.
Horizontal-edge filterSame idea, but compares the pixels above with the pixels below. It lights up on side-to-side lines: the curb, the shoulders, the top of the head.

Two hand-set filters so you can watch them work, plus a simplified shape layer and verdict. Nothing is saved.

Why "convolutional." The filter never changes as it slides. The same nine numbers check every spot in the frame, which is why the network can find a person on the left edge, the right edge, or dead center. Stack layers and the filters build on each other: edges become arms and wheels, those become people and cars. That's the 2012 ImageNet idea from Chapter 1, running many times a second on every camera.

Why the weather mattered. A filter only fires when the match is strong enough. Fog washed out the contrast, so the edges fell under that line and the shapes never formed. Rain added noise, so the filter fired on raindrops too. Nothing about the code changed. The frame did. That's why a coverage map lists weather (section 2.8), and it's why the camera isn't the car's only sensor: lidar and radar see through what blinds a camera, and sensor fusion has to decide whom to believe (section 2.3).

Responsible AI · question 2 of 4 from section 2.8

Does the AI know where it's allowed to be confident?

Compare the fog with section 1, where the network was 100 percent sure and wrong. A car can't always tell when it's out of its depth, so a responsible company draws the line for it: an operational design domain, the streets, speeds, weather, and hours where the car has proven itself. Outside it, the car should stop or ask for help. A small domain isn't a failure. Not knowing you've left it is.

Remember this: Should Seattle's domain include heavy rain on day one? Who should decide where that line goes: the company, the city, or both?

For the pitch

Seattle rains on more days than any of the five cities in your company's safety record. Offering a wet-season test period before full service costs you a season of revenue. Not offering it means your first rainy-night mistake is the headline, and the council will remember you didn't offer.

In class: Teach it to decide (reinforcement learning at a left turn)

Seeing isn't driving. Once the car knows what's out there, it still has to decide what to do, and there's no labeled right answer for that. So the planner learns by trial and error: try, get a score, adjust. That's reinforcement learning, and the score sheet is the reward function. You write it. About 12 minutes.

The task is an unprotected left turn: no green arrow, oncoming traffic, and the car has to pick a gap. Go too early and it risks a crash. Wait too long and it blocks traffic, annoys the rider, and ends up in someone's video. The car's whole habit comes down to one number it learns: how big a gap it needs before it goes. And because perception is never perfect, the car misjudges gaps a little, which is exactly what section 1 showed you.

Mini-Planner · write the reward function, then let the car practice 2,000 turns
Your job
  1. Read the goal and the catch, just below.
  2. Write the score sheet, then train. The sliders are the reward function: points the car gets or loses for each thing that can happen. Start with the chapter's sheet and press Train. The car practices 2,000 turns, then the board tests it.
  3. Watch it drive, then try the other two presets. Each fails a different check. Then write your own sheet that passes.

The goal, and the catch. Two different scores are at work here. The car only sees your score sheet: it practices 2,000 turns and settles on whatever habit earns the most points. It never sees the safety board’s three checks, which are what the city actually wants: finish at least 285 of 300 test turns, 1 crash or fewer, and wait under 8 seconds on average. You can’t tell the car to pass the board. You can only set the points so that a car chasing points passes anyway. The results show up next to the car in step 3.

After you train, this chart shows the car's average score for every 100 practice turns. Look for the climb (the car trying things and learning what scores) and then the flat part (it has settled on a habit). That's reinforcement learning: no one shows it the right answer; the score sheet does all the teaching.

A toy model, not Waymo's, which isn't published. Gaps, misjudgments, and crash odds are invented; the learning is real trial and error in your browser, so two runs can settle on slightly different habits. Nothing is saved.

The car didn't learn to drive well. It learned to score well. When the sheet barely punished waiting, the car waited, forever, because nothing told it a rider was getting angry. When the sheet barely punished crashes, it took gaps a person never would. Every time, it did exactly what the numbers said. Nobody voted on those numbers. Someone in a product meeting picked them.

That's why section 2.4 calls the reward function strategy. Early Waymo cars were famously timid, so in 2025 the company tuned them to be more assertive, and the street felt it: lane changes without signaling, and one illegal U-turn in San Bruno with nobody in the driver's seat. Somebody moved a slider like yours.

Responsible AI · question 4 of 4 from section 2.8

Who answers when it's wrong?

In a real company the numbers are chosen by people in a meeting, and there's no driver to ticket or sue when it goes wrong. Section 2.8 says someone has to own those choices: the company, the city that issued the permit, or both.

Remember this: A robotaxi crashes because of a reward setting. Who should answer for it: the engineer who set it, the executive who approved it, the company, or the city that let it drive? Pick one and say why.

For the pitch

The council can't read your model, but it could read your score sheet: what the car is rewarded for and how much. Offering to show it to an independent reviewer costs you a piece of strategy competitors would love to see. Refusing costs you trust, and after the San Bruno U-turn, "trust us" is a weak pitch.

Your planner just practiced 2,000 left turns. A real one can't practice by crashing real cars, and the long tail is too rare to wait for.     Is this real?

    Yes. In February 2026 Waymo announced the Waymo World Model, built on Google DeepMind's Genie 3, a general world model trained on a very large and varied set of videos. Because Genie 3 learned from video of everything, not just driving, Waymo says it can generate scenes its fleet has never seen, including an elephant in the road, and produce both camera and lidar views of them. Engineers can steer it with plain-language prompts, much like your ingredient rows (Waymo). The announcement didn't include independent tests of how realistic the scenes are.

    Practice can be manufactured; reality can't. Your weirdest scene might take more real miles than the fleet will ever drive, and the world model made it in a second. That's why simulated miles outnumber real ones by a wide margin (section 2.5), and why a competitor can't catch up just by buying the same sensors. But look at the last wildcard. A world model only recombines what's in its video. A simulated pedestrian is the model's idea of a pedestrian, so the practice is honest where the data was and silent where it wasn't.

    Simulation is how a company stretches its domain before it drives somewhere new: practice a rainy Seattle night a million times. But a world model's rain is the model's idea of rain. Practice isn't proof, and the gap between the simulation and the real street is where the next surprise lives.

    Remember this: What would have to be true before simulated Seattle Novembers count as evidence that the car can handle real ones?

    For the pitch

    Simulation is your best answer to "you've never driven here." But you couldn't afford to practice everything, and it's your simulation, scored by your company. Paying an outside party to test Seattle scenarios costs money and control, and it's the only version of the answer the council can check.

    In class: Check Waymo's data, then build the pitch math

    At the hearing, the council will ask two questions before anything else: how many crashes would your cars prevent here, and how many people who drive for a living would lose work?     Part A: Meet the data (about 7 minutes)

    What it is. Waymo runs a public Safety Impact data hub. Every crash its driverless cars are in has to be reported to federal regulators (NHTSA), no matter who was at fault. Waymo divides those crashes by the miles its cars drove. Then it compares that rate with a human benchmark: how often human drivers crash per mile, from police reports, on the same kinds of streets in the same cities.

    How to read it. Every rate below is crashes per million miles. The gray bar is human drivers. The pink bar is Waymo. The gap between them, times the miles you drive, is the crashes avoided. These are the real numbers, through June 2026.

    1. The same car avoids about 14 injury crashes per 10 million miles in Phoenix and about 60 in San Francisco. Click both cities. What's actually different?

    Open the answer

    Waymo's own rate barely moves (0.56 versus 0.67). The human rate moves a lot: San Francisco drivers crash more than three times as often per mile as Phoenix drivers. So "crashes avoided" depends mostly on which humans you compare against. Seattle isn't in this data, so whoever picks the comparison city picks the answer.

    2. Look at the crash types. Which one improved the least? Why might that be?

    Open the answer

    Front-to-rear crashes: 88 against an expected 169, only about half as many. In a rear-end crash the car in back usually does the hitting, and Waymo's count includes every crash, whoever was at fault. A car that stops when a human driver wouldn't expect it can still get hit from behind.

    3. Which city's numbers should you trust least?

    Open the answer

    Atlanta. It has only about 8.6 million miles, around 3 percent of the total. With few miles, a handful of crashes swings the rate a lot. A serious-injury rate of 0.00 there means none so far, not none forever.

    4. What does Seattle have that none of these five cities do?

    Open the answer

    Rain on far more days of the year, plus snow and ice some winters. Waymo's own FAQ says its cities don't get much snow, so neither the robotaxi numbers nor the human comparison include it. The data can't tell you how the car does on a dark, wet Seattle night in January.

    5. Who counted, and who checks?

    Open the answer

    Waymo counts its own crashes from the reports it must file with NHTSA. The human numbers come from police reports, adjusted by Waymo's researchers; for example, they assume about a third of human injury crashes never get reported to police, and add them back. Waymo posts the raw files so anyone can redo the math, and its methods are in peer-reviewed journals, but the papers are written by Waymo's own researchers. That's real transparency, and it's still the company grading itself.

    This week’s case: Waymo in Seattle

    Waymo has been driving its cars around Seattle and the Eastside since fall 2025, with a trained human behind the wheel, because Washington law doesn’t allow paid driverless rides (KING 5). In the 2026 session, legislators filed one bill that would require a human safety operator in every autonomous vehicle carrying passengers (HB 1984) and another that would open the door to driverless operation statewide (SB 6243). Neither passed. In October 2025, rideshare drivers and Teamsters rallied downtown against Waymo’s plans.

    Both sides can use it. Side A: Waymo reports far fewer crashes than human drivers across hundreds of millions of miles, and Seattle still has a person in every driver’s seat. Side B: those drivers organized, won the strongest pay protections in the country, and showed up to say no.

    To resolve it: a city that says yes trades drivers’ paychecks for fewer crashes, and a city that says no trades fewer crashes for drivers’ paychecks. Neither trade is free. Name the one number that would make you say yes, the one that would make you say no, and who should keep the score.

    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

    Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

    The four toys (sections 1 to 4)
    Self-contained, no network calls, nothing persists. Mini-Perception is a real two-hidden-layer network (6, 4, 3, 4) trained by backpropagation on the student's labels from a fixed starting seed; the scenes and their six clue scores are invented, and the three rare test scenes were built so that a network trained on the ten ordinary ones misreads them. Mini-Camera runs two standard 3 by 3 edge filters over an invented 20 by 24 frame; its shape layer and verdict are simplified for display. Mini-Planner is trial-and-error learning over nine gap thresholds with invented gap, misjudgment, and crash odds; with the chapter's score sheet it settles on a habit that passes the board, the other two presets fail it in different ways, and two runs can differ. The cartoon replays real test turns from the board; its timing is stylized, but each turn's gaps, the car's misjudgment, and the outcome come from the simulation. Mini-World's rarity multipliers are invented and labeled as order of magnitude only; the practice-budget exercise uses invented levels of real experience per weather type and an invented Seattle weather mix, except that Waymo's own FAQ confirms its cities get almost no snow.
