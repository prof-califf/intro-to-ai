The Bellingham Pitch

You're a strategy analyst at a robotaxi company, and your boss wants to launch in Bellingham. Before you can pitch the city council, you have to understand what you're selling and what it costs the town. So today you open the car up. You train a tiny neural network to see the road, watch a filter scan a camera frame, write the reward function that teaches the car when to turn, and prompt a world model to invent a street it has never driven. Then you build the pitch math with Claude, crashes avoided on one side and driver work replaced on the other, decide what to offer the council before it asks, and debate the question Seattle is fighting over right now: should the safety numbers win, or the drivers' jobs?

1

In class: Teach it to see (a neural network on road scenes)

A robotaxi's cameras don't see a pedestrian. They see numbers. A neural network turns those numbers into a label, and it learns how by being shown examples someone already labeled. Today that someone is you. About 12 minutes.

In a real car, the early layers of the network find the clues on their own, straight from the pixels (section 2 shows how). Here they're already found for you, as six clues scored 0 to 1: legs, wheels, tall and upright, moving fast, a big metal body, and flutters like it weighs nothing. Your labels decide what the clues mean.

Mini-Perception · teach a network what's on the road
Your job
  1. Label ten scenes as Pedestrian, Cyclist, Vehicle, or Ignore (things the car can drive through, like a bag or a shadow). After each one, the network retrains on everything you've labeled so far.
  2. Watch it guess first. From the second scene on, the network guesses before you answer. Watch the guesses get better, and watch the lines in the diagram thicken as it settles on what matters.
  3. Press Test it on new scenes. Eight scenes it has never seen arrive. Five are ordinary. Three come from the long tail.
  4. Then fix it the way a company would, with more data. Add four rare scenes the data team labeled, and test again.

Is this real?

The child stepping out from behind a parked SUV is. On January 23, 2026, a Waymo struck a child who ran into the street from behind a double-parked SUV, two blocks from a Santa Monica elementary school at drop-off time. The child had minor injuries. Waymo says its car braked from about 17 mph to under 6 before contact, and that its model shows a fully attentive human would have hit the child at about 14. Federal regulators opened an investigation into how the car behaves around schools (NHTSA, Axios). A partly hidden person is the long tail at its hardest: most of what the camera sees is the SUV.

That's deep learning, small. Labeled examples go in, weights come out, and every new scene gets a label and a confidence. Three things to carry forward.

It learned your labels, not the world. It never knew what a pedestrian is. It learned which clues went with the word you clicked.

It was confidently wrong. Look at the confidence on the three rare scenes before you added data. A network doesn't know what it hasn't seen, so it doesn't know to be unsure. That's why a robotaxi has an operational design domain (section 2.8): the company decides where it's allowed to be confident, because the network can't.

More data shortened the tail, and the tail kept going. Four labeled scenes fixed most of the failures, and the hardest one, a child mostly hidden by an SUV, is still hard, because most of what the camera sees there really is an SUV. Then a person on a horse showed up, and your four labels don't even have a box for it. That's every recall in section 2.6, one at a time.

For the pitch

The first question at the hearing will be some version of "what does your car do with something it has never seen?" The honest answer is "sometimes it's confidently wrong, so we limit where it drives." That answer is an offer with a cost: a smaller service area at launch, and fewer rides to bill.

In section 1 the clues were handed to you. A real car finds its own, straight from the camera's pixels, with a convolutional network: small filters that slide across the whole frame looking for one pattern each. Here's one filter, sliding. About 6 minutes.

Why "convolutional." The filter never changes as it slides. The same nine numbers check every spot in the frame, which is why the network can find a person on the left edge, the right edge, or dead center. Stack layers and the filters build on each other: edges become arms and wheels, those become people and cars. That's the 2012 ImageNet idea from Chapter 1, running many times a second on every camera.

Why the weather mattered. A filter only fires when the match is strong enough. Fog washed out the contrast, so the edges fell under that line and the shapes never formed. Rain added noise, so the filter fired on raindrops too. Nothing about the code changed. The frame did. That's why a coverage map lists weather (section 2.8), and it's why the camera isn't the car's only sensor: lidar and radar see through what blinds a camera, and sensor fusion has to decide whom to believe (section 2.3).

For the pitch

Bellingham rains, and none of the five cities in your company's safety record is this wet. Offering a wet-season test period before full service costs you a season of revenue. Not offering it means your first rainy-night mistake is the headline, and the council will remember you didn't offer.

3

In class: Teach it to decide (reinforcement learning at a left turn)

Seeing isn't driving. Once the car knows what's out there, it still has to decide what to do, and there's no labeled right answer for that. So the planner learns by trial and error: try, get a score, adjust. That's reinforcement learning, and the score sheet is the reward function. You write it. About 12 minutes.

The task is an unprotected left turn: no green arrow, oncoming traffic, and the car has to pick a gap. Go too early and it risks a crash. Wait too long and it blocks traffic, annoys the rider, and ends up in someone's video. The car's whole habit comes down to one number it learns: how big a gap it needs before it goes. And because perception is never perfect, the car misjudges gaps a little, which is exactly what section 1 showed you.

Mini-Planner · write the reward function, then let the car practice 2,000 turns
Your job

  2. Read what it learned, then the safety board. The board tests the habit on 300 new turns. It passes only if at least 95 percent finish, there's at most 1 crash, and the average wait is under 8 seconds.
  3. Try the other two presets. Each fails the board a different way. Why?
  4. Then write your own. Find a score sheet you could defend to the board and to the rider.

A toy model, not Waymo's, which isn't published. Gaps, misjudgments, and crash odds are invented; the learning is real trial and error in your browser, so two runs can settle on slightly different habits. Nothing is saved.

The car didn't learn to drive well. It learned to score well. When the sheet barely punished waiting, the car waited, forever, because nothing told it a rider was getting angry. When the sheet barely punished crashes, it took gaps a person never would. Every time, it did exactly what the numbers said. Nobody voted on those numbers. Someone in a product meeting picked them.

That's why section 2.4 calls the reward function strategy. Early Waymo cars were famously timid, so in 2025 the company tuned them to be more assertive, and the street felt it: lane changes without signaling, and one illegal U-turn in San Bruno with nobody in the driver's seat. Somebody moved a slider like yours.

For the pitch

The council can't read your model, but it could read your score sheet: what the car is rewarded for and how much. Offering to show it to an independent reviewer costs you a piece of strategy competitors would love to see. Refusing costs you trust, and after the San Bruno U-turn, "trust us" is a weak pitch.

4

At home: Let it practice where it's never been (a world model)

Your planner just practiced 2,000 left turns. A real one can't practice by crashing real cars, and the long tail is too rare to wait for. So it practices inside a world model: an AI that has learned how the world looks and moves from huge amounts of video, and can generate a street that never happened. About 6 minutes, and this one is for after class, before you write item 2.

    The rarity multipliers are invented, order of magnitude only; the point is that rare things multiply. The 271 million is Waymo's reported driverless miles through June 2026. Nothing is saved.

    Is this real?

    Yes. In February 2026 Waymo announced the Waymo World Model, built on Google DeepMind's Genie 3, a general world model trained on a very large and varied set of videos. Because Genie 3 learned from video of everything, not just driving, Waymo says it can generate scenes its fleet has never seen, including an elephant in the road, and produce both camera and lidar views of them. Engineers can steer it with plain-language prompts, much like your ingredient rows (Waymo). The announcement didn't include independent tests of how realistic the scenes are.

    Practice can be manufactured; reality can't. Your weirdest scene might take more real miles than the fleet will ever drive, and the world model made it in a second. That's why simulated miles outnumber real ones by a wide margin (section 2.5), and why a competitor can't catch up just by buying the same sensors. But look at the last wildcard. A world model only recombines what's in its video. A simulated pedestrian is the model's idea of a pedestrian, so the practice is honest where the data was and silent where it wasn't.

    For the pitch

    Simulation is your best answer to "you've never driven here." You can stage a Bellingham November before the first car arrives. But it's your simulation, scored by your company. Paying for an outside party to test Bellingham-specific scenarios costs money and control, and it's the only version of the answer the council can check.

    7

    At home: Under the hood, how the four toys make one car

    Put sections 1 to 4 back together and you have section 2.1's loop, small.

    Perception is a neural network. Section 2's filters turn pixels into clues; section 1's layers turn clues into labels with a confidence. It learned from labeled examples, and it's only as good as what it saw. The real one has billions of weights instead of your few dozen, reads lidar and radar as well as cameras, and still misses the costume it never met.

    Planning is reinforcement learning. Your planner never saw a correct answer. It tried, got a score from your sheet, and drifted toward whatever scored well. That's how AlphaGo learned Go in Chapter 1, and it's why the reward function is the most quietly important document in the company.

    Both practice in a world model. Simulation is where the rare scenes get manufactured, for perception to label and for the planner to try. It shortens the tail. It can't end it, because it can only imagine what its video showed it.

    And the human? Out of the loop in the moment (section 2.7). But people chose the labels, the six clues, the reward numbers, the ingredients, and the coverage map. The car's judgment is theirs, frozen into weights and executed many times a second on a street where people live.

    Claude's part. In section 5 Claude wrote a working ledger in a minute. It didn't decide whether to trust Waymo's count, what a Bellingham driver earns, or what belongs in the box at the bottom. You did, and those choices, not the code, are where the argument with the council actually lives.

    Your part. In section 6 you decided what your company would give up to launch. No model made that call. It came down to whose costs you were willing to carry, which is the question the toys can't answer for you.

    This week’s case: Waymo in Seattle

    Waymo has been driving its cars around Seattle and the Eastside since fall 2025, with a trained human behind the wheel, because Washington law doesn’t allow paid driverless rides (KING 5). In the 2026 session, legislators filed one bill that would require a human safety operator in every autonomous vehicle carrying passengers (HB 1984) and another that would open the door to driverless operation statewide (SB 6243). Neither passed. In October 2025, rideshare drivers and Teamsters rallied downtown against Waymo’s plans.

    Both sides can use it. Side A: Waymo reports far fewer crashes than human drivers across hundreds of millions of miles, and Seattle still has a person in every driver’s seat. Side B: those drivers organized, won the strongest pay protections in the country, and showed up to say no.

    6. Spring 2026

      Who feels safe

      A USC survey of Los Angeles County residents found that women feel a lot safer from harassment in a Waymo than in an Uber or Lyft, while men’s answers barely differ. Waymo’s own surveys and reporters’ interviews point the same way: many women riding alone or late choose a car with no stranger in it.

      The catch: the same survey found only 9 percent rated robotaxis safe, fewer than rated ride-hailing safe (12 percent), and far fewer than rated driving themselves safe (27 percent). Feeling safe from a person isn’t the same as feeling safe from a crash.

      LAist. (2026). Waymos in L.A., but how many people are taking them? Open source NBC News. Are taxis safer with no driver? These women think so. Open source

    7. Through June 2026

      Waymo’s own count

      Across 271.3 million driverless miles in five metro areas, Waymo reports 82 percent fewer injury crashes (841 fewer) and 95 percent fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes (55 fewer) than human drivers would have had on the same roads.

      The catch: it’s Waymo’s analysis, in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, and Atlanta. None of them is as wet as Seattle, and the human comparison includes drunk and distracted drivers.

      Waymo. (2026, September 24). From the road. Open source

    Notice the pattern. Every big crash number either comes from Waymo or was released with Waymo. The failures got found because regulators and reporters went looking. Side A leans on the numbers. Side B asks who counted them.

    Who drives for a living: the jobs numbers

    Now the other column. The people who drive are real, many of them have few other options, and in Washington they’ve organized.

    1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
    2. 2021

      Who drives

      In a Pew survey, 5 percent of U.S. adults said they had ever driven for a ride-hailing app, and 2 percent in the past year. Lower-income adults were more likely to do gig work, and 42 percent of lower-income current or recent gig workers said it was their main job.

      The catch: it’s from 2021, and many drivers drive part time.

      Pew Research Center. (2021, December 8). The state of gig work in 2021. Open source

    3. 2022

      Washington’s driver law

      After years of organizing, Washington’s rideshare drivers won a state law, HB 2076, with a guaranteed minimum pay rate per trip, paid sick leave, and workers’ compensation. Its supporters called it the highest statewide pay floor for rideshare drivers in the country.

      The catch: it was a compromise. Drivers stayed independent contractors, not employees, and some labor groups opposed it for that reason.

      KING 5. (2022). Uber, Lyft drivers win minimum pay, sick leave. Open source Washington House Democrats. (2022). HB 2076 press release. Open source

    4. 2024 to 2034

      The federal forecast

      The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts about 447,900 taxi, ride-hailing, shuttle, and chauffeur jobs, and projects them to grow 9 percent over the decade, much faster than average, with about 58,800 openings a year.

      The catch: a projection, not a count, and it lumps ride-hailing in with shuttle and limo work. It doesn’t say how much robotaxis were built into it.

      U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Occupational outlook handbook: Taxi drivers, shuttle drivers, and chauffeurs. Open source

    5. Late 2025

      The paycheck in robotaxi cities

      Gridwise, which tracks drivers’ earnings, found trips per hour fell 5.3 percent in robotaxi cities versus 2.6 percent nationwide, and hourly pay fell in Los Angeles (3.7 percent), San Francisco, and Phoenix while it rose 1.8 percent nationally.

      The catch: in San Francisco, drivers earned 16 percent more per quarter because the platforms cut their own fees, and Gridwise says it can’t separate robotaxis’ effect from everything else in those markets.

      Gridwise Analytics. (2026). 2026 AV impact report. Open source

    6. October 2025 to March 2026

      Seattle’s drivers say no

      Rideshare drivers, Teamsters, and labor advocates rallied in downtown Seattle against Waymo’s plans, citing jobs and safety. The president of the Washington Drivers Union told KIRO 7 there are about 30,000 rideshare drivers in Washington.

      The catch: the 30,000 is the union’s estimate, and it counts everyone who drives, including people who drive a few hours a week.

      KING 5. (2025, October). ‘Hell no, Waymo’: Seattle rideshare drivers, union advocates rally. Open source KIRO 7, via Yahoo News. (2026, March). Driverless car app Waymo moves forward in Seattle as ride-share drivers protest. Open source

    7. July 2026

      A middle path, proposed

      In Washington, D.C., a proposed bill would allow robotaxis but cap the fleet at 200 cars until 2028 and charge operators 15 cents for every mile driven.

      The catch: it hasn’t passed, and unions still oppose it.

      Washington Times. (2026, July 13). D.C. Council weighs future of automated ridesharing vehicles that draw the ire of unions. Open source

    Notice the pattern. The job loss shows up first as fewer trips an hour, not pink slips, because drivers are contractors. The numbers are mixed, and the people are not hypothetical. Side B leans on this record. Side A will point at the federal forecast and the middle path.

    Side A · left half

    Yes: the safety numbers win

    Seattle lets robotaxis carry paying riders with no driver, and lets them grow as fast as riders choose them.

    What you have to prove: that the safety numbers are real enough to be worth the paychecks they cost, even though the company counted most of them, in cities drier than this one.

    Side B · right half

    No: the drivers’ jobs win

    Seattle keeps a person in every driver’s seat. Robotaxis can keep testing, with a safety driver aboard.

    What you have to prove: that protecting those jobs is worth the crashes human drivers will keep causing, and say what Seattle does about those crashes instead.

    Talking points: your group takes one

    Each point has a claim, the real evidence behind it, and the comeback to expect. The tags jump to the full record, with sources.

    Side A: yes, the safety numbers win

    “Human drivers kill people every single day.”

    The evidenceAn estimated 36,640 people died on U.S. roads in 2025, about a hundred a day. In Seattle, 30 were killed and 221 seriously injured, most of the dead pedestrians hit by drivers. In 2023, 30 percent of U.S. traffic deaths involved a drunk driver. The human record

    Expect them to say“Almost none of those crashes involved a taxi. Robotaxis don’t take drunk people out of their own cars.”

    “The numbers say robotaxis crash far less.”

    The evidenceAcross 271.3 million driverless miles, Waymo reports 82 percent fewer injury crashes and 95 percent fewer serious-or-worse crashes than human drivers on the same roads. Swiss Re, an insurer, found 92 percent fewer bodily injury claims. The safety record

    Expect them to say“That’s Waymo’s own count, and the insurer’s study was released with Waymo. And none of those five cities is as wet as Seattle.”

    The evidenceA trauma surgeon who reviewed the crash data called self-driving cars a public health breakthrough. The safety record

    Expect them to say“One doctor reading the company’s data. Federal regulators are investigating after a Waymo hit a child near a school.”

    “No driver means no stranger in the car.”

    The evidenceUber’s own report counted 3,824 reports of serious sexual assault on its U.S. rides in 2019 and 2020. In a 2026 USC survey, women felt a lot safer from harassment in a Waymo than in an Uber or Lyft. The human record The safety record

    Expect them to say“Riders were the accused in 43 percent of those reports, so drivers get hurt too. And in that same survey, fewer people rated robotaxis safe than rated Uber safe.”

    “Driving jobs are growing anyway.”

    The evidenceThe Bureau of Labor Statistics projects taxi, ride-hailing, shuttle, and chauffeur jobs to grow 9 percent from 2024 to 2034, with about 58,800 openings a year. The jobs record

    Expect them to say“That’s a forecast. In cities that already have robotaxis, trips per hour are falling twice as fast as everywhere else.”

    Side B: no, the drivers’ jobs win

    1

    “Thousands of Washington families drive for a living.”

    The evidenceThe Washington Drivers Union says about 30,000 people drive rideshare in the state. Nationally, 42 percent of lower-income gig workers say gig work is their main job. The jobs record

    Expect them to say“That’s the union’s count, and plenty of those people drive a few hours a week. The Pew numbers are from 2021.”

    2

    “Washington drivers fought for these jobs, and won.”

    The evidenceWashington’s 2022 driver law guarantees minimum pay per trip, paid sick leave, and workers’ compensation, the strongest statewide floor in the country. Robotaxis would replace a job the state just made better. The jobs record

    Expect them to say“Then make the robotaxi company pay in too, the way D.C.’s bill would charge 15 cents a mile. Protecting drivers doesn’t require keeping the crashes.”

    3

    “It’s already hitting paychecks.”

    The evidenceIn robotaxi cities, drivers’ trips per hour fell 5.3 percent at the end of 2025, about twice the national drop, and hourly pay fell in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Phoenix while it rose nationally. The jobs record

    Expect them to say“Gridwise says it can’t separate robotaxis from everything else, and San Francisco drivers earned 16 percent more per quarter.”

    4

    “The safety record is kept by the company that profits from it.”

    The evidenceWaymo’s headline numbers are Waymo’s own analysis, and the insurance study was released with Waymo. When Cruise dragged a pedestrian, California’s DMV said it only learned about the dragging from another agency. The safety record

    Expect them to say“Cruise isn’t Waymo, and the regulator pulled Cruise’s permit within weeks. The oversight worked.”

    5

    “People don’t trust it yet, and a driver does more than drive.”

    The evidenceIn a 2026 USC survey, only 9 percent of people rated robotaxis safe from crashes. A San Francisco driver told NBC that he would never have let a rider be harassed the way one Waymo rider was while she sat trapped in the car. The safety record

    Expect them to say“In the same survey, women felt much safer from harassment in a robotaxi. For a lot of riders, the driver is the risk.”

    Prep, 12 minutes
    1. Get your point (1 min). Groups of 2 or 3. Count off 1 to 5 on your side. That’s your talking point.
    2. Check the evidence (3 min). Click the tag on your point to jump to its record, reread the entries behind it, and open at least one source.
    3. Pressure-test it with Claude (5 min). Fill in the brackets and send:
    I'm in a class debate on this question: should Seattle let driverless robotaxis take over its taxi and rideshare work? I'm on Side [A, yes, the safety numbers win / B, no, the drivers' jobs win]. My point is: [paste the claim]. My evidence is: [paste the evidence]. Then argue against me as hard as you can: what's the other side's best answer, and where is my point weakest? Don't go easy on me.

    If Claude cites a study, check it against the records above. Then close it. You can’t read from Claude during the debate.

    1. Write your notes (3 min), in your own words: your point in one sentence, the evidence, the comeback you expect, and your answer to it.

    No AI? Skip step 3. Instead, read two entries in the other side’s record and write the strongest thing they’ll say to you.

    Rounds, 25 minutes
    1. Team up (3 min). Join the other groups on your side. Pick your best two points, an opener, and a rebutter.
    2. Openings (6 min). Side A, then Side B. About 45 seconds each: your claim, your evidence, and its source.
    3. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    4. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new points.
    5. Switch (4 min). What was the other side’s best point? Someone new says it in one sentence.
    6. What would change your mind? (4 min). Name the specific evidence you’d have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

    To resolve it: a city that says yes trades drivers’ paychecks for fewer crashes, and a city that says no trades fewer crashes for drivers’ paychecks. Neither trade is free. Name the one number that would make you say yes, the one that would make you say no, and who should keep the score.

    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

    Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

    The four toys (sections 1 to 4)
    Self-contained, no network calls, nothing persists. Mini-Perception is a real two-hidden-layer network (6, 4, 3, 4) trained by backpropagation on the student's labels from a fixed starting seed; the scenes and their six clue scores are invented, and the three rare test scenes were built so that a network trained on the ten ordinary ones misreads them. Mini-Camera runs two standard 3 by 3 edge filters over an invented 20 by 24 frame; its shape layer and verdict are simplified for display. Mini-Planner is trial-and-error learning over nine gap thresholds with invented gap, misjudgment, and crash odds; with the chapter's score sheet it settles on a habit that passes the board, the other two presets fail it in different ways, and two runs can differ. Mini-World's rarity multipliers are invented and labeled as order of magnitude only.
