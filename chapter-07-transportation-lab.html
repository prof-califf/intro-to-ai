The Bellingham Pitch

You're a strategy analyst at a robotaxi company, and your boss wants to launch in Bellingham. Before you can pitch the city council, you have to understand what you're selling and what it costs the town. So today you open the car up. You train a tiny neural network to see the road, watch a filter scan a camera frame, write the reward function that teaches the car when to turn, and prompt a world model to invent a street it has never driven. Then you build the pitch math with Claude, crashes avoided on one side and driver work replaced on the other, decide what to offer the council before it asks, and debate the question underneath the whole chapter: a public health breakthrough, or a job killer?

Your role. You work for the company, but you can't win this permit by only seeing the company's side. The council will weigh riders against drivers, crashes against paychecks. So will you, because the pitch that ignores the other column is the one that loses the vote.

1

In class: Teach it to see (a neural network on road scenes)

A robotaxi's cameras don't see a pedestrian. They see numbers. A neural network turns those numbers into a label, and it learns how by being shown examples someone already labeled. Today that someone is you. About 12 minutes.

In a real car, the early layers of the network find the clues on their own, straight from the pixels (section 2 shows how). Here they're already found for you, as six clues scored 0 to 1: legs, wheels, tall and upright, moving fast, a big metal body, and flutters like it weighs nothing. Your labels decide what the clues mean.

Mini-Perception · teach a network what's on the road
Your job
  1. Label ten scenes as Pedestrian, Cyclist, Vehicle, or Ignore (things the car can drive through, like a bag or a shadow). After each one, the network retrains on everything you've labeled so far.
  2. Watch it guess first. From the second scene on, the network guesses before you answer. Watch the guesses get better, and watch the lines in the diagram thicken as it settles on what matters.
  3. Press Test it on new scenes. Eight scenes it has never seen arrive. Five are ordinary. Three come from the long tail.
  4. Then fix it the way a company would, with more data. Add four rare scenes the data team labeled, and test again.

Is this real?

The child stepping out from behind a parked SUV is. On January 23, 2026, a Waymo struck a child who ran into the street from behind a double-parked SUV, two blocks from a Santa Monica elementary school at drop-off time. The child had minor injuries. Waymo says its car braked from about 17 mph to under 6 before contact, and that its model shows a fully attentive human would have hit the child at about 14. Federal regulators opened an investigation into how the car behaves around schools (NHTSA, Axios). A partly hidden person is the long tail at its hardest: most of what the camera sees is the SUV.

That's deep learning, small. Labeled examples go in, weights come out, and every new scene gets a label and a confidence. Three things to carry forward.

It learned your labels, not the world. It never knew what a pedestrian is. It learned which clues went with the word you clicked.

It was confidently wrong. Look at the confidence on the three rare scenes before you added data. A network doesn't know what it hasn't seen, so it doesn't know to be unsure. That's why a robotaxi has an operational design domain (section 2.7): the company decides where it's allowed to be confident, because the network can't.

More data shortened the tail, and the tail kept going. Four labeled scenes fixed most of the failures, and the hardest one, a child mostly hidden by an SUV, is still hard, because most of what the camera sees there really is an SUV. Then a person on a horse showed up, and your four labels don't even have a box for it. That's every recall in section 2.8, one at a time.

For the pitch

The first question at the hearing will be some version of "what does your car do with something it has never seen?" The honest answer is "sometimes it's confidently wrong, so we limit where it drives." That answer is an offer with a cost: a smaller service area at launch, and fewer rides to bill.

Why "convolutional." The filter never changes as it slides. The same nine numbers check every spot in the frame, which is why the network can find a person on the left edge, the right edge, or dead center. Stack layers and the filters build on each other: edges become arms and wheels, those become people and cars. That's the 2012 ImageNet idea from Chapter 1, running many times a second on every camera.

Why the weather mattered. A filter only fires when the match is strong enough. Fog washed out the contrast, so the edges fell under that line and the shapes never formed. Rain added noise, so the filter fired on raindrops too. Nothing about the code changed. The frame did. That's why a coverage map lists weather (section 2.7), and it's why the camera isn't the car's only sensor: lidar and radar see through what blinds a camera, and sensor fusion has to decide whom to believe (section 2.2).

For the pitch

Bellingham rains, and none of the five cities in your company's safety record is this wet. Offering a wet-season test period before full service costs you a season of revenue. Not offering it means your first rainy-night mistake is the headline, and the council will remember you didn't offer.

3

In class: Teach it to decide (reinforcement learning at a left turn)

Seeing isn't driving. Once the car knows what's out there, it still has to decide what to do, and there's no labeled right answer for that. So the planner learns by trial and error: try, get a score, adjust. That's reinforcement learning, and the score sheet is the reward function. You write it. About 12 minutes.

The task is an unprotected left turn: no green arrow, oncoming traffic, and the car has to pick a gap. Go too early and it risks a crash. Wait too long and it blocks traffic, annoys the rider, and ends up in someone's video. The car's whole habit comes down to one number it learns: how big a gap it needs before it goes. And because perception is never perfect, the car misjudges gaps a little, which is exactly what section 1 showed you.

Mini-Planner · write the reward function, then let the car practice 2,000 turns
Your job

  2. Read what it learned, then the safety board. The board tests the habit on 300 new turns. It passes only if at least 95 percent finish, there's at most 1 crash, and the average wait is under 8 seconds.
  3. Try the other two presets. Each fails the board a different way. Why?
  4. Then write your own. Find a score sheet you could defend to the board and to the rider.

A toy model, not Waymo's, which isn't published. Gaps, misjudgments, and crash odds are invented; the learning is real trial and error in your browser, so two runs can settle on slightly different habits. Nothing is saved.

The car didn't learn to drive well. It learned to score well. When the sheet barely punished waiting, the car waited, forever, because nothing told it a rider was getting angry. When the sheet barely punished crashes, it took gaps a person never would. Every time, it did exactly what the numbers said. Nobody voted on those numbers. Someone in a product meeting picked them.

That's why section 2.3 calls the reward function strategy. Early Waymo cars were famously timid, so in 2025 the company tuned them to be more assertive, and the street felt it: lane changes without signaling, and one illegal U-turn in San Bruno with nobody in the driver's seat. Somebody moved a slider like yours.

For the pitch

The council can't read your model, but it could read your score sheet: what the car is rewarded for and how much. Offering to show it to an independent reviewer costs you a piece of strategy competitors would love to see. Refusing costs you trust, and after the San Bruno U-turn, "trust us" is a weak pitch.

4

At home: Let it practice where it's never been (a world model)

Your planner just practiced 2,000 left turns. A real one can't practice by crashing real cars, and the long tail is too rare to wait for. So it practices inside a world model: an AI that has learned how the world looks and moves from huge amounts of video, and can generate a street that never happened. About 6 minutes, and this one is for after class, before you write item 2.

    The rarity multipliers are invented, order of magnitude only; the point is that rare things multiply. The 271 million is Waymo's reported driverless miles through June 2026. Nothing is saved.

    Is this real?

    Yes. In February 2026 Waymo announced the Waymo World Model, built on Google DeepMind's Genie 3, a general world model trained on a very large and varied set of videos. Because Genie 3 learned from video of everything, not just driving, Waymo says it can generate scenes its fleet has never seen, including an elephant in the road, and produce both camera and lidar views of them. Engineers can steer it with plain-language prompts, much like your ingredient rows (Waymo). The announcement didn't include independent tests of how realistic the scenes are.

    Practice can be manufactured; reality can't. Your weirdest scene might take more real miles than the fleet will ever drive, and the world model made it in a second. That's why simulated miles outnumber real ones by a wide margin (section 2.3), and why a competitor can't catch up just by buying the same sensors. But look at the last wildcard. A world model only recombines what's in its video. A simulated pedestrian is the model's idea of a pedestrian, so the practice is honest where the data was and silent where it wasn't.

    For the pitch

    Simulation is your best answer to "you've never driven here." You can stage a Bellingham November before the first car arrives. But it's your simulation, scored by your company. Paying for an outside party to test Bellingham-specific scenarios costs money and control, and it's the only version of the answer the council can check.

    7

    At home: Under the hood, how the four toys make one car

    Put sections 1 to 4 back together and you have section 2.1's loop, small.

    Perception is a neural network. Section 2's filters turn pixels into clues; section 1's layers turn clues into labels with a confidence. It learned from labeled examples, and it's only as good as what it saw. The real one has billions of weights instead of your few dozen, reads lidar and radar as well as cameras, and still misses the costume it never met.

    Planning is reinforcement learning. Your planner never saw a correct answer. It tried, got a score from your sheet, and drifted toward whatever scored well. That's how AlphaGo learned Go in Chapter 1, and it's why the reward function is the most quietly important document in the company.

    Both practice in a world model. Simulation is where the rare scenes get manufactured, for perception to label and for the planner to try. It shortens the tail. It can't end it, because it can only imagine what its video showed it.

    And the human? Out of the loop in the moment (section 2.4). But people chose the labels, the six clues, the reward numbers, the ingredients, and the coverage map. The car's judgment is theirs, frozen into weights and executed many times a second on a street where people live.

    Claude's part. In section 5 Claude wrote a working ledger in a minute. It didn't decide whether to trust Waymo's count, what a Bellingham driver earns, or what belongs in the box at the bottom. You did, and those choices, not the code, are where the argument with the council actually lives.

    Your part. In section 6 you decided what your company would give up to launch. No model made that call. It came down to whose costs you were willing to carry, which is the question the toys can't answer for you.

    8

    In class: Debate

    The question: Robotaxis, a public health breakthrough or a job killer? It's 2031. Washington has created a path for paid driverless rides, and a robotaxi company has applied to run in Bellingham. Left half of the room is Side A: a public health breakthrough, so the city should say yes. Right half is Side B: a job killer, so the city should say no. In groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4: that number is your piece of evidence below. The Gilman Street rules apply.

    Chapter 7 put it in one line: the crashes it prevents are invisible and statistical, and the jobs it ends have names and addresses (section 4.5). Your ledger from section 5 is that sentence with numbers in it. Today you argue about which column should win, and you'll be handed a side you might not pick. Leave your company badge at the door: in the debate, you speak for Bellingham.

    Before you argue about the robot, remember the status quo. Today, every ride in Bellingham has a person driving it. Those drivers earn a living, and those drivers also crash: tens of thousands of Americans die on the road every year, almost all of them in crashes involving human drivers. So the real choice in 2031 isn't between robotaxis and a safe, fully employed city. It's between two imperfect systems, and the question is who pays for each one's mistakes.

    Side A · left half of the room

    A public health breakthrough: say yes

    Robotaxis prevent crashes, and crashes kill and injure people every day. A city that says no is choosing crashes it will never see, count, or explain. Start from your ledger's crash column, section 4.1's trauma surgeon, and the riders in section 4.2 who can't drive or don't feel safe with a stranger.

    The cost to name: real people lose income, and the safety numbers you're leaning on are mostly the company's own, from sunnier cities than this one.

    Side B · right half of the room

    A job killer: say no

    Robotaxis move money from local drivers to a company somewhere else, and the people who pay are the ones with the fewest other options. Start from your ledger's driver column, section 4.3's drivers, and the fact that the safety record is kept by the company that profits from it.

    The cost to name: the crashes human drivers will keep causing, which is a real number too, and the riders who keep waiting for a ride they can take alone.

    Nobody in this debate is arguing about a real driver or a real crash victim. You're arguing about a rule, the way a council member would.

    The evidence below is from today. Use it to argue about 2031. Side A: you have to show the crash benefit is real enough to outweigh the drivers' cost, and say who should pay that cost. Side B: you have to show the job loss is real and large, and say what your city does about the crashes instead.

    What 2031 could look like: four scenarios

    Each scenario starts from how it works today, then imagines Bellingham in 2031. Every benefit and issue is grounded in something that has already happened or been measured. The first two are Side A's world; the last two are Side B's. Use them in your prep, and steal from the other side's.

    Open streets

    Side A's world: yes, citywide

    Today: Waymo gives about 500,000 paid rides a week across 10 U.S. cities (TechCrunch, March 2026). In Washington it has tested in Seattle and Bellevue, but Seattle's transportation department says state law has to change before anyone can sell a driverless ride here (KNKX). In 2031: robotaxis run anywhere in Bellingham, day and night.

    Benefits

    • The crash column from your ledger, every year, if the numbers hold here.
    • A ride for people who can't drive, at the hour they choose. In section 4.2 a legally blind rider described being alone in a car for the first time in her life.

    Issues

    • The driver column from your ledger, every year, landing on people who chose driving because it fit their lives.
    • The safety record comes from five sunnier metro areas. Bellingham's rain and dark winter afternoons are exactly where section 2's filters struggled.

    Yes, with a bill attached

    Side A's world: yes, with conditions

    Today: cities that allow robotaxis mostly set no condition for the drivers they replace. Chapter 7 floats one: a per-ride fee that funds retraining (section 4.5). In 2031: Bellingham says yes, charges a fee on every robotaxi ride for a driver transition fund, and slows the cars down around schools.

    Benefits

    • The company that captures the drivers' income pays part of their cost. That's value capture (section 4.5) handled on purpose.
    • School zones get extra margin, the place regulators are already watching: federal investigators opened a probe in January 2026 after a Waymo struck a child near a school.

    Issues

    • Every dollar of fee is a reason to launch in the next city instead, where the drivers get nothing (section 4.5).
    • Retraining money doesn't hand a 55-year-old driver a new career, and nobody has shown what the fund should buy.

    Keep the wheel

    Side B's world: no driverless rides

    Today: this is Washington now. No paid driverless rides, and a state House committee still studying how to allow them (work session, March 2026). In 2031: still a person in every driver's seat in Bellingham.

    Benefits

    • Driver income stays local, and nobody's job is the test case.
    • No fleet of cars recording the street around the clock, and no company running its experiment on roads where nobody consented (section 3, Question 4).

    Issues

    • The crashes stay at the human rate. An estimated 36,640 people died on U.S. roads in 2025 (NHTSA). "Wait and see" isn't free (section 4.1).
    • The riders who can't drive, or don't want a stranger at 1 a.m., keep waiting.

    The slow lane

    Side B's world: not yet, and not on the company's count

    Today: the main safety record is Waymo's analysis of its own crashes. In 2031: Bellingham allows a small, capped pilot, and the cap only rises when an independent party, not the company, shows the crash numbers hold on Bellingham's streets in Bellingham's weather. Drivers get years of notice.

    Benefits

    • The city, not the company, keeps the track record. That's section 2.9's power question, answered.
    • Drivers see it coming and can plan, instead of finding out from their earnings app.

    Issues

    • Every slow year is a year of crashes the cars might have prevented.
    • A small market with strict rules may never get a pilot at all. Companies go where the rules are easiest.

    Evidence 3 · The paycheck

    In one line: in robotaxi cities, drivers are getting fewer trips an hour, and the pay picture is mixed.

    5.3%drop in trips per hour in robotaxi cities, vs. 2.6% nationwide
    −3.7%change in drivers' hourly pay in Los Angeles over a year, while it rose 1.8% nationwide
    +16%San Francisco drivers' quarterly earnings, as the platforms cut their fees
    • The squeeze: Gridwise, which tracks rideshare drivers' earnings, found trips per hour fell almost twice as fast in robotaxi cities as nationwide at the end of 2025, and hourly pay fell in Los Angeles, San Francisco, and Phoenix while it rose nationally (Gridwise).
    • The twist: in San Francisco, riders paid the same, the platforms took a much smaller share of each fare, and drivers earned more per quarter. One way to read it: the platforms are giving up their own margin to keep drivers on the road while robotaxis compete. That can't last forever, and nobody knows how long it lasts.
    • The catch: Gridwise says its comparison can't separate the robotaxis' effect from everything else going on in those markets. Displacement shows up first as fewer trips an hour, not pink slips, because drivers are contractors.

    Push your thinking with Claude

    In section 5 you checked Claude's numbers. Here, turn it around: tell Claude to argue against you. It's one of the fastest ways out of your own bubble, because it will find the other side's best point before they do. If it cites a study you plan to use, look it up and check that it says what Claude says it says.

    1. Send the sample prompt under your evidence card, with your side filled in.
    2. Then make it argue against you:
    Argue against me
    Now argue against me as hard as you can. What's the best point the other side will make, and where is my case weakest? Don't go easy on me.

    If it goes soft or starts agreeing with you, push back: "You're being too nice. Argue the other side for real." Then close Claude and write your notes in your own words. You can't read from Claude during the debate.

    How the debate runs

    Prep, 15 minutes.

    1. Get your side (2 min). Left half Side A, right half Side B. Groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4.
    2. Research with Claude (10 min). Your card, the two prompts, then your notes in your own words.
    3. Team up (3 min). Join one or two other groups on your side, so your team is 6 or 7 people: three teams per side. Pick your single best point, an opener, and a rebutter.

    Rounds, 22 minutes.

    1. Openings (6 min). A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3. About 45 seconds each: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your plan.
    2. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    3. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first this time. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
    4. Switch (4 min). Thirty seconds with your team: what was the other side's best point? Then someone new argues it in one sentence.
    5. What would change your mind? (4 min). Out loud. Name the specific thing you would have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

    To resolve it: a city that says yes is trading drivers' income for fewer crashes, and a city that says no is trading fewer crashes for drivers' income. Neither trade is free. Back in your analyst's seat: which condition in your pitch survives this debate, and what did the debate show you'd have to add?

    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

    Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
