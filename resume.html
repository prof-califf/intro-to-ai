Turning the labs into a resume and a portfolio

Starting with Lab 4, you've been building real things with AI. This page shows you how to put them on a resume and a portfolio site.

The formula · each lab as a resume line · the skills section · a portfolio site, built with AI

Links print with full web addresses.
1

The formula

The data analyst Karina Samsonova, who writes a newsletter on data careers and has reviewed hundreds of CVs for hiring, gives one piece of advice to students with no job experience yet: you've done things, you just haven't written them down properly. A class project is experience. The mistake is burying it in one line that says "group project for MIS class." Her formula for writing up any project has four parts:

What you did → what you worked with → what you found → what it means for the business

"Used ChatGPT for a class assignment" is true and weak. "Built an AI-powered resume scanner that scores a resume against a job posting, ran my own resume through it, and kept only the suggestions that were true" is the same work, written so somebody would want to ask you about it. Her whole guide is in her video for fresh graduates.

Three more of her rules that apply to you now:

  • Don't call yourself an "aspiring" anything. You're a student who has already done the work.
  • Take "teamwork" and "time management" out of your skills section. Everybody claims them. Use the space for what you can actually do.
  • Put the work somewhere a stranger can see it: a personal site, a GitHub page, or a folder of PDFs you can link to.
2

Lead with the judgment, not the tool

Almost every applicant can say they use ChatGPT. Saying it tells a recruiter nothing, and in five years the tools you used this quarter will have new names. What lasts is the judgment this course kept asking for: what you tested, what you caught, what you decided a person should stay in charge of, and what you'd keep, fix, or drop. That's what an employer is actually worried about when they hire someone to work with AI.

So lead with what you did and found. Name the tool last, in passing. Labs 1 to 3 taught the ideas the rest of the course runs on. The pieces worth showing start at Lab 4, when you started building things with Claude. Below is each one written that way. Some labs used fictional data, like the job postings and Jordan's resume in Lab 9, so say so: "simulated job postings" is honest, and nobody will hold it against you. Use these as a starting point, not text to paste. Rewrite each in your own words, put in your own results, and cut anything you didn't actually do. A line copied from a course page reads like one.

Lab 4 · Inside the Graph

Built a chatbot app on a nonprofit's real drug-research data (Every Cure), set up to answer only from that data and to say when the data can't answer, then checked its answers against the source. Found [what it got right and where it overreached].

Lab 5 · The Footprint Machine

Built an interactive calculator comparing the energy use of AI with everyday tech, using only published estimates I could cite, and checked every number the AI put in against its source. Found [your finding].

Lab 6 · You Are the Auditor

Built an AI-powered resume scanner that scores a resume against a job posting and suggests rewrites, ran my own resume through it, and kept only the suggestions that were true. Also ran a controlled bias test of an AI screener with two resumes that differed in one detail. Found [your finding].

Lab 7 · You Are the City Council

Built an interactive page that weighs a robotaxi company's published safety numbers against the paid driving work it would replace in Seattle, with sliders for every assumption, to support a city permit decision. Recommended [your decision].

Lab 8 · The Red Lines

Wrote a rule for which military AI systems should be banned, then built a "treaty tester" that applies my rule to six kinds of weapons and flags the cases it can't decide. Found [where your rule broke].

Lab 9 · The Job Scout

Built a working AI agent in n8n that reads job postings, decides on its own when to check a suspicious employer, ranks the matches, and tailors a resume. Then audited its output for invented qualifications and kept a person as the final approval before anything is sent. Found [what it got right and what it invented]. (Simulated job postings.)

Lab 10 & 11 · Govern the Agent

Wrote six governance rules for the agent I built: what data it can see, what it may do alone, who approves its work, what gets logged, who answers for mistakes, and which rules are built into the design. Found and fixed a data-exposure problem by changing the tool, not the instructions.

Prototype project

With a team of four, designed an AI system for [the problem]: specified its data, model, output, feedback loop, and human oversight, tested the design against five critical questions, decided what to keep, fix, and drop with the cost of each, and pitched it to the class. This is usually your strongest piece.

3

The skills section

This is the one place on a resume where you do name tools, plainly, because most resumes are read by software before a person sees them, and that software looks for words. You need both: the bullets for the person, the skills line for the scanner.

Give the skills section three parts. First, the judgment skills, because those last: checking AI output for errors and invented facts; testing an AI system with a controlled comparison; deciding where a person approves the work; estimating a cost honestly, with a range; writing the rules an AI system runs under. Second, the ideas, named plainly: predictive, generative, and agentic AI; human in the loop; minimum necessary access. Third, the tools by name, each with one clause about what you did: "Claude (built interactive tools and a chatbot app, and checked their numbers); n8n (built an AI agent with three tools and a review step)." The scanner finds the names. The person reads the clause.

4

Build a portfolio, not just a resume

A resume tells someone what you did. A portfolio shows them. When a recruiter can see the agent you built, read the rules you wrote for it, and see the problem you started from, a line on a page becomes work they can judge for themselves. The interview starts from what you made instead of what you claim. Most business students apply with a resume alone, which is the best reason to build one.

One site for all of college, starting with MIS 201

Build the portfolio once and keep adding to it. The site has a short header about you, then one card for each class that produced work worth showing, and inside each class card, a smaller card for each project. MIS 201 can be your first class card. Next quarter, when another class gives you a project you're proud of, add its card. By graduation the site shows four years of work, and how it got better.

Write every project card as a short case study, in the order of the formula: the question, what you worked with, what you did, and what you found. Then add two things a resume has no room for: how you checked the AI's work, and what you'd do next if the project kept going. Those two lines show judgment, which is what an interviewer is trying to see.

Build it with AI, in an afternoon

You don't need to know how to build a website, and you don't need to pay for anything. The free version of Claude can do all of it. Use it the way this course taught: let the AI do the parts you haven't learned yet, and keep every claim on the page yours.

  1. Gather your work. Put your best pieces in one folder: your Prototype paper and slides as PDFs, screenshots of the tools you built in Labs 4 to 8, your Lab 9 screenshots, your Lab 10 & 11 rules, your writeups. Write two or three sentences of notes for each: the question, what you found, and what you'd do next. For your Prototype project, the prompt below lists exactly what to fill in.
  2. Fill in the prompt below, then paste it into Claude or ChatGPT. Replace every bracketed part with your own details. Leave an optional line blank if it doesn't apply. The AI will build the page and ask you for anything it needs.
  3. Make it yours by talking it through. The first version is a starting point. Keep the conversation going and say what you want: "make it feel more professional," "the cards look crowded on my phone," "add a small photo of me in the header." When you're stuck, ask: "what would make this stand out to a recruiter in finance?" or "give me three color schemes to choose from." Let it suggest, then decide for yourself.
  4. Check every word. Read the page as a recruiter would. Change anything that isn't in your voice, and delete anything you didn't actually do. You've spent a quarter catching AI inventing things; this is one more place to look.
  5. Look at it. Save the file as index.html and open it in your browser. Ask the AI to fix anything that looks wrong on a laptop or a phone.
  6. Publish it. Put it on GitHub Pages, which is free. The AI will walk you through it, or use the guide below the prompt. You'll get a web address you can share.
  7. Link it everywhere. Put the address at the top of your resume and in the Featured section of your LinkedIn profile.
  8. Add a card every quarter. When a class gives you work worth showing, ask the AI to add a new class card.
The prompt

Copy this into a document first, replace everything in [BRACKETS] with your own details, then paste the whole thing into Claude or ChatGPT.

I am a business student at Western Washington University. Help me build a portfolio website of the projects I have done in college, as a single HTML file that I can host for free on GitHub Pages.

About me (use exactly what I write here):
- Name: [YOUR NAME]
- Major and concentration: [FOR EXAMPLE: BUSINESS ADMINISTRATION, MIS CONCENTRATION]
- Expected graduation: [QUARTER AND YEAR]
- One sentence about the problems I like to solve: [YOUR SENTENCE]
- Email: [YOUR EMAIL]
- LinkedIn: [YOUR LINKEDIN PROFILE LINK]
- Resume PDF: [LINK TO YOUR RESUME, OR WRITE "LATER"]
- GitHub (optional): [LINK, OR LEAVE BLANK]
- Accent color (optional): [A COLOR YOU LIKE, OR LEAVE BLANK]

How it should be organized:
- A top bar with my name on the left and three links on the right: Projects, About, Contact.
- A header with my name, a one-line headline (my major and the kind of work I want), my expected graduation, my one sentence, and two buttons: "View my resume" and "Contact me." Then small text links to LinkedIn and anything else I gave above. Leave out any link I left blank.
- A short "Skills" row under the header: the skills I list, as small tags, so a recruiter can scan them in five seconds.
- A "Projects" section with one group for each class that produced work worth showing. Each class card shows the course number and name, the quarter I took it, and one sentence about what the class was about.
- Inside each class group, one card for each project. Each project card has a short title, a few skill tags, and one bolded line with the result. Underneath, it says: the question or problem, what I did, what I found or built, how I checked it, and what I would do next. Each project card links to the work itself (a PDF, a screenshot, or a GitHub repository).
- A short "About" section (three or four sentences in my voice) and a "Contact" section at the bottom with my email and LinkedIn.
- Start with one class: MIS 201, Intro to AI in Business. I will add other classes later, so make adding a class group easy, and show me exactly which block of HTML to copy when I do.

How it should look:
- It should look like a polished professional site that would impress a recruiter at a consulting firm or a tech company: calm, confident, and clean. Think of a well-designed company website, not a student assignment.
- Mostly white and off-white, with near-black text and generous white space. Use one accent color, sparingly, for links, buttons, tags, and thin lines. Use my accent color, or a deep navy if I left it blank. No big blocks of bright color.
- Content centered in a column about 1,000 pixels wide, so lines stay easy to read on a big screen.
- Two fonts from Google Fonts: a refined serif for my name and section headings (like Source Serif 4) and a clean sans-serif for everything else (like Inter). Clear sizes: a large name, medium headings, comfortable body text.
- Project cards in a grid: two or three per row on a laptop, one on a phone. White cards with a thin light-gray border, soft rounded corners, and a subtle shadow that grows slightly when you hover.
- Class groups separated by a small uppercase label (like "MIS 201 · INTRO TO AI IN BUSINESS · FALL 2026") and a thin line, not by colored boxes.
- Small touches that make it feel finished: a favicon with my initials, smooth scrolling to each section, a simple footer with my name and the year, and a layout that still looks good when printed.

Rules:
- Do not invent anything about me or my work. Before you write any code, look at what I filled in and ask me for anything that is missing, a few questions at a time. The finished page must never show brackets or placeholder text; if I leave something out, leave that part out of the page.
- Some of my MIS 201 labs used fictional data, like the job postings and the student resume in the agent lab. Describe those as "simulated" or "fictional."
- For any project where I used AI, add one line saying what the AI did and what I checked or decided myself.
- Keep it simple: plain HTML and CSS in one file, no frameworks. It must be easy to read on a phone and work with a screen reader: headings in order, alt text on every image, and strong color contrast.
- When the page is finished, walk me step by step through publishing it free on GitHub Pages, assuming I have never used GitHub.

Optional: step-by-step guide to publishing on GitHub Pages (about 15 minutes, no coding)

GitHub is a free website where people store files, and GitHub Pages turns those files into a public website. You only need a web browser.

  1. Make a free account. Go to github.com and sign up. Pick your username carefully, because it becomes part of your web address. Something close to your real name, like janedoe, looks best to a recruiter.
  2. Make a new repository. A repository is just a folder for your website. Click the + in the top right corner, then New repository.
  3. Name it exactly right. Type your username followed by .github.io. If your username is janedoe, type janedoe.github.io. Set it to Public, then click Create repository.
  4. Upload your files. Click uploading an existing file, or Add file, then Upload files. Drag in your index.html and any PDFs and images it links to. The website file must be named index.html. Then click the green Commit changes button, which just means save.
  5. Turn on the website. Click Settings, then Pages in the menu on the left. Under Source, choose Deploy from a branch. Under Branch, choose main, leave the folder as / (root), and click Save. If it's already set this way, you don't need to change anything.
  6. Wait a few minutes, then visit your site. Go to https://janedoe.github.io, using your own username. It can take up to ten minutes to appear the first time.
  7. To update it later, go back to the repository, click Add file, then Upload files, and drag in the new version of index.html. It replaces the old one, and your site updates within a few minutes.

If something looks different or goes wrong: GitHub moves its buttons around from time to time. Take a screenshot, paste it into your AI conversation, and ask, "Where do I find this now?" If your site shows a 404 page, the two usual causes are a repository name that doesn't match your username exactly, or a website file that isn't named index.html.

Then add one project that's only yours. The labs show you can work from a brief. One project you chose yourself shows you can find a question. Pick a small problem you actually have, from your job, a club, or your family, and take it through what this course taught: name the system, decide where the human sits, test it, and say what you'd keep, fix, or drop. Your Prototype project is already a version of this.

Four rules for anything you post

Your own words. The descriptions on the site are yours, for the same reason the resume lines are. Say what was simulated, and what AI did. Label fictional data as fictional, and say where AI helped and what you checked. Show your work, not an answer key. Post your findings, screenshots, and rules, but not a step-by-step solution to the lab, so the next class still has to do its own thinking. Nothing private. Never post your own contact details inside a screenshot, anyone else's personal data, or anything from an employer without permission. Lab 10 & 11 called this minimum necessary access; it applies to your website too.
