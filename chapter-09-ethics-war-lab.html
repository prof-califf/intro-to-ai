The Red Lines

You lead the policy team at a frontier AI company. Eight government requests are on your desk. For each: accept, accept with conditions, or decline. This is the actual format of the decisions in this week's chapter — and the actual discovery: everyone has values. The line is where they get expensive.

Eight requests · three answers each · one line, located

1

Before you start

Two ground rules. First, no abstaining: "it depends" must become a condition you can write down. Second, track what each answer costs — declines cost revenue and government relationships; accepts cost talent (some of your best people have said they'll quit over certain work) and, depending on your view, more. There is no score to win. There is a line to find, and it's yours.

No Wednesday session this week, so this lab runs take-home: do the sort before Monday if you can — the Monday discussion is better when the room has already felt where its lines disagree.

3

Pressure-test the line

a

Take your most confident decline and write the request's strongest rebuttal — the two sentences the government negotiator would actually say back. Then get a second opinion from a live AI: “You're the government's negotiator. I'm an AI company declining this request: [paste the card]. Give me your strongest two-sentence rebuttal.” Does your decline survive both — and which rebuttal was stronger, yours or the machine's?

b

Take your most confident accept and apply the dual-use ratchet from the chapter: name the follow-on request it makes harder to refuse next year. If you can't name one, look harder — that's what the ratchet means.

c

For every "with conditions": apply the governance-theater test to your own condition. Enforcement mechanism, verification, consequence for violation — which does your condition actually have? A condition with none is an accept wearing a costume.

4

Run the four questions

1

What kind of AI is this? Across your eight answers, did the kind of AI ever drive your decision — or was it always the use, the user, and the stakes? What does that tell you about where the ethics actually live?

2

Where is the human? Rank the eight requests by how meaningful the human control is in each. Did your accepts cluster at one end? Was that deliberate?

3

Who is accountable when it fails? Pick one accept. A failure occurs downstream — trace the chain from the incident back to your desk. Write the sentence you'd say when it reaches you.

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Which of your accepted requests has the least visible failure mode? Did that factor into your answer when you gave it — honestly?

5

The writeup

Due Friday afternoon · post to Canvas
  1. The sort. Your eight answers (screenshot), plus the profile reading.
  2. The line. In one sentence, the principle that separates your accepts from your declines. It must actually fit your answers — if it doesn't, revise the sentence or the answers, and tell us which you revised.
  3. The pressure test. Your step 3 results: the rebuttal that came closest to moving you, the ratchet you identified, and whether any of your conditions failed the theater test.
  4. The four questions. Your answers, including the accountability sentence.
  5. Your move. Chapter 2 asked what's yours that no dial can turn up; this week asks what's yours that no contract can buy. One sentence: a line you'd hold in your own career even at real cost — specific enough that a future employer could violate it. That's the commitment card.
