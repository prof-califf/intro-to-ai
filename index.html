About this book

Most conversations about AI start with "is it good or bad?" This book starts somewhere more useful: which AI system, built by whom, for what, and what happens when it fails. Every chapter takes one real system at one real moment and asks those questions. Every lab has you do the work yourself, often with a live AI on the other side.

📰
Real cases, current ones

A drug-hunter, a résumé screener, a robotaxi, a chatbot in a courtroom, the AI agents that escaped a safety test this summer. Every chapter carries the date it was checked.

🧪
Labs where you build and break things

Train a tiny neural network on your own drawings. Teach a matchbox machine to play. Audit a hiring AI. Verify a docket of court cases. Nothing to install; it all runs in the browser.

🔍
The five critical AI questions

The only thing to memorize. Asked of every system, every week, until they're reflex, and then used on the AI you meet outside class.

🐢
Slow AI, on purpose

Not pro-AI, not anti-AI. Critical AI literacy means knowing when to use it and when not to, and saying so. The book discloses its own AI use, and its conflict of interest, on the about page.

The five critical AI questions

This book takes a critical approach to AI, and "critical" doesn't mean negative. It means looking closely instead of taking anyone's word for it: not the company's, not the critics', not this book's. Critical AI literacy is the skill of knowing what an AI system is, what it's for, how it fails, who benefits from your believing what you're told about it, and when to use it and when not to. That's the throughline of every chapter and every case, and it comes down to five questions.

The first three describe the system: name it, find out why it was built and for whom, and follow what happens when it breaks. The fourth is the critical one: every claim about a system, from a CEO, a headline, or a friend, comes from someone with something to gain, so ask who's saying it and what would settle it. The fifth is your call, and it isn't a decision until you've said what it costs. Chapter 1 runs all five on its own case. Every chapter after that runs them on a new one, until you can run them on your own.

1What is the AI system, and what does it do?
2Why does it exist, and who benefits?
3What happens when it fails, and who finds out?
4Who's saying that, and how would we know?
5Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

How each week works

1Read the chapter

Over the weekend. One real case, the concepts it needs, and the five questions run on it. Monday is the case, then discussion.

2Do the lab

Wednesday, in class, in pairs, at the computers in our lab classroom. A short recap of Monday, then the lab, then a debate: each lab ends with a real argument about the system you just ran, two sides, and the cost of each.

3Write it up

Friday night, on Canvas: the lab writeup and a five-question quiz on the chapter. The writeup's last item is a commitment card: one sentence you actually keep. AI as a tutor is fine; AI as the author is not, and you say which.

Midterm Wednesday, October 21. Last class Wednesday, December 2. Final exam Thursday, December 10, 10:30 a.m. Full dates on the schedule; grading and policies on the syllabus; how we argue on the rules; the final project on the Prototype project page.
