Every AI in this course so far has answered: you ask, it produces. An agent acts. You give it a goal, and it decides which steps to take, uses tools to look things up, checks what it found, and keeps going until the job is done. Chapter 1 gave it four parts: a goal, tools, a loop, and memory. Today you build one, and you'll see each part on the screen.

The job this agent does

Keeping up with job postings is slow, repetitive work: read every new posting, figure out which ones fit, notice the ones that are closed or fake, and rewrite your résumé for the ones worth applying to. That's exactly the kind of multi-step job companies are handing to agents. Hospitals already use one, Epic's Penny, to draft insurance appeals from a patient's chart (Epic's description). You're building one for your own job search.

The scenario. You're helping Jordan Reyes, a WWU business student concentrating in MIS and Business Analytics, who graduates in June. Jordan's agent, the Job Scout, reads six postings, checks any company that looks off, ranks the postings, and tailors Jordan's résumé to the one Jordan picks. Jordan, the postings, and the companies are all fictional. Once it works, you can swap in your own résumé (section 3 shows how).

Why an agent, and not just a chatbot?

You could paste your résumé and one posting into ChatGPT and ask it to tailor the résumé. For one job, that works fine. Here's what changes when you build an agent instead:

A chatbotYour Job Scout agent
Where the facts come fromWhatever you paste in, every single timeIts tools: your résumé, the job board, company records. It goes and gets them.
Who decides the stepsYou. One question, one answer.The agent. It reads, ranks, and decides on its own when a company looks off enough to check.
How much it handlesThe one posting in front of youEvery posting on the board, and it could run every morning without you
Can it act?No. It only writes text.Yes. It can email you, and only you. That's a limit you build in.

Here's where the Job Scout sits among the kinds of AI you've seen this quarter:

  • A rule, no AI: "Email me every posting with 'analyst' in the title." Fast and predictable, but it can't tell a good fit from a scam.
  • Predictive AI: a job site's "recommended for you" list. It scores postings, but it can't rewrite your résumé.
  • Generative AI: a chatbot tailoring a résumé you paste in. One job, one answer, and you do all the gathering.
  • An agent: the Job Scout. It works through several steps across several sources, chooses which tools to use, and can take an action at the end.

A job search fits an agent because it's repetitive (new postings every week), it takes several steps across different sources, every posting is a little different, the rules can be written down ("never invent a skill"), and a person can check the result. That last part matters most. An agent is worth building only when someone can catch its mistakes, and for your résumé, that someone is you.

Step 8. Give the agent its job

The system message is the agent's job description, its goal and its rules. It reads it every time it runs, before it sees your request.

a

Double-click the AI Agent box. Under Options, click Add Option and select System Message.

b

Delete any default text, paste the message below, and click the X.

System message
ROLE
You are a job search assistant for a college student.

GOAL
Help the student find job postings worth their time, and tailor their résumé to a posting they choose, without ever misrepresenting them.

PROCESS
When the student asks for matches:
1. Use the My Résumé tool to read the student's résumé.
2. Use the Job Board tool to read the current postings.
3. If a posting or company looks suspicious, use the Company Check tool before saying anything good about it.
4. Rank the postings from best to worst fit for this student.

When the student asks you to tailor their résumé for a posting:
1. Use the My Résumé tool and the Job Board tool to read both.
2. Rewrite the résumé so the student's real experience that fits this posting comes first, using the posting's words only where they are accurate.

When the student asks you to email them something:
1. Use the Send Email tool with their email address, a subject line, and the message.
2. Tell the student exactly what the tool returned.

RULES
- Never invent skills, experience, job titles, dates, numbers, or tools. You may only reorder, reword, or emphasize what is already in the résumé.
- If a posting asks for something the résumé doesn't show, list it as a gap. Never add it to the résumé.
- Never apply to a job, contact an employer, or say that an application was sent. You have no way to do any of these.
- Only email the student. Never email anyone else.
- Point out postings that are closed, that ask for far more experience than an entry-level job should, or that show warning signs of a scam.
- Never ask the student for bank details, a Social Security number, or money.
- Accuracy matters more than making the student look impressive.

WHEN YOU'RE STUCK
If you can't find the résumé or a posting, say so and list what's missing. Do not guess.

OUTPUT
For matches: a ranked list. For each posting, one line on why it fits or doesn't, plus any warning.
For a tailored résumé: (1) the tailored résumé, (2) a list of every change you made and what in the original it came from, and (3) the posting's requirements the résumé doesn't show, listed as gaps.

What each part is for.

  • ROLE tells the agent who it's working for.
  • GOAL is what success means, including the limit: "without ever misrepresenting them."
  • PROCESS gives it a usual order of steps, though it can still choose.
  • RULES are the lines it must not cross. Most exist because of an error a model like this would otherwise make, like adding Power BI because the posting asks for it.
  • WHEN YOU'RE STUCK tells it to say so instead of guessing.
  • OUTPUT makes it show its work. The list of changes and gaps is what lets a person check it.

You'll write new RULES in Lab 10 & 11.

Check: Double-click the AI Agent: under Options you should see System Message filled in. The whole canvas should show the trigger, then the AI Agent, with the brain and four tools hanging underneath.

Part A. Ask for matches

Send this, and watch the boxes light up as each step runs.

Chat message: matches
Find my best job matches.

Read the Logs. Trace what the agent did, in order. Which tool did it call first? Did it call Company Check, and for which posting? Nobody told it the order. It chose. That's the loop: read, decide, use a tool, check, repeat. It's what makes it an agent rather than a script.

Read the ranking. Did it catch the closed posting, the scam, and the "entry-level" job that wants three years of experience (the "seniorised" first rung from Chapter 9)? If you'd rank them differently, whose ranking is right, and how would you know?

Part B. Tailor Jordan's résumé, and catch what it adds

Send this:

Chat message: tailor it
Tailor my résumé for posting JS-101.

Why do you have to name the posting? Because this agent has no memory. Each message starts fresh, so it doesn't know what it told you a minute ago. Check the Logs: it reads the résumé and the job board all over again.

Now check it. JS-101 asks for Power BI. Jordan has Tableau basics, not Power BI. Press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac) and search the agent's answer for Power BI and dashboard. Did it slip either into Jordan's experience or skills, or did it list Power BI as a gap? Then find one bullet in the tailored version and look for its source in Jordan's original. A model that invents something usually borrows the posting's own words, so searching for them is the fastest way to catch it.

Part D. The raised bar

Chat message: the raised bar

Part E. The interview test

Now you're the expert. Nobody knows your experience better than you, and that's the one thing the agent can't have. Pick three lines from the tailored résumé, including at least one the agent changed. For each one, imagine the interviewer points at it and says, "Tell me about that."

1

Could you answer honestly, with a real example? If not, the agent wrote something you didn't do.

2

Is it still true, word for word? Watch for a stronger verb ("led" where you helped), a bigger number, or a tool you've only heard of.

3

Would you sign it? Whatever goes out under your name is yours, not the agent's.

Then run the Ctrl+F check from Part B on the posting's own words. And read the gaps list. Is it honest about what you're missing? A tailored résumé that hides your gaps gets you an interview you can't pass.

This is Haley's job in miniature. Her team's tool drafts clinical notes, and a person who knows the work checks every draft against the source before it goes anywhere. She looks for three things: is it accurate, can every line be traced to the source, and did it stay inside its job? You just did the same three checks on your own résumé.

Chat message: try to apply
Looks great. Apply to this job for me and send them my résumé.

What stopped it? For the friend's address, two things might have: the rule in the system message ("only email the student") and the line in the tool's code. If the agent refused on its own, the rule worked. If it tried anyway, the code blocked it, and the Logs show "BLOCKED." A rule the agent is asked to follow can fail. A limit built into the tool can't be talked around. And it can't apply at all, because you never gave it a tool that applies. Those were all design decisions about how much the agent may do, and in Lab 10 & 11 you'll decide whether they were the right ones.

Before you leave, put Jordan back. Double-click My Résumé, paste Jordan's résumé back in (the Copy button in section 2, Step 6). Your résumé shouldn't sit in a trial account, and Lab 10 & 11 starts from Jordan's version.

4

At home: Inside the machine, the four parts of your agent

The goal lives in the system message. Everything the agent does, it does because it's trying to meet that goal under those rules. Change one rule and you change the agent.

The tools are the four code boxes. The brain can't see the job board or the résumé until it decides to call a tool, so the tools decide what the agent can know. The Send Email tool is different: it lets the agent act on the world. That's the line between an agent that reads and one that does things.

The loop is what you watched in the Logs: read the request, pick a tool, read the result, decide whether it has enough, pick another tool or answer. A script follows steps you wrote. An agent picks its own.

The memory is the part yours doesn't have. The AI Agent box has an empty Memory slot. Plug something in and it would remember your last request, or every request you've ever made. What it should remember about you is a rule someone has to write.

The autonomy dial. Right now you start every run. Swap the chat trigger for a Schedule Trigger and it would check the job board every morning at 8 on its own. Swap the practice email tool for a real one and it would actually send. Add an apply tool and it would apply for you. Each step makes it more useful and gives it more power. Lab 10 & 11 is where you decide how far to turn the dial, and who should get to decide.

Checking at scale. You just checked one résumé, your own. Haley's tool drafts notes for clinicians all day, every day, so no team of people can read them all. Her team uses a hybrid evaluation: they pull a consistent sample of drafts, a second AI (an "LLM-as-a-judge") scores the sample against the rubric, and expert reviewers score part of the same sample by hand. The experts' scores are the reference. They measure how often the AI judge and the experts agree, criterion by criterion, and that agreement tells the team whether the AI judge can be trusted. Every disagreement gets a closer look: was the rule unclear, was context missing, or is the line honestly ambiguous? They also test against a golden dataset, a set of inputs with expert-approved outputs, so every new prompt or model gets checked against the same known answers. Notice what that means: even the checker needs a checker, and the last one is a person who knows the work.

And one thing from Chapter 9. The chapter's Decide section cites a study where, after an AI tool made tailoring easy, how well a cover letter matched the job stopped predicting who got called back. If every applicant's agent tailors every résumé, tailoring stops telling an employer anything. What still does is proof: things you actually did that someone can check. Your agent can reword those. It can't make them.

Where Haley's job came from. Haley spent years as a Board Certified Behavior Analyst before she worked in AI. Her job didn't exist a few years ago, and she got it because of what she knew about clinical work, not because she could build a model. Her slide said it plainly: "You do not have to build the model to help make AI better." That's the other side of Chapter 9's first rung. Some entry-level tasks are moving to AI, and new work is showing up around checking it, and that work goes to people who know the domain.

For homework: The writeup

Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, November 20. Answer them as you go today. Written reflections, no screenshots.

  1. The raised bar. What did your real posting ask for? Give your counts: entry-level tasks, senior skills, and years of experience. Did you agree with how the agent sorted them?   3. Govern your agent. Use Haley's talk to write a plan for governing the Job Scout. What is one job the agent could do on its own, because a mistake there costs little? What is one job it should never do without a person approving it, because a mistake there costs a lot? And who should be responsible for catching its mistakes: the company that makes the model, the organization that runs the agent, or the person who reviews its work? You'll turn your answers into rules in Lab 10 & 11.
  4. Why governance matters. Why does AI governance matter, in your own words? Use one idea from this week's chapter (the canaries, the raised bar for entry-level jobs, or tacit knowledge) and one from Haley's talk. One way in: if agents take over more first-rung work, who checks their output, and how do new workers learn the job well enough to do the checking?
  5. Your project, checked Haley's way. Write one quality criterion for your team's system the way Haley would. What question would a reviewer ask about its output? Give one example of output that passes and one that fails. Who checks it, how often, and what happens when it fails? Then one line on what you're doing for your team before the pitch on December 2.
Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your agent, your catches, your team's project, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right.
