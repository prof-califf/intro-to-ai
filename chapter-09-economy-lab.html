Run the Firm

You're the operations analyst at a mid-sized company, and the CEO wants an AI plan. Chapter 9 said a general-purpose technology pays off only when work is reorganized around it, and that the first people to feel the reorganization are the ones trying to get hired. Today you feel both from the inside. You run the firm for eight quarters and live through the dip, decide how many junior people to hire while AI does their old work, and map a job you actually want with Claude. Then you sit in the hiring manager's chair and debate what to do when AI can do most of the old junior work: cut entry-level hiring, or rebuild the entry-level job?

Section 1 is the dynamo from section 2.2, with you as the manager: buying tools is the extensive margin and redesigning work is the intensive margin (section 2.3). Section 2 is section 2.4's automating-versus-complementing choice, made with hiring. Section 3 puts codified and tacit knowledge on a job you want. And the debate takes the case into the hiring manager’s chair: what should a company do while the cause is still being argued?

1

In class: Eight quarters in the chair

Your firm starts at a productivity index of 100. Each quarter, pick one move. Adopt AI tools hands your people copilots inside today's workflows: quick wins that shrink each round. Redesign a workflow rebuilds one process around AI: it costs you now and pays permanently later. Hold steady is always available, and always defensible to the board. Play it twice: once however you like, once trying to beat your own final number. About 12 minutes.

Quarter 1 of 8 · productivity 100

The fiscal year opens. Your competitors are all issuing press releases about AI. Your board has read every one of them.

Year two begins without you, the review

What the model bakes in, on purpose: tools give +4, then +2, then +1, then nothing (the copilot plateau). Each redesign costs 4 points the quarter you do it and 1 the next, then pays +7 every quarter after, permanently. The numbers are invented; the shape is Paul David's J-curve. Nothing is saved.

Try the two pure strategies if you haven't: tools every quarter, and redesign early then add tools. The first tops out at 107. The second finishes around 121, after two ugly quarters. That gap is the chapter's whole argument, in integers, and the dip is why most real firms never collect it. A manager's review comes every quarter. The payoff comes later.

Now look at what real firms are choosing. Open the Stanford AI Adoption Monitor and find this month's readings for worker adoption and firm adoption. In the model's language, how much of the economy is on "adopt tools," and how much is on "redesign"?

2

In class: Build the senior bench (automating or complementing)

Your redesign worked: AI now does most of the first-draft, look-it-up, fill-in-the-template work your junior hires used to do. So the CEO asks the obvious question: why hire juniors at all? Here's what that decision does over eight years. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Bench · who will be your seniors in five years?
Your job
  1. Pick how many juniors to hire each year. Zero is the automating build: AI does the junior work, and nobody new comes in. Hiring juniors and giving them AI as a coach is the complementing build.
  2. Pick what the rest of your industry does. If other firms keep training juniors, you can hire their experienced people later, at a premium. If everyone stops, there's no one to hire.
  3. Press Run eight years and read the table. Seniors do the judgment work AI can't: the client who's about to walk, the number that's wrong before you can say why. One retires or leaves every year. A junior becomes senior after three years.

Invented numbers: 8 seniors to start, 7 needed for the judgment work, one leaving each year, three years to train a junior, outside hires at one and a half times the cost when any are available. The shape is the Canaries study's point, not a forecast. Nothing is saved.

The entry-level job was never just cheap labor. It was the company's training system, the way tacit knowledge got passed from one person to the next. The automating build looks cheaper every year until the year the seniors run out, and by then it's three years too late to start. And if every firm makes the same choice, nobody has anyone to poach. That's the chapter's "uncomfortable sentence" from the employer's side: AI reproduces the codified work, so the scarce thing becomes the tacit kind, and the only way to grow it is to hire someone who doesn't have it yet.

3

In class: Map your first job with Claude

Don't want to use AI? Do this instead

Same 20 minutes, no chatbot. Search O*NET OnLine (the U.S. Labor Department's job database) for a job you want after graduation. Copy ten of its listed tasks into a sheet. For each, mark codified or tacit, and whether today's AI could do it (low, some, high). Then mark which tasks an automating build would hand to AI and which a complementing build would keep with you. Count what's left of the job under each build, and name the two tasks you'd most want to get good at.

Section 2 was the employer's view. Now take yours. You'll ask Claude for a small interactive page that breaks a job you want into tasks and shows what happens to it under each build. Then you check its work against the government's own job database. About 20 minutes; finish at home if you need to.

Your manager asks every new hire the same question: which parts of your job should AI do, and which should stay with you? You'll answer it for the job you actually want. You're the person in the loop. Claude makes the page. You decide whether its tags are right.

4

At home: Under the hood, what the toys were computing

The firm simulator has no AI in it at all. It's the economics of AI with the AI removed: a small bonus that plateaus for tools, and a dip followed by a permanent gain for redesign. That the strategic problem survives without any AI in the model is the point. The hard part of a general-purpose technology isn't the technology. It's the reorganization, and who absorbs the dip.

The bench is a pipeline with a delay. Juniors take three years to become seniors, and seniors leave on their own schedule. Any system with a delay punishes decisions that look fine in the short run. That's why the Canaries gap shows up in hiring, not firing: the cost of not opening the door arrives years later, at a different desk.

Your job map is section 2.4 on one person. Exposure says how much of a job AI could touch. The build says what the employer does with that. Codified tasks are where AI is strong, because they're written down, which is what the models trained on. Tacit tasks are where you're scarce.

Claude's part. Claude listed twelve tasks and tagged them in a minute. It couldn't tell you which job you'd rather have, and its sense of what AI can do is a claim about itself. You checked it against the government's list and your own judgment, which is the tacit part.

5

In class: Debate

The question: AI can now do most of the old junior work at your company. Do you cut entry-level hiring, or rebuild the entry-level job? Left half of the room is Side A: cut it, and hire experienced people. Right half is Side B: rebuild it, and keep hiring juniors for a bigger job. About 45 minutes: 8 to read the records below, 12 to prep, 25 to debate. The Gilman Street rules apply.

Today you’re the hiring manager, not the applicant. You’re deciding what a company should do, the way you did with the senior bench in section 2. And before you start, one honest thing about the room: nobody here is arguing about whether you will get a job. The evidence below is mixed, the first rung is moving, and a lot of what’s hard about this job market right now has nothing to do with AI.

This week’s case: the canaries

Stanford researchers used ADP payroll records to compare young workers in jobs AI can do a lot of with young workers in jobs it can’t. Their August 2026 update found workers ages 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs about 19 percent behind their less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. The same update’s headline: no widespread displacement (Stanford Digital Economy Lab).

Both sides can use it. Side A: the gap is real, it’s in exactly the junior work AI does, and it’s widening. Side B: the authors call it a correlation, not proof of cause, and the jobs where AI helps people instead of replacing them aren’t shrinking.

Before you blame AI: the economy right now

Start with the honest part. The job market in fall 2026 is strange for reasons that hit everyone, not just people in AI-exposed jobs. Every number in the next two records lands on top of this one, which is why it’s so hard to tell how much is AI.

  1. WhenWhat happenedSource
  2. 2025

    A slow year to look for work

    Employers added an average of about 9,700 jobs a month in 2025, the weakest hiring outside a recession since 2002, by the AP’s account of Labor Department data.

    AP, via WTTW. (2026, October 2). US hiring slows and unemployment ticks higher. Open source

  3. 2025 to 2026

    Tariffs

    Broad new tariffs raised costs for consumers and for American companies that import parts. Stanford economists flagged tariffs, interest rates, and a low-hire, low-fire labor market among the biggest economic issues for 2026.

    The catch: it’s hard to separate what tariffs did to hiring from everything else happening at the same time. That’s true of AI too.

    Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research. (2026). The U.S. economy in 2026: What to watch for. Open source

  4. Since February 2026

    War and oil

    The war with Iran set off a major oil shock. The World Bank projected energy prices up 24 percent in 2026 and warned the hit would arrive in waves: energy first, then food, then broader inflation and higher interest rates.

    World Bank, Commodity Markets Outlook, as reported by The Standard, April 2026. Open source

  5. March 2026

    Recent graduates

    Unemployment for recent college graduates, ages 22 to 27, was about 5.6 percent, up from 3.6 percent in March 2019.

    The catch: higher than before, and still most recent graduates are working.

    Federal Reserve Bank of New York. (2026). Open source

  6. September 2026

    Low hire, low fire

    Employers added 29,000 jobs and unemployment ticked up to 4.2 percent. Companies aren’t laying off many people, but they aren’t hiring many either, and a measure of gross hiring has been stuck in a rut for more than two years.

    The catch: few layoffs is good news for people who have jobs. Few openings is bad news for people looking for their first one.

    AP, via WTTW. (2026, October 2). Open source

Notice the pattern. A slow, cautious economy hits first-time job seekers hardest, AI or no AI, because the first thing a nervous company stops doing is opening new seats. Side A will say caution is exactly why you hire experienced people. Side B will say that’s the economy talking, not AI, and cutting the pipeline fixes the wrong problem.

What the data says: AI and young workers

Now the part that is about AI. These are the strongest numbers for Side A, each with its catch.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. February 2026

    The timing question

    When the Stanford authors added the broadest set of controls, the declines in AI-exposed jobs only became statistically significant in 2024. They say the earlier declines are likely driven by things other than AI.

    The catch for Side B: from 2024 on, the controls didn’t make the gap go away.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab. (2026, February). Canaries, interest rates, and timing. Open source

  3. 2026

    Specific jobs

    On Stanford’s live dashboard, early-career software developers and customer service workers show large declines. Stock clerks show almost no age pattern. Young home health aides, whose work is relational and physical, are gaining faster than older ones.

    The catch: the sample is 25,000 firms that use ADP, and the lab says it represents neither the whole U.S. labor market nor every ADP firm.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab and ADP Research. Canaries Dashboard. Open source

  4. 2026

    Automating versus helping

    Jobs where more of the AI use is automation, people handing a whole task to AI, show declines or weaker growth for young workers. Jobs where AI is used to help people work show no clear relationship with employment.

    The catch: the usage data comes from the Anthropic Economic Index, and Anthropic makes Claude. It’s also a correlation.

    Canaries Dashboard, “Automation v. Augmentation.” Open source

  5. 2026

    Who’s in the exposed jobs

    Among workers ages 22 to 25, 43.8 percent of women work in the most AI-exposed group of jobs, compared with 32.4 percent of men. The researchers say the gender gap they see comes mostly from which jobs people hold, not from unequal effects within the same job.

    Canaries Dashboard, “Gender partition,” updated July 22, 2026. Open source

  6. 2025 to 2026

    The firm-level test

    Tracking tens of millions of résumés, two Harvard researchers found junior employment falls at firms that start building generative AI into their work, while senior employment holds.

    The catch: other careful studies using different data find small effects, including whole-population records from Finland and Norway that show no clear effect on young workers.

    Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger, SSRN. Open source Humlum & Vestergaard, Becker Friedman Institute. Open source Finland, ETLA. Open source Norway, CESifo. Open source

Notice the pattern. The gap shows up in specific jobs, at specific ages, where AI does the work instead of helping with it. That’s a real signal. It’s also a correlation in a cautious economy, and two whole countries don’t see it. Side A leans on the signal. Side B leans on the catches.

The first rung is moving: what’s growing

The evidence that the entry-level job is changing shape rather than disappearing. These are the strongest numbers for Side B, each with its catch.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. February 2026

    Reshaped, not erased

    The Economist’s economics correspondent reports that over three years, white-collar employment grew by about 3 million jobs, software developers were up 7 percent and paralegals 20 percent, and jobs that mix technical and people skills, like project managers and information security analysts, grew more than 30 percent. Secretaries and administrative assistants fell about 20 percent.

    The catch: these are The Economist’s own figures, counting all ages. The same video says entry-level analysts and programmers are the most exposed, and Stanford finds young software developers down sharply.

    The Economist. (2026, February 23). Why AI won’t wipe out white-collar jobs [Video]. Open source

  3. June 2026

    The “seniorised” entry level

    Looking at 2.4 million U.S. entry-level job ads, PwC found AI-exposed entry-level roles are seven times more likely to ask for skills that used to be senior, like judgment and leadership. Openings for these roles grew 35 percent since 2019, while other entry-level roles shrank 10 percent.

    The catch: job ads show what employers ask for, not who got hired. And PwC sells AI consulting.

    PwC. (2026). 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer [Press release]. Open source

  4. Ramp data

    The heaviest AI spenders

    In Ramp’s spending data, the companies spending most on AI grew their entry-level headcount by about 12 percent.

    The catch: those firms were already larger and growing faster.

    Ramp. AI jobs impact. Open source

  5. July 2026

    Economists on steering it

    Over 200 economists and AI experts, including 17 Nobel laureates, called for building the incentives, guardrails, and institutions to steer AI toward complementing people.

    The catch: a statement is a goal, not a result.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab, homepage, on the July 2026 statement. Open source

Notice the pattern. When people count all ages, AI-touched jobs are mostly growing. When they look only at the youngest workers, the picture is worse. And the entry-level jobs that are growing ask for more on day one. Side B leans on the growth. Side A asks where a 22-year-old gets senior skills.

Side A · left half

Cut it: hire experienced people

AI does the old junior work. The company hires people who already have judgment, and pays more for fewer of them.

What you have to prove: that the savings now are worth the empty bench later, and say where your seniors come from in five years.

Side B · right half

Rebuild it: keep hiring juniors, for a bigger job

Juniors start higher: checking AI’s work, handling the exceptions, learning judgment on purpose. The company pays to train them.

What you have to prove: that the training cost is worth it in a cautious economy, and say how a 22-year-old learns senior skills without the old junior work.

Rounds, 25 minutes
  1. Team up (3 min). Join the other groups on your side. Pick your best two points, an opener, and a rebutter.
  2. Openings (6 min). Side A, then Side B. About 45 seconds each: your claim, your evidence, and its source.
  3. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
  4. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new points.
  5. Switch (4 min). What was the other side’s best point? Someone new says it in one sentence.
  6. What would change your mind? (4 min). Name the specific reading on the dashboard, or the study result, that would move you. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

To resolve it: design the new first job. What does a 22-year-old do on day one when AI does the old junior work? How do they learn judgment, and who pays for the training? Then the personal version: name one skill from that job description you could start building this year.

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
