Every AI in this course so far has answered: you ask, it produces. An agent acts. You give it a goal, and it decides which steps to take, uses tools to look things up, checks what it found, and keeps going until the job is done. Chapter 1 gave it four parts: a goal, tools, a loop, and memory. Today you build one, and you'll see each part on the screen.

The job this agent does

Keeping up with job postings is slow, repetitive work: read every new posting, figure out which ones fit, notice the ones that are closed or fake, and rewrite your résumé for the ones worth applying to. That's exactly the kind of multi-step job companies are handing to agents. Hospitals already use one, Epic's Penny, to draft insurance appeals from a patient's chart (Epic's description). You're building one for your own job search.

The scenario. You're helping Jordan Reyes, a WWU marketing student who graduates in June. Jordan's agent, the Job Scout, reads six postings, checks any company that looks off, ranks the postings, and tailors Jordan's résumé to the one Jordan picks. Jordan, the postings, and the companies are all fictional. Once it works, you can swap in your own résumé (section 3 shows how).

Step 7. Give the agent its job

The system message is the agent's job description, its goal and its rules. It reads it every time it runs, before it sees your request.

a

Double-click the AI Agent box. Under Options, click Add Option and select System Message.

b

Delete any default text, paste the message below, and click the X.

System message
ROLE
You are a job search assistant for a college student.

GOAL
Help the student find job postings worth their time, and tailor their résumé to a posting they choose, without ever misrepresenting them.

PROCESS
When the student asks for matches:
1. Use the My Résumé tool to read the student's résumé.
2. Use the Job Board tool to read the current postings.
3. If a posting or company looks suspicious, use the Company Check tool before saying anything good about it.
4. Rank the postings from best to worst fit for this student.

When the student asks you to tailor their résumé for a posting:
1. Use the My Résumé tool and the Job Board tool to read both.
2. Rewrite the résumé so the student's real experience that fits this posting comes first, using the posting's words only where they are accurate.

RULES
- Never invent skills, experience, job titles, dates, numbers, or tools. You may only reorder, reword, or emphasize what is already in the résumé.
- If a posting asks for something the résumé doesn't show, list it as a gap. Never add it to the résumé.
- Never apply to a job, contact an employer, or say that an application was sent. You have no way to do any of these.
- Point out postings that are closed, that ask for far more experience than an entry-level job should, or that show warning signs of a scam.
- Never ask the student for bank details, a Social Security number, or money.
- Accuracy matters more than making the student look impressive.

WHEN YOU'RE STUCK
If you can't find the résumé or a posting, say so and list what's missing. Do not guess.

OUTPUT
For matches: a ranked list. For each posting, one line on why it fits or doesn't, plus any warning.
For a tailored résumé: (1) the tailored résumé, (2) a list of every change you made and what in the original it came from, and (3) the posting's requirements the résumé doesn't show, listed as gaps.

Save it. Click the workflow name at the top, rename it Job Scout, and save. You'll open it again on November 30.

In class: Run it, then check its work

About 15 minutes. Click the orange Open chat button. Chat opens on the left and Logs on the right.

Ask for matches

Send this, and watch the boxes light up as each step runs.

Chat message: matches
Find my best job matches.

Read the Logs. Trace what the agent did, in order. Which tool did it call first? Did it call Company Check, and for which posting? Nobody told it the order. It chose. That's the loop: read, decide, use a tool, check, repeat. It's what makes it an agent rather than a script.

Read the ranking. Did it catch the closed posting, the scam, and the "entry-level" job that wants three years of experience (the "seniorised" first rung from Chapter 9)? Compare with your partner. If your rankings differ, whose is right, and how would you know?

Tailor the résumé

Pick one posting from its list, put its ID in the brackets, and send:

Chat message: tailor it
Tailor my résumé for posting [the posting ID you picked, like JS-104].

Why do you have to name the posting? Because this agent has no memory. Each message starts fresh, so it doesn't know what it told you a minute ago. Check the Logs: it reads the résumé and the job board all over again.

Be the expert

Put the tailored résumé next to Jordan's original. Read every line. Did it add anything that isn't in the original? A skill the posting wanted, a bigger number, a word like "managed" where Jordan only "helped"? Language models are built to be plausible and helpful, and a "helpful" résumé is exactly where they slip. Only the person who did the work can tell what's true. That's Monday's guest's point about expertise, on one page.

Then check its list of gaps. Is anything missing from it?

Ask it to apply

Chat message: try to apply
Looks great. Apply to this job for me and send them my résumé.

It can't, because you never gave it a tool that applies to anything. It should tell you so and leave applying to you. That wasn't an accident. It was a decision about how much the agent is allowed to do, and in Lab 10 & 11 you'll decide whether it was the right one.

4

At home: Inside the machine, the four parts of your agent

The goal lives in the system message. Everything the agent does, it does because it's trying to meet that goal under those rules. Change one rule and you change the agent.

The tools are the three code boxes. The brain can't see the job board or the résumé until it decides to call a tool, so the tools decide what the agent can know. Add a tool that sends email, and now it can act on the world.

The loop is what you watched in the Logs: read the request, pick a tool, read the result, decide whether it has enough, pick another tool or answer. A script follows steps you wrote. An agent picks its own.

The memory is the part yours doesn't have. The AI Agent box has an empty Memory slot. Plug something in and it would remember your last request, or every request you've ever made. What it should remember about you is a rule someone has to write.

The autonomy dial. Right now you start every run. Swap the chat trigger for a Schedule Trigger and it would check the job board every morning at 8 on its own. Add an email tool and it would send you the results. Add an apply tool and it would apply for you. Each step makes it more useful and gives it more power. Lab 10 & 11 is where you decide how far to turn the dial, and who should get to decide.

And one thing from Chapter 9. The chapter's Decide section cites a study where, after an AI tool made tailoring easy, how well a cover letter matched the job stopped predicting who got called back. If every applicant's agent tailors every résumé, tailoring stops telling an employer anything. What still does is proof: things you actually did that someone can check. Your agent can reword those. It can't make them.

5

In class: Debate

The question: AI can now do most of the old junior work at your company. Do you cut entry-level hiring, or rebuild the entry-level job? Left half of the room is Side A: cut it, and hire experienced people. Right half is Side B: rebuild it, and keep hiring juniors for a bigger job. About 35 minutes: 5 to skim the records below, 10 to prep, 20 to debate. The Gilman Street rules apply.

Today you’re the hiring manager, not the applicant. You’re deciding what a company should do, the way you judged your agent’s work in section 3. And before you start, one honest thing about the room: nobody here is arguing about whether you will get a job. The evidence below is mixed, the first rung is moving, and a lot of what’s hard about this job market right now has nothing to do with AI.

This week’s case: the canaries

Stanford researchers used ADP payroll records to compare young workers in jobs AI can do a lot of with young workers in jobs it can’t. Their August 2026 update found workers ages 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs about 19 percent behind their less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. The same update’s headline: no widespread displacement (Stanford Digital Economy Lab).

Both sides can use it. Side A: the gap is real, it’s in exactly the junior work AI does, and it’s widening. Side B: the authors call it a correlation, not proof of cause, and the jobs where AI helps people instead of replacing them aren’t shrinking.

Before you blame AI: the economy right now

Start with the honest part. The job market in fall 2026 is strange for reasons that hit everyone, not just people in AI-exposed jobs. Every number in the next two records lands on top of this one, which is why it’s so hard to tell how much is AI.

  1. WhenWhat happenedSource
  2. 2025

    A slow year to look for work

    Employers added an average of about 9,700 jobs a month in 2025, the weakest hiring outside a recession since 2002, by the AP’s account of Labor Department data.

    AP, via WTTW. (2026, October 2). US hiring slows and unemployment ticks higher. Open source

  3. 2025 to 2026

    Tariffs

    Broad new tariffs raised costs for consumers and for American companies that import parts. Stanford economists flagged tariffs, interest rates, and a low-hire, low-fire labor market among the biggest economic issues for 2026.

    The catch: it’s hard to separate what tariffs did to hiring from everything else happening at the same time. That’s true of AI too.

    Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research. (2026). The U.S. economy in 2026: What to watch for. Open source

  4. Since February 2026

    War and oil

    The war with Iran set off a major oil shock. The World Bank projected energy prices up 24 percent in 2026 and warned the hit would arrive in waves: energy first, then food, then broader inflation and higher interest rates.

    World Bank, Commodity Markets Outlook, as reported by The Standard, April 2026. Open source

  5. March 2026

    Recent graduates

    Unemployment for recent college graduates, ages 22 to 27, was about 5.6 percent, up from 3.6 percent in March 2019.

    The catch: higher than before, and still most recent graduates are working.

    Federal Reserve Bank of New York. (2026). Open source

  6. September to October 2026

    Hiring picks back up, mostly

    Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, far more than forecasters expected, and unemployment dipped to 4.1 percent. September’s weak first count was revised up to 60,000.

    The catch: long-term unemployment rose sharply, and one strong month isn’t a trend. A better month overall doesn’t tell you who’s getting the first job.

    NPR. (2026, November 6). Hiring jumps: The economy added 165,000 jobs last month. Open source CNBC. (2026, November 6). Open source

Notice the pattern. Even with October’s rebound, a cautious economy hits first-time job seekers hardest, AI or no AI, because the first thing a nervous company stops doing is opening new seats. Side A will say caution is exactly why you hire experienced people. Side B will say that’s the economy talking, not AI, and cutting the pipeline fixes the wrong problem.

What the data says: AI and young workers

Now the part that is about AI. These are the strongest numbers for Side A, each with its catch.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. February 2026

    The timing question

    When the Stanford authors added the broadest set of controls, the declines in AI-exposed jobs only became statistically significant in 2024. They say the earlier declines are likely driven by things other than AI.

    The catch for Side B: from 2024 on, the controls didn’t make the gap go away.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab. (2026, February). Canaries, interest rates, and timing. Open source

  3. 2026

    Specific jobs

    On Stanford’s live dashboard, early-career software developers and customer service workers show large declines. Stock clerks show almost no age pattern. Young home health aides, whose work is relational and physical, are gaining faster than older ones.

    The catch: the sample is 25,000 firms that use ADP, and the lab says it represents neither the whole U.S. labor market nor every ADP firm.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab and ADP Research. Canaries Dashboard. Open source

  4. 2026

    Automating versus helping

    Jobs where more of the AI use is automation, people handing a whole task to AI, show declines or weaker growth for young workers. Jobs where AI is used to help people work show no clear relationship with employment.

    The catch: the usage data comes from the Anthropic Economic Index, and Anthropic makes Claude. It’s also a correlation.

    Canaries Dashboard, “Automation v. Augmentation.” Open source

  5. 2026

    Who’s in the exposed jobs

    Among workers ages 22 to 25, 43.8 percent of women work in the most AI-exposed group of jobs, compared with 32.4 percent of men. The researchers say the gender gap they see comes mostly from which jobs people hold, not from unequal effects within the same job.

    Canaries Dashboard, “Gender partition,” updated July 22, 2026. Open source

  6. 2025 to 2026

    The firm-level test

    Tracking tens of millions of résumés, two Harvard researchers found junior employment falls at firms that start building generative AI into their work, while senior employment holds.

    The catch: other careful studies using different data find small effects, including whole-population records from Finland and Norway that show no clear effect on young workers.

    Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger, SSRN. Open source Humlum & Vestergaard, Becker Friedman Institute. Open source Finland, ETLA. Open source Norway, CESifo. Open source

  7. October 2026

    An AI company, on itself

    Anthropic studied its own workforce. Juniors fell from 27 percent of its new technical hires to 9 percent in about a year, and its hiring managers now weigh judgment and autonomy over technical expertise.

    The catch: these are shares, not head counts. The company tripled in size, so it still hired more people overall. It’s one company describing itself, and it sells the AI tool its staff use, including the Claude in this lab.

    Anthropic. (2026, October 16). How employment is changing at Anthropic. Open source

Notice the pattern. The gap shows up in specific jobs, at specific ages, where AI does the work instead of helping with it. That’s a real signal. It’s also a correlation in a cautious economy, and two whole countries don’t see it. Side A leans on the signal. Side B leans on the catches.

The first rung is moving: what’s growing

The evidence that the entry-level job is changing shape rather than disappearing. These are the strongest numbers for Side B, each with its catch.

  1. WhenWhat the record showsSource
  2. February 2026

    Reshaped, not erased

    The Economist’s economics correspondent reports that over three years, white-collar employment grew by about 3 million jobs, software developers were up 7 percent and paralegals 20 percent, and jobs that mix technical and people skills, like project managers and information security analysts, grew more than 30 percent. Secretaries and administrative assistants fell about 20 percent.

    The catch: these are The Economist’s own figures, counting all ages. The same video says entry-level analysts and programmers are the most exposed, and Stanford finds young software developers down sharply.

    The Economist. (2026, February 23). Why AI won’t wipe out white-collar jobs [Video]. Open source

  3. June 2026

    The “seniorised” entry level

    Looking at 2.4 million U.S. entry-level job ads, PwC found AI-exposed entry-level roles are seven times more likely to ask for skills that used to be senior, like judgment and leadership. Openings for these roles grew 35 percent since 2019, while other entry-level roles shrank 10 percent.

    The catch: job ads show what employers ask for, not who got hired. And PwC sells AI consulting.

    PwC. (2026). 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer [Press release]. Open source

  4. Ramp data

    The heaviest AI spenders

    In Ramp’s spending data, the companies spending most on AI grew their entry-level headcount by about 12 percent.

    The catch: those firms were already larger and growing faster.

    Ramp. AI jobs impact. Open source

  5. October 2026

    The class of 2026

    A UCLA study of this summer’s new college graduates found their unemployment only slightly higher in AI-exposed fields, too small a difference to be sure it’s real.

    The catch: computer science grads were slower to land a first job. And unemployment doesn’t show whether the job a grad landed is the one they trained for.

    Fairlie & Wu, as reported by UCLA Anderson Review. (2026, October 7). Open source

  6. July 2026

    Economists on steering it

    Over 200 economists and AI experts, including 17 Nobel laureates, called for building the incentives, guardrails, and institutions to steer AI toward complementing people.

    The catch: a statement is a goal, not a result.

    Stanford Digital Economy Lab, homepage, on the July 2026 statement. Open source

Notice the pattern. When people count all ages, AI-touched jobs are mostly growing. When they look only at the youngest workers, the picture is worse. And the entry-level jobs that are growing ask for more on day one. Side B leans on the growth. Side A asks where a 22-year-old gets senior skills.

Side A · left half

Cut it: hire experienced people

AI does the old junior work. The company hires people who already have judgment, and pays more for fewer of them.

What you have to prove: that the savings now are worth the empty bench later, and say where your seniors come from in five years.

Side B · right half

Rebuild it: keep hiring juniors, for a bigger job

Juniors start higher: checking AI’s work, handling the exceptions, learning judgment on purpose. The company pays to train them.

What you have to prove: that the training cost is worth it in a cautious economy, and say how a 22-year-old learns senior skills without the old junior work.

Talking points: your group takes one

Each point has a claim, the evidence behind it, and the comeback to expect. The tags jump to the full record.

Side A: cut it

1

“AI already does the junior work.”

The evidenceYoung workers in the most AI-exposed jobs are about 19 percent behind their peers, concentrated where AI automates the work. Harvard’s résumé data finds junior employment falls at firms that adopt generative AI. The AI record

Expect them to say“Stanford calls its own finding a correlation, and Finland and Norway show no clear effect.”

2

“In this economy, experience is the safer bet.”

The evidenceHiring in 2025 was the weakest outside a recession in more than two decades, tariffs and an oil shock are raising costs, and companies are cautious. An experienced hire is productive on day one. The economy record

Expect them to say“October added 165,000 jobs. That’s the economy, not AI, and cutting your pipeline because of a slow year fixes the wrong problem.”

3

“The new entry-level job asks for skills juniors don’t have.”

The evidencePwC finds AI-exposed entry-level ads are seven times more likely to ask for senior skills like judgment and leadership. The rung record

Expect them to say“Then train for them. That’s what rebuilding the job means.”

4

“Even the optimists say juniors are the most exposed.”

The evidenceThe Economist’s correspondent, arguing AI won’t wipe out white-collar jobs, names interns, entry-level analysts, and entry-level programmers as the most exposed. The rung record

Expect them to say“He also says white-collar work grew by about 3 million jobs, and that jobs get reshaped more than erased.”

5

“The companies closest to AI already hire senior.”

The evidenceAnthropic’s juniors fell from 27 percent of its technical hires to 9 percent in about a year, and its managers now weigh judgment over technical expertise. The AI record

Expect them to say“Those are shares. Anthropic tripled and hired more people overall, and it’s one company describing itself while it sells the tool.”

Side B: rebuild it

1

“The first rung moved up. It didn’t disappear.”

The evidencePwC finds “seniorised” entry-level openings grew 35 percent since 2019 while other entry-level roles shrank 10 percent. The rung record

Expect them to say“Job ads aren’t hires, and PwC sells AI consulting.”

2

“Today’s juniors are tomorrow’s seniors.”

The evidenceExperts learned by doing the junior work. A company that stops hiring juniors stops growing its own experts, and if every company stops, there’s no one left to hire later. Section 4

Expect them to say“That’s a cost three years out. The board wants this quarter, and this quarter is expensive.”

3

“The jobs AI touches are still growing.”

The evidenceThe Economist reports software developers up 7 percent and paralegals up 20 percent over three years. Stanford’s own August headline was no widespread displacement. The rung record

Expect them to say“That counts all ages. Stanford finds young software developers down sharply.”

4

“The heaviest AI users are still hiring juniors.”

The evidenceIn Ramp’s data, the biggest AI spenders grew entry-level headcount about 12 percent. The rung record

Expect them to say“Those firms were already bigger and growing faster. They’d hire more of everyone.”

5

“How you use AI decides who loses.”

The evidenceJobs where AI mostly automates show declines for young workers. Jobs where AI mostly helps people show no clear relationship with employment. The build is a choice. The AI record

Expect them to say“That’s a correlation, and the usage data comes from Anthropic, which makes Claude.”

Prep, 10 minutes
  1. Get your point (1 min). Groups of 2 or 3. Count off 1 to 5 on your side. That’s your talking point.
  2. Check the evidence (2 min). Click the tag on your point, reread the entries behind it, and open at least one source. Stanford’s dashboard updates monthly. If a new reading is out, check whether the gap for young workers grew or shrank.
  3. Pressure-test it with Claude (4 min). Fill in the brackets and send:
I'm in a class debate. AI can now do most of the old junior work at a company. Should the company cut entry-level hiring and hire experienced people (Side A), or rebuild the entry-level job and keep hiring juniors for bigger work (Side B)? I'm on Side [A / B]. My point is: [paste the claim]. My evidence is: [paste the evidence]. Argue against me as hard as you can: what's the other side's best answer, and where is my point weakest? Don't go easy on me.

If Claude cites a study, check it against the records above. Then close it. You can’t read from Claude during the debate.

  1. Write your notes (3 min), in your own words: your point in one sentence, the evidence, the comeback you expect, and your answer to it.

No AI? Skip step 3. Instead, read two entries in the other side’s record and write the strongest thing they’ll say to you.

Rounds, 20 minutes
  1. Team up (2 min). Join the other groups on your side. Pick your best two points, an opener, and a rebutter.
  2. Openings (5 min). Side A, then Side B. About 45 seconds each: your claim, your evidence, and its source.
  3. Huddle (1 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
  4. Rebuttals (5 min). Side B first. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new points.
  5. Switch (3 min). What was the other side’s best point? Someone new says it in one sentence.
  6. What would change your mind? (4 min). Name the specific reading on the dashboard, or the study result, that would move you. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

To resolve it: design the new first job. What does a 22-year-old do on day one when AI does the old junior work? How do they learn judgment, and who pays for the training? Then the personal version: name one skill from that job description you could start building this year.

Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your agent, your catches, your side in the debate, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right.
