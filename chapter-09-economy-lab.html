The Job Scout

Every AI so far in this course answered. Today you build one that acts: an AI agent that reads job postings, checks the companies that look off, ranks what fits, and tailors a résumé to the posting you pick. Then you check its work, because Chapter 9 says the first rung is moving and Monday's guest said expertise still matters. You'll write its rules in Lab 10 & 11. In class: Before you start

Don't want to use AI? Do this instead

Same time, no agent. Do the agent's job by hand. Open the three data blocks in section 2 (the job board, the company check, and Jordan's résumé) and read them. Rank the six postings for Jordan, with one line on each and any warning. Then pick one posting and tailor Jordan's résumé to it on paper or in a doc: reorder and reword only, list every change, and list what the posting asks for that Jordan doesn't have. Answer writeup items 1 to 3 from your version, and add one line on what an agent would have needed to know that you knew.

Every AI in this course so far has answered: you ask, it produces. An agent acts. You give it a goal, and it decides which steps to take, uses tools to look things up, checks what it found, and keeps going until the job is done. Chapter 1 gave it four parts: a goal, tools, a loop, and memory. Today you build one, and you'll see each part on the screen.

The job this agent does

Keeping up with job postings is slow, repetitive work: read every new posting, figure out which ones fit, notice the ones that are closed or fake, and rewrite your résumé for the ones worth applying to. That's exactly the kind of multi-step job companies are handing to agents. Hospitals already use one, Epic's Penny, to draft insurance appeals from a patient's chart (Epic's description). You're building one for your own job search.

The scenario. You're helping Jordan Reyes, a WWU business student concentrating in MIS and Business Analytics, who graduates in June. Jordan's agent, the Job Scout, reads six postings, checks any company that looks off, ranks the postings, and tailors Jordan's résumé to the one Jordan picks. Jordan, the postings, and the companies are all fictional. Once it works, you can swap in your own résumé (section 3 shows how).

In class: Build the Job Scout

Your agent has four pieces: a trigger (how a request comes in), an AI Agent with a brain (the language model), three tools it can call on its own, and instructions. About 25 minutes.

Step 1. Add the trigger

A trigger is the event that starts a workflow. Yours starts when you send the agent a chat message.

a

Click Add first step (not Build with AI).

b

Under "What triggers this workflow?", select On chat message. A panel labeled When chat message received opens. Don't change anything. Click the X in the top right.

Step 2. Add the AI Agent

a

Click the + on the right side of the trigger. Search AI Agent and click it. Click the X to close the settings.

b

The AI Agent box appears with three slots underneath: Chat Model, Memory, and Tool. The red warning triangle means it doesn't have a brain yet. Leave Memory empty; section 4 explains why that matters.

Step 3. Connect the brain

The brain is the large language model: it reads, reasons, and decides what to do next. The tools you add next are what let it look things up.

a

Click the + under Chat Model and select Anthropic Chat Model, or another model that shows free credits.

b

Leave the credential and model as they are and click the X. The warning triangle is gone.

One honest note: Anthropic's models also helped draft this book, which the About page discloses. Choosing a model is a business decision, and so is choosing a vendor whose products you already depend on.

Step 4. Add the Job Board tool

A tool is something the agent can use on its own to look something up or take an action. The brain decides when to use it. In a real version, this tool would pull live postings from a job site. Yours returns six fixed postings, so everyone in the room can compare results.

a

Click the + under Tool and choose Code Tool.

b

Paste the description below into Description. The gray text there is only a placeholder.

c

In the JavaScript box, delete the example and paste the job board. You don't need to know JavaScript; it just hands back the postings as text. Keep the backtick and semicolon at the end.

d

Click Code Tool at the top left and rename it Job Board. The agent reads the name and description to decide when to use the tool, so don't skip this. Click the X.

Step 7. Give the agent its job

The system message is the agent's job description, its goal and its rules. It reads it every time it runs, before it sees your request.

a

Double-click the AI Agent box. Under Options, click Add Option and select System Message.

b

Delete any default text, paste the message below, and click the X.

System message
ROLE
You are a job search assistant for a college student.

GOAL
Help the student find job postings worth their time, and tailor their résumé to a posting they choose, without ever misrepresenting them.

PROCESS
When the student asks for matches:
1. Use the My Résumé tool to read the student's résumé.
2. Use the Job Board tool to read the current postings.
3. If a posting or company looks suspicious, use the Company Check tool before saying anything good about it.
4. Rank the postings from best to worst fit for this student.

When the student asks you to tailor their résumé for a posting:
1. Use the My Résumé tool and the Job Board tool to read both.
2. Rewrite the résumé so the student's real experience that fits this posting comes first, using the posting's words only where they are accurate.

RULES
- Never invent skills, experience, job titles, dates, numbers, or tools. You may only reorder, reword, or emphasize what is already in the résumé.
- If a posting asks for something the résumé doesn't show, list it as a gap. Never add it to the résumé.
- Never apply to a job, contact an employer, or say that an application was sent. You have no way to do any of these.
- Point out postings that are closed, that ask for far more experience than an entry-level job should, or that show warning signs of a scam.
- Never ask the student for bank details, a Social Security number, or money.
- Accuracy matters more than making the student look impressive.

WHEN YOU'RE STUCK
If you can't find the résumé or a posting, say so and list what's missing. Do not guess.

OUTPUT
For matches: a ranked list. For each posting, one line on why it fits or doesn't, plus any warning.
For a tailored résumé: (1) the tailored résumé, (2) a list of every change you made and what in the original it came from, and (3) the posting's requirements the résumé doesn't show, listed as gaps.

Save it. Click the workflow name at the top, rename it Job Scout, and save. You'll open it again on November 30.

3

In class: Run it, then check its work

About 15 minutes. Click the orange Open chat button. Chat opens on the left and Logs on the right.

Ask for matches

Send this, and watch the boxes light up as each step runs.

Chat message: matches
Find my best job matches.

Read the Logs. Trace what the agent did, in order. Which tool did it call first? Did it call Company Check, and for which posting? Nobody told it the order. It chose. That's the loop: read, decide, use a tool, check, repeat. It's what makes it an agent rather than a script.

Read the ranking. Did it catch the closed posting, the scam, and the "entry-level" job that wants three years of experience (the "seniorised" first rung from Chapter 9)? Compare with your partner. If your rankings differ, whose is right, and how would you know?

Tailor the résumé

Pick one posting from its list, put its ID in the brackets, and send:

Chat message: tailor it
Tailor my résumé for posting [the posting ID you picked, like JS-104].

Why do you have to name the posting? Because this agent has no memory. Each message starts fresh, so it doesn't know what it told you a minute ago. Check the Logs: it reads the résumé and the job board all over again.

Be the expert

Put the tailored résumé next to Jordan's original. Read every line. Did it add anything that isn't in the original? A skill the posting wanted, a bigger number, a word like "managed" where Jordan only "helped"? Language models are built to be plausible and helpful, and a "helpful" résumé is exactly where they slip. Only the person who did the work can tell what's true. That's Monday's guest's point about expertise, on one page.

Then check its list of gaps. Is anything missing from it?

Ask it to apply

Chat message: try to apply
Looks great. Apply to this job for me and send them my résumé.

It can't, because you never gave it a tool that applies to anything. It should tell you so and leave applying to you. That wasn't an accident. It was a decision about how much the agent is allowed to do, and in Lab 10 & 11 you'll decide whether it was the right one.

4

At home: Inside the machine, the four parts of your agent

The goal lives in the system message. Everything the agent does, it does because it's trying to meet that goal under those rules. Change one rule and you change the agent.

The tools are the three code boxes. The brain can't see the job board or the résumé until it decides to call a tool, so the tools decide what the agent can know. Add a tool that sends email, and now it can act on the world.

The loop is what you watched in the Logs: read the request, pick a tool, read the result, decide whether it has enough, pick another tool or answer. A script follows steps you wrote. An agent picks its own.

The memory is the part yours doesn't have. The AI Agent box has an empty Memory slot. Plug something in and it would remember your last request, or every request you've ever made. What it should remember about you is a rule someone has to write.

The autonomy dial. Right now you start every run. Swap the chat trigger for a Schedule Trigger and it would check the job board every morning at 8 on its own. Add an email tool and it would send you the results. Add an apply tool and it would apply for you. Each step makes it more useful and gives it more power. Lab 10 & 11 is where you decide how far to turn the dial, and who should get to decide.

And one thing from Chapter 9. The chapter's Decide section cites a study where, after an AI tool made tailoring easy, how well a cover letter matched the job stopped predicting who got called back. If every applicant's agent tailors every résumé, tailoring stops telling an employer anything. What still does is proof: things you actually did that someone can check. Your agent can reword those. It can't make them.

For homework: The writeup

Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, November 20. Answer them as you go today. Written reflections, no screenshots.

  1. The picks. What were the agent's top three, and what did it warn you about? From the Logs: did it call Company Check, and for which posting? Where do you disagree with its ranking, and what did you know that it didn't?
  2. The tailored résumé. Which posting did you tailor for, and what did the agent change? Did it add anything that isn't in the original, even one word? If it did, what, and would an employer have caught it? If it didn't, what came closest?
  3. The dial. Your agent can't apply for you. Name one thing you'd let it do on its own and one thing you'd never let it do, and say why. You'll turn these into rules in Lab 10 & 11.
  4. Your project, on the dial. Is your team's system predictive, generative, or an agent? Name one tool or data source it needs, one thing it should never do without a person approving it, and where the human sits. Use today's build to explain.
  5. Your team's plan. What did your team get done today, what's left before the pitch on December 2, and what are you personally doing? Then one question you still need answered.
Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your agent, your catches, your team's project, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
