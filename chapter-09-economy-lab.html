Run the Firm

You're the operations analyst at a mid-sized company, and the CEO wants an AI plan. Chapter 9 said a general-purpose technology pays off only when work is reorganized around it, and that the first people to feel the reorganization are the ones trying to get hired. Today you feel both from the inside. You run the firm for eight quarters and live through the dip, decide how many junior people to hire while AI does their old work, and map a job you actually want with Claude. Then you debate the question the chapter wouldn't settle: is AI closing the door on entry-level jobs, or is something else?

eight quarters in the chair · the senior bench · your first job, mapped with Claude · under the hood · one debate

From Chapter 9 · what this lab runs on

How today runs. Almost all of this lab happens in class, right now, at the machine in front of you. Work through the sections in order. At the end there are five questions to upload to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, November 20, and most people finish most of them before they leave, so answer them as you go.

Section 1 is the dynamo from section 2.2, with you as the manager: buying tools is the extensive margin and redesigning work is the intensive margin (section 2.3). Section 2 is section 2.4's automating-versus-complementing choice, made with hiring. Section 3 puts codified and tacit knowledge on a job you want. And the debate is the causal fight from section 3, the case.

1

In class: Eight quarters in the chair

Your firm starts at a productivity index of 100. Each quarter, pick one move. Adopt AI tools hands your people copilots inside today's workflows: quick wins that shrink each round. Redesign a workflow rebuilds one process around AI: it costs you now and pays permanently later. Hold steady is always available, and always defensible to the board. Play it twice: once however you like, once trying to beat your own final number. About 12 minutes.

Quarter 1 of 8 · productivity 100

The fiscal year opens. Your competitors are all issuing press releases about AI. Your board has read every one of them.

Year two begins without you, the review

What the model bakes in, on purpose: tools give +4, then +2, then +1, then nothing (the copilot plateau). Each redesign costs 4 points the quarter you do it and 1 the next, then pays +7 every quarter after, permanently. The numbers are invented; the shape is Paul David's J-curve. Nothing is saved.

Try the two pure strategies if you haven't: tools every quarter, and redesign early then add tools. The first tops out at 107. The second finishes around 121, after two ugly quarters. That gap is the chapter's whole argument, in integers, and the dip is why most real firms never collect it. A manager's review comes every quarter. The payoff comes later.

Now look at what real firms are choosing. Open the Stanford AI Adoption Monitor and find this month's readings for worker adoption and firm adoption. In the model's language, how much of the economy is on "adopt tools," and how much is on "redesign"?

2

In class: Build the senior bench (automating or complementing)

Your redesign worked: AI now does most of the first-draft, look-it-up, fill-in-the-template work your junior hires used to do. So the CEO asks the obvious question: why hire juniors at all? Here's what that decision does over eight years. About 10 minutes.

Mini-Bench · who will be your seniors in five years?
Your job
  1. Pick how many juniors to hire each year. Zero is the automating build: AI does the junior work, and nobody new comes in. Hiring juniors and giving them AI as a coach is the complementing build.
  2. Pick what the rest of your industry does. If other firms keep training juniors, you can hire their experienced people later, at a premium. If everyone stops, there's no one to hire.
  3. Press Run eight years and read the table. Seniors do the judgment work AI can't: the client who's about to walk, the number that's wrong before you can say why. One retires or leaves every year. A junior becomes senior after three years.

Invented numbers: 8 seniors to start, 7 needed for the judgment work, one leaving each year, three years to train a junior, outside hires at one and a half times the cost when any are available. The shape is the Canaries study's point, not a forecast. Nothing is saved.

The entry-level job was never just cheap labor. It was the company's training system, the way tacit knowledge got passed from one person to the next. The automating build looks cheaper every year until the year the seniors run out, and by then it's three years too late to start. And if every firm makes the same choice, nobody has anyone to poach. That's the chapter's "uncomfortable sentence" from the employer's side: AI reproduces the codified work, so the scarce thing becomes the tacit kind, and the only way to grow it is to hire someone who doesn't have it yet.

3

In class: Map your first job with Claude

Don't want to use AI? Do this instead

Same 20 minutes, no chatbot. Search O*NET OnLine (the U.S. Labor Department's job database) for a job you want after graduation. Copy ten of its listed tasks into a sheet. For each, mark codified or tacit, and whether today's AI could do it (low, some, high). Then mark which tasks an automating build would hand to AI and which a complementing build would keep with you. Count what's left of the job under each build, and name the two tasks you'd most want to get good at.

Section 2 was the employer's view. Now take yours. You'll ask Claude for a small interactive page that breaks a job you want into tasks and shows what happens to it under each build. Then you check its work against the government's own job database. About 20 minutes; finish at home if you need to.

Your job

Your manager asks every new hire the same question: which parts of your job should AI do, and which should stay with you? You'll answer it for the job you actually want. You're the person in the loop. Claude makes the page. You decide whether its tags are right.

Free accounts run out of messages

Building a page uses a free Claude account's messages up fast, because Claude rewrites the whole page every time you send something. If you hit the limit, Claude tells you when your messages come back, and your chat and page are saved. Send each step once, and if a check turns up problems, list every fix in one message.

4

In class: Under the hood, what the toys were computing

The firm simulator has no AI in it at all. It's the economics of AI with the AI removed: a small bonus that plateaus for tools, and a dip followed by a permanent gain for redesign. That the strategic problem survives without any AI in the model is the point. The hard part of a general-purpose technology isn't the technology. It's the reorganization, and who absorbs the dip.

The bench is a pipeline with a delay. Juniors take three years to become seniors, and seniors leave on their own schedule. Any system with a delay punishes decisions that look fine in the short run. That's why the Canaries gap shows up in hiring, not firing: the cost of not opening the door arrives years later, at a different desk.

Your job map is section 2.4 on one person. Exposure says how much of a job AI could touch. The build says what the employer does with that. Codified tasks are where AI is strong, because they're written down, which is what the models trained on. Tacit tasks are where you're scarce.

Claude's part. Claude listed twelve tasks and tagged them in a minute. It couldn't tell you which job you'd rather have, and its sense of what AI can do is a claim about itself. You checked it against the government's list and your own judgment, which is the tacit part.

5

In class: Debate

The question: Is AI closing the door on entry-level jobs, or is something else? Left half of the room is Side A: AI is a main reason young workers in exposed jobs are falling behind. Right half is Side B: something else is doing most of it: remote work, interest rates, and an economy coming off a hiring boom. In groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4: that number is your piece of evidence below. The Gilman Street rules apply.

This one is personal, and the evidence really is split, which is why it's a good debate. Economists who all looked carefully at real data disagree, in public, and there's a dashboard that updates every month. You'll be handed a side. Argue it with the best evidence it has, then admit what would move you.

Side A · left half of the room

Yes: AI is a main cause

The gap is concentrated exactly where the theory predicts: the youngest workers, in the most exposed jobs, in roles where AI automates rather than complements. It survives the obvious controls, and it kept widening after interest rates peaked. Start from the Canaries study and your senior-bench run.

The cost to name: the study is descriptive, not causal, by its authors' own account, and some careful studies find no clear effect.

Side B · right half of the room

No: mostly something else

Young graduates had a hard year for reasons that hit at the same time as ChatGPT: remote work makes training juniors harder, interest rates cooled hiring, and companies were correcting after a pandemic hiring spree. Start from the New York Fed analysis, the firm-level tests, and the Nordic records.

The cost to name: if you're wrong, telling a cohort "it's just the economy" means nobody prepares them for the change that's actually happening.

Nobody in this debate is arguing about a real applicant. You're arguing about what the evidence shows, the way an economist or a hiring manager would have to.

Your evidence: four pieces, one per number

Every side gets all four. If your number hands you evidence that seems to help the other side, that's your job today: find what it means for yours, and what your side would rather nobody noticed.

Evidence 1 · The payroll signal

In one line: young workers in the most exposed jobs are falling behind, and the gap is widening.

19%below where they'd be if they'd kept pace with less-exposed peers, ages 22 to 25
about 0comparable gap for experienced workers in the same jobs
monthlyhow often the Canaries Dashboard updates
  • The study: Stanford researchers used ADP payroll records, real paychecks, and found the gap concentrated where AI automates work, with employment flat or rising where it complements (Stanford Digital Economy Lab).
  • The stress tests: the gap survives excluding tech companies and controlling for interest-rate exposure and remote work.
  • The catch: the authors call it descriptive, not causal. The gap shrinks when education is accounted for, and some trends started before ChatGPT.

Evidence 2 · The firm-level test

Push your thinking with Claude

In section 3 you checked Claude's tags. Here, turn it around: tell Claude to argue against you. If it cites a study you plan to use, look it up in the chapter and check that it says what Claude says it says.

Argue against me
Now argue against me as hard as you can. What's the best point the other side will make, and where is my case weakest? Don't go easy on me.

If it goes soft, push back: "You're being too nice. Argue the other side for real." Then close Claude and write your notes in your own words. You can't read from Claude during the debate.

How the debate runs

Prep, 15 minutes.

  1. Get your side (2 min). Left half Side A, right half Side B. Groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4.
  2. Research with Claude (10 min). Your card, the two prompts, then your notes in your own words.
  3. Team up (3 min). Join one or two other groups on your side, so your team is 6 or 7 people: three teams per side. Pick your single best point, an opener, and a rebutter.

Rounds, 22 minutes.

  1. Openings (6 min). A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3. About 45 seconds each: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your position.
  2. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
  3. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first this time. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
  4. Switch (4 min). Thirty seconds with your team: what was the other side's best point? Then someone new argues it in one sentence.
  5. What would change your mind? (4 min). Out loud. Name the specific reading on the Canaries Dashboard, or the specific study result, that would move you. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

To resolve it: suppose nobody settles the cause for five more years. As the operations analyst from section 2, what should your company do about its entry-level pipeline in the meantime, and who pays for it?

For homework: The writeup

Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, November 20. Answer them as you go today. Written reflections, no screenshots.

  1. The dip. Give both runs' final numbers and shapes: plateau, J-curve, flat, or something else. Name the quarter you felt the most pressure to avoid the dip, and what exactly it threatened. Then this month's Adoption Monitor readings, and one sentence explaining the gap between worker and firm adoption from the inside: not "firms are slow," but why.
  2. The bench. How many juniors did it take to keep the judgment work covered in year 8, and what did that cost compared with hiring none? What happened when the whole industry stopped hiring juniors? In two sentences, explain why a company that closes the entry-level door this year might not notice for three years.
  3. Your first job. Attach the page you built with Claude, as an HTML file or PDF. Name one task Claude got wrong or invented compared with O*NET, and one tag you changed and why. How much of the job is left for you under each build? (No-AI path: your sheet's ten tasks, tags, and counts.)
  4. The debate. Which side were you handed, and what was the other side's best point? Where do you actually land on the cause, and what specific reading or study would change your mind? Did Claude's pushback find the weak spot in your case before the other side did?
  5. Your commitment card. From your job map: the one tacit skill you'll start building before you graduate, and how, specifically (a class, a job, a person you'll learn from). Then what it costs you this year, in time or money. Three sentences.
Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.
