Run the Firm

You run a 40-person firm for eight quarters. Each quarter, one strategic move. The dynamo lesson says reorganization beats adoption, but reorganization dips first and your board watches every quarter. Feel the pressure that kept factories on belts and shafts for forty years.

Eight quarters · three moves · one curve with your name on it

From Chapter 9 · what this lab runs on

Running the firm for eight quarters is the dynamo (2.2) with you as the manager. Buying the tool is the extensive margin; redesigning the workflow is the intensive margin (2.3), and the dip you'll feel is why quarterly incentives punish the redesign. Every hiring choice is section 2.4's automating-versus-complementing decision, and the chart you'll watch is the J-curve the chapter warns about.

1

Eight quarters in the chair

Your firm starts at a productivity index of 100. Each quarter, pick one move. Adopt AI tools hands your people copilots inside existing workflows, quick wins that shrink with each round. Redesign a workflow rebuilds one process around AI, it costs you now and pays permanently later. Hold steady is always available, and always defensible to the board. Play it twice: once however you like, once trying to beat your own final number.

Quarter 1 of 8 · productivity 100

The fiscal year opens. Your competitors are all issuing press releases about AI. Your board has read every one of them.

Year two begins without you, the review

What the model bakes in, on purpose

Tools give +4, then +2, then +1, then nothing: the copilot plateau. Each redesign costs −4 the quarter you do it and −1 the next, then pays +7 every quarter after, permanently: Paul David's J-curve in miniature. The numbers are invented; the shape is the research finding. Your job is to notice what the shape does to your decisions while a board is watching.

2

Compare your curves

a

Screenshot both runs. Note each run's final productivity and its shape: plateau, J-curve, flat, or something hybrid.

b

Find the quarter in either run where you felt the pull to avoid the dip, or took it and had to sit through the board's reaction. That feeling is the forty years, compressed. Name it precisely for the writeup: what exactly did the dip threaten?

c

Try the two pure strategies if you haven't: all tools every quarter, and redesign-early-then-tools. The gap between those two final numbers is this chapter's entire argument, in integers.

3

Check the instrument

Your simulated firm just lived the choice real firms face. Now look at what real firms are actually choosing: open the Stanford AI Adoption Monitor and find the current readings for worker adoption versus firm adoption, and anything the Monitor shows about depth of use, the intensive margin.

a

Record this month's numbers for individual and firm adoption. In the model's language: how much of the economy is on "adopt tools," and how much is on "redesign"?

b

Given what the dip felt like from the chair, write one sentence explaining the gap you just recorded, from the inside. Not "firms are slow." Why are they slow?

4a

Inside the machine: what the simulator was actually computing

The firm you ran is a small model of Chapter 9's dynamo (2.2). Under the hood, three things move your curve. Adoption alone raised output a little and cost money, because a tool bolted onto an unchanged workflow is the swapped engine: that's the extensive margin (2.3). Redesign cost more and made output fall for several quarters, because reorganizing work is disruptive; that's the dip. And only after the dip did the redesigned firm's output climb past the adopter's, because the workflow now let the tool change what the firm could do: the intensive margin. The reference curves the lab shows are the same model run with fixed choices, so your gap from them is your choices, not luck.

The hiring lever is section 2.4. Each time you chose to automate a role rather than complement it, the model cut a junior position's cost this quarter and, quietly, reduced the tacit knowledge available in later quarters, which is why the automate-heavy firms plateau. That's the Canaries study's finding as a rule in a simulator: the entry-level role was the training system, and cutting it is a loan against the future.

4b

Debate: who pays for the dip?

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · pick a side, argue it, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

You felt why the redesign is rare: it costs now and pays later, and someone has to eat the quarters in between. Argue who should.

  • Side A: firms, and the market will sort it. Companies that reorganize through the dip win the forty years; the ones that don't lose. Shareholders who punish the dip are pricing in the risk, and that's their right. Cost: the dynamo's payoff took a generation, and a manager's tenure is three years, so the market rewards exactly the behavior the chapter says fails.
  • Side B: the public, through the workers. The cost of the dip lands hardest on the entry-level cohort that never gets hired; subsidize apprenticeships, first jobs, and training so the tacit layer survives the transition. Cost: taxpayers funding a company's reorganization, and no guarantee the company keeps the junior after the subsidy ends.

Then the causal question the chapter refuses to settle: if the gap is remote work and interest rates and not AI, does either side's policy change? Name the instrument reading that would tell you.

4

Talk it out

Groups of four, then the room. Bring what you just saw on your own screen.

Discussion questions · about 15 minutes

1. The dip. Where did your firm's numbers dip, and did you stick with the reorganization? Why?

2. Who pulls the plug?. In a real company, who would have wanted to quit during the dip, and what would they point to?

3. The instrument. What did the live data show that surprised you, and what would you want it to measure next?

4. Your first redesign. If you ran a small business here in Bellingham, what's the first job you'd redesign around AI, and what would the dip cost?

5

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? The simulator contains no AI at all; it's the economics of AI with the AI removed. What does it say that the strategic problem survives the removal?

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who benefited from your reorganization, quarter by quarter, and who bore the dip? The model politely hid some of that inside "+7"; un-hide it.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? A firm that never reorganizes never has a visible failure, just a slow fade the statistics catch years late. What's the "missed cure" of this chapter, and who finds out?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Your chart says your strategy worked. Your board would say what? Your workers? What would you need to show each of them, and what would change your own mind about the strategy?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Keep your strategy, fix it, or start over: name the cost of the dip in your run, who ate it, and whether you'd sign off on it for a real firm.

6

The writeup

Due Friday, 11:59 p.m. · post to Canvas
  1. The runs. Both screenshots, final numbers, and shape names.
  2. The dip. Your step 2b answer: the quarter the pressure was strongest, and what exactly the dip threatened. Then the real-world translation: name one actual reason a real manager can't take the dip (bonus structures, quarterly earnings, a promotion window), and one structure that would let them.
  3. The instrument. This month's Monitor numbers and your one-sentence inside explanation of the gap.
  4. The five questions. Your answers, including who bore your redesigns.
  5. Your move. Back to your Day 1 workflow, one last time: the intensive-margin version of scaling it, the actual redesign, who it affects, and the dip it would cause. Three sentences. Wednesday's commitment card takes the first one.
