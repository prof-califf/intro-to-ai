Inside the Graph

EveryCure's real graph has millions of nodes. Ours has thirteen — small enough to see whole, real enough that every connection below comes from actual medicine. Your job: run the prediction yourself, then break it the way the world breaks it.

Three drugs · six diseases · one machine you can see all the way through

1

Meet the graph

Below is a miniature biomedical knowledge graph. Drugs on the left, diseases on the right, biological pathways in the middle. Every line is a simplified version of a documented relationship — including the famous ones: sirolimus really is the drug that saved Fajgenbaum, and propranolol really was discovered to treat infantile hemangioma after decades as a blood-pressure drug. Click any node to light up what it connects to. Spend two minutes just exploring before you score anything. Then the scoring, which is simpler than it looks: select a drug and press Score, and the system counts — for every disease on the board — how many distinct routes of three hops or fewer connect them through the biology. That count is the score. Nothing fancier is happening. It's EveryCure's ranking step shrunk to desk size: their system weighs millions of drug–disease pairs with machine learning; ours scores fifteen by counting paths. But both are asking the identical question of every pair: how strongly does the assembled research record already imply this connection? One design note before you start: this is the one lab in the course with no live AI in it, on purpose. The point this week is to see inside a prediction system — every dot, every line, every score in front of you is the entire machine. Nothing is hidden, so nothing needs to be taken on trust. Enjoy that while it lasts; it's the last system in this book you'll be able to say that about.

The graph

Step 1: click any node to light up what it connects to. Step 2: click one of the three drugs (left column) to select it — then the score button below wakes up.

How the score works

The scoring here is the honest toy version of what EveryCure does: count the distinct paths (up to three hops) connecting a drug to a disease. More independent paths through the biology = a stronger implied connection. The real system weighs paths with machine learning instead of counting them — but the logic, and the weakness you're about to find, are the same.

2

Run the predictions

a

Select Sirolimus and score it. Record the full ranking. Where does Castleman disease land — and can you trace, by clicking, the exact paths that put it there?

b

Score Propranolol. The system should surface infantile hemangioma — a repurposing that real doctors found by accident in 2008. Would this graph have found it on purpose? What research had to exist first?

c

Score Metformin. Notice its direct line to type 2 diabetes — approved uses are the easy, already-known edges. The interesting rows are everything below it. Which of metformin's indirect matches would you send to the human review team, and which would you discard? Write down your cut line and your reason.

3

Find the gap

Now the chapter's argument, live. Toggle Show research attention — node size now reflects how connected each node is, our stand-in for how much science has studied it. Find ROHHAD syndrome. It's real: an ultra-rare childhood disease, on the order of a hundred documented cases worldwide, no approved treatment, and very little research. In our graph, that lack of research is a single thin line.

a

Score all three drugs again and record what each predicts for ROHHAD. The scores aren't low because no drug could help. They're low because almost nothing connects — nobody has looked.

b

Write the mechanism in your own words, one sentence: how exactly did the research gap become the prediction gap? You'll need this sentence Friday.

4

Unpublish the science

Last experiment. Toggle Rewind: before the mTOR research. This deletes the findings connecting the mTOR pathway to immune disease — roughly the knowledge Fajgenbaum's own work helped establish. Then score sirolimus again.

Watch what happens to Castleman disease. Same drug. Same disease. Same underlying biology in the real world — the drug would still work. But the prediction is gone, because the graph never knew. Sit with that for a second: the machine didn't discover the cure. It inherited a discovery. Somewhere in the graph's real, million-node version, the next Castleman is sitting at zero right now, waiting for a paper that hasn't been written.

Why this experiment matters beyond medicine

Every predictive system in this course — lending models, hiring screens, news feeds — runs on some recorded version of the past. This lab is the cleanest place to see the rule: the system's blind spots are the record's blind spots, inherited at scale. When we meet hiring data in Chapter 6, bring this toggle with you in your head.

5

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? You've now operated it yourself. Describe the graph in one sentence to someone who thinks all AI is ChatGPT.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? What was the tool built to surface, and for whom? Who benefits when a prediction is right, and who benefits from the research that was never done?

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? You just watched a failure nobody would ever detect: ROHHAD at zero. Who, in the real world, is even positioned to notice that kind of nothing?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Your cut line says these predictions are worth a trial. Says who: you, the graph, or the papers behind it? What would you need to see before you'd defend the cut line to a patient?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Keep the prediction, fix the gap, or discard the match: for one disease you rewound, say which and what it would cost to light it back up.

6

The writeup

Due Friday, 11:59 p.m. · post to Canvas
  1. The rankings. Your recorded scores for all three drugs, plus the paths behind sirolimus–Castleman traced in your own words.
  2. The cut line. Which metformin matches you'd send to human review, which you'd discard, and the rule you used to decide.
  3. The gap. Your one-sentence mechanism for ROHHAD, plus: what would it actually take, in the real world, to raise that score honestly?
  4. The rewind. What the mTOR experiment showed, and one other domain (not medicine) where you suspect the same "undrawn edge" problem exists. Name the missing record.
  5. Your move. One sentence you'll carry into the rest of this course about what predictive AI can and cannot see. This becomes your commitment card Wednesday.
