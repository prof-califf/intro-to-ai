Inside the Graph

Chapter 4 said a predictive AI can only find what its record contains, and that where the doctor stands is a setting with a price. Today you work both halves with your own hands. You build a small knowledge graph out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it what might help, and find out what it can and can't tell apart. Then you train a small language model on the same facts and find out what it says when one of them is false. You play the doctor in the loop and find out what you actually catch. You read a slice of Every Cure's real scores with Claude, and you check its reading against the numbers. Then the room debates whether a doctor should still sign off on a diagnosis, now that a major medical journal has argued AI alone might do better.

In class: Build a knowledge graph, then ask it for a cure (Every Cure's MATRIX, in miniature)

In Chapter 4 you read how Every Cure's MATRIX suggests old drugs for new diseases. It runs on a knowledge graph, and a knowledge graph is less mysterious than it sounds: it's a pile of short sentences, each one a thing, an arrow, and another thing. "Caffeine blocks adenosine." "Adenosine causes sleepiness." Stack enough of them and you can follow the arrows to answers nobody wrote down. Here you build a small one out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it questions, and find out what it's good at and what it can't tell apart.

Every fact on the cards is real, simplified to one line. Nothing here is medical advice.

Why build one? Every Cure uses a knowledge graph to find old drugs worth studying for diseases that have no treatment. Yours does the same thing in miniature. Say a patient wants to regrow their hair. Ask the model you train, and it suggests a blood pressure pill, because doctors noticed blood pressure patients growing hair and someone wrote it down. That's repurposing. The graph only suggests; people decide what's worth testing. In section 3 you'll see the real scores Every Cure's graph gives.

    The takeaway

    A knowledge graph is a pile of sentences and a rule for walking the arrows. It even holds a real repurposing: a blood pressure pill for hair loss, found because doctors noticed a side effect and someone wrote it down. It told Benadryl from caffeine only because each arrow said whether it causes or blocks. Put a chatbot on top and you get the answers in plain words, which is one common way companies build them. But the chatbot believed your candy sentence as readily as the real ones, because every sentence looks the same to it, and it kept saying what you'd deleted until you trained it again. Neither one knows anything that happened after the last sentence went in. A study published tomorrow, a new warning on a label, a finding that turns out to be wrong: none of it gets in until someone adds it or takes it out. MATRIX is this machine with millions of sentences, which is why it can find what no person would. It's also why someone has to keep feeding it, and keep weeding it. And since nothing inside it can check a sentence, who checks the answer before anyone acts on it? That's section 2.

    In class: You're the doctor in the loop

    Your chatbot repeats whatever is in the graph, true or false, and it knows nothing about the patient in front of it. So something has to stand between an AI's answer and a patient, and that's usually a person. Where that person stands is called the loop. There are three places to put them:

    Today you start in the loop. Watch what happens when the line gets long.

    Your turn: pretend you're the doctor

    For the next ten minutes you're playing a doctor at a clinic. Not a real one: you don't need any medical knowledge, and these patients are made up. Your clinic has a chat assistant like the one you trained, hooked up to a knowledge graph with the same facts you built. When a patient comes in for an appointment, you type their main complaint, like "trouble sleeping," and the assistant asks the knowledge graph what helps and suggests the top answer. It only sees the complaint you typed, not the rest of what the patient told you.

    That's where you come in. Read the patient's note, then approve the suggestion or overrule it. Approve it, and a pharmacy agent takes over: an AI that acts on its own, writing the prescription into the patient's medical record and sending it to the pharmacy. Overrule it, and nothing is sent. Every right call can be made from two things on your screen: the patient's note and what the knowledge graph knows. There are eight patients. Some suggestions are good and some aren't.

    This is closer to real than it sounds. In January 2026, Utah let an AI from a startup called Doctronic renew some prescriptions and send them to the pharmacy, inside a state pilot that relaxes the usual rules. Doctors had to review its first 250 renewals in each kind of drug before it could act on its own, and Utah's medical licensing board has pushed back, saying it only learned about the program from the news. Your shift has the same setup: a person approves, then an AI sends.

    The takeaway

    A person in the loop is only a safeguard if they look. You can fail in two directions. Approving a bad suggestion because checking takes effort is automation bias. Overruling a good one because it looks strange is algorithmic aversion. Chapter 4 names both in section 2.5. Minoxidil for hair loss looks strange, and it's the real repurposing from section 1. Real doctors fail the same two ways. And your shift had AI on every side of you: an AI drafted the visit note, an AI suggested the drug, and an AI sent the order. AI-drafted visit notes are becoming common in real clinics, and in a setup like this the doctor's click is often the only human check in the whole chain. Ask yourself the question every hospital has to answer: what would make the person in the loop actually catch things? Fewer cases, a reason written for every approval, the key fact shown next to each suggestion? Each one costs doctor time, and that's the price of standing in the loop. Chapter 4 calls the spot where you stood the human gate: the point where a person decides whether an AI's output goes forward.

    4

    In class: Under the hood, how each one worked, Claude included

    You've now used four things: your own knowledge graph and its small language model, the clinic's AI assistant, Every Cure's real MATRIX data, and Claude. Here's what was inside each one, and how they fit together.

    1 The knowledge graph

    Look at "Everything your mini knowledge graph knows." That list is the whole machine. Each line is one sentence with three parts, a thing, an arrow, and another thing, and the picture is just those sentences drawn as dots and lines. Asking a question is one move repeated: start from anything you can take, follow the arrows, and see what reaches the problem. That's how a blood pressure pill shows up when you ask about hair loss. The pill had a side effect, somebody noticed it in patients and wrote it down, and now it's one line in the graph. That's drug repurposing, the same thing Every Cure does in section 2.2, and in real life it's how a blood pressure pill became Rogaine.

    Coffee connects to trouble sleeping just as surely as Benadryl does, so a graph that only checked for connections would suggest both. Yours read each arrow as a plus or a minus and multiplied along the path, which is how it knew coffee makes sleep worse and Benadryl makes driving worse. Real systems like MATRIX read far more than plus and minus, and weight each line by how strong the evidence is, but the idea is the same. And it believed "Candy reduces pain" as readily as anything on the cards, because it has no way to check a sentence. It only has the sentences.

    The real MATRIX is built in four layers, and you played three of them today:

    1. The facts. Most come from medical databases that people built by hand, one fact at a time. Some come from a computer program that read the summary of every study in the main medical research index and pulled out facts like "this drug treats that disease" on its own. There are far too many for anyone to check one by one, so people check samples and the top matches instead. You played this layer when you added cards, including the false one.
    2. The graph. Every Cure combines those databases into one giant graph. That part is careful bookkeeping, not AI.
    3. The ranking. This is where the AI is. Models score every possible drug and disease pair by the paths between them. You played a tiny version with the plus and minus scoring.
    4. The people. Experts read the studies behind the top matches and decide which ones are worth a trial. That's your job in section 2.

    So in the real system, a false fact doesn't need a student to add it. A program misreading one study summary can put it there, and unless that fact ends up in a top match someone reviews, the graph believes it as readily as "Candy reduces pain." The graph can only find what someone wrote down, and what gets written down depends on what someone paid to study.

    2 The small language model

    The model under your graph is a very small version of how many companies now build chatbots on their own data. In this lab, training means the model reads your graph's sentences and keeps them to answer from. Real chatbots use a large language model, trained on far more text, to word the answer. The knowledge graph, or a database, finds the facts, and a language model puts them into plain words. Because it only answers from your graph, it can't make up a fact, which is why it said it wouldn't guess when nothing connected. But it recommended candy just as smoothly as Benadryl, because the graph told it to. And it kept recommending minoxidil after you deleted that fact, until you trained it again. Hooking a chatbot to your data makes it stick to the data. It doesn't make the data right, and it doesn't keep the data current.

    3 The doctor in the loop

    No model here either. The eight patients are made up, and every suggestion comes straight from the graph's own facts, plus one from the candy sentence somebody typed in. Each right call turns on a single fact, which is why the facts stayed on screen. The Approve all button shows up halfway through on purpose. It's the temptation every busy reviewer faces: the suggestions have mostly been fine, the line is long, and checking takes effort. The real version is the "on the loop" setting, where people only look at what gets flagged, and the question is always who decides what gets flagged.

    4 Claude

    Claude is a large language model, a full-size version of the small one you trained in section 1. It learned from a huge amount of text, so it can read numbers it has never seen, explain them, and write them up for a boss in seconds. Three things shaped what you got back.

    1. The prompt. You told it who you are, what the data is, and exactly what to write. It did. Change the prompt and you change the answer.
    2. The data you pasted. When you said "based only on this data," it stuck to your data, and its pick was mostly whatever ranked first. It can only be as good as the ten rows you gave it.
    3. What it already knows. When you asked it to go beyond your data, it drew on its training, and sometimes a web search. That's how it can name a study or an approved drug your data leaves out. It's also how it can name a study that doesn't exist, which is why you checked one.

    Building the briefing page was the same move: it writes the code for a chart the way it writes a sentence, so the chart is only as right as the numbers underneath. Every Cure works this way at scale. MATRIX produces the scores, and people, sometimes with tools like Claude, decide what the scores mean and what to test.

    In class: Debate

    Should regulators stop requiring a doctor to sign off on an AI's diagnosis? Left half of the room is Side A, right half is Side B. In groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4: that number is your piece of evidence below. The Gilman Street rules apply.

    Monday's Every Cure case had a person at every gate. MATRIX ranked the drugs; Fajgenbaum's team read the ranking, Joseph's doctors decided to give the chemotherapy, and Joseph agreed to it. In August 2026, a viewpoint in JAMA, one of the most highly respected and highly ranked medical journals, argued that for thinking work like diagnosis, that person may be making the answer worse. Its authors wrote that "AI-alone medical care is likely to be better than physician-only or physician-AI hybrid care," and they asked regulators to stop requiring a doctor in the loop by default. In section 2 you were the doctor in the loop. Today you decide whether the rule that put you there should stay.

    Side A · left half of the room

    Yes, for diagnosis

    For the thinking work of medicine, like taking a history, making a diagnosis, and choosing tests, let the AI make the call. Move the doctor on the loop or out of it, and test the AI the way we test a drug. Start from the JAMA viewpoint and the 74, 76, and 90.

    The cost to name: when it's wrong, nobody is in the room, and the best evidence so far comes from cases on paper, not from real patients.

    Side B · right half of the room

    No, keep the doctor in the loop

    A licensed person signs every diagnosis, and the AI proposes. The doctor is the one who sorts out which facts matter, and the one who answers when it goes wrong. Start from the doctors' groups, Robert Wachter, and Every Cure's human gate.

    The cost to name: on the best numbers we have, adding the doctor dragged a 90 down to a 76, and every signature is doctor time that patients wait for.

    Nobody in this debate is arguing about a real patient's care. You're arguing about a rule, the way a regulator or a hospital board would.

    Your evidence: four pieces, one per number

    Every side gets all four. If your number hands you evidence that seems to help the other side, that's your job today: find what it means for yours, and what your side would rather nobody noticed.

    Evidence 1 · The JAMA viewpoint and the study behind it

    In one line: on written-up cases, AI alone scored higher than doctors.

    Evidence 2 · The pushback

    In one line: clean written-up cases aren't real patients.

    • The doctors' groups: six of them, including the American Medical Association, said most of the evidence comes from written-up practice cases, not real patients in real hospitals.
    • Robert Wachter (runs UCSF's department of medicine, advises Curai) agreed that given a clean set of facts, the AI often wins. Then he named two problems:
    • Garbage in, garbage out: the 90 was scored on cases a doctor had already boiled down. In an Oxford study, when ordinary people described the same cases in their own words, the AI made the wrong call about two-thirds of the time.
    • The doorman fallacy: a person does a dozen other things that only look unimportant until you remove them.

    Push your thinking with Claude

    In section 3 you checked Claude's numbers against the grid. Here, turn it around: tell Claude to argue against you. It's one of the fastest ways out of your own bubble, because it will find the other side's best point before they do. If it cites a study you plan to use, look it up in Google Scholar or the WWU library and check that it says what Claude says it says.

    1. Send the sample prompt under your evidence card, with your side filled in.
    2. Then make it argue against you:
    Argue against me
    Now argue against me as hard as you can. What's the best point the other side will make, and where is my case weakest? Don't go easy on me.

    If it goes soft or starts agreeing with you, push back: "You're being too nice. Argue the other side for real." Then close Claude, log out, and write your notes in your own words. You can't read from Claude during the debate.

    How the debate runs

    Prep, 15 minutes.

    1. Get your side (2 min). Left half Side A, right half Side B. Groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4.
    2. Research with Claude (10 min). Your card, the two prompts, then your notes in your own words.
    3. Team up (3 min). Join one or two other groups on your side, so your team is 6 or 7 people: three teams per side. Pick your single best point, an opener, and a rebutter.

    Rounds, 22 minutes.

    1. Openings (6 min). A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3. About 45 seconds each: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your plan.
    2. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    3. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first this time. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
    4. Switch (4 min). Thirty seconds with your team: what was the other side's best point? Then someone new argues it in one sentence.
    5. What would change your mind? (4 min). Out loud. Name the specific thing you would have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

    To resolve it: the three trials put the human in three different places and got three different answers. Name one task where each side would move the doctor, how it should be tested first, and who answers when it's wrong.

    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.
