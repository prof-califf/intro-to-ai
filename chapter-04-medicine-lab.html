Inside the Graph

Chapter 4 said a predictive AI can only find what its record contains, and that where the doctor stands is a setting with a price. Today you work both halves with your own hands. You build a small knowledge graph out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it what might help, and find out what it can and can't tell apart. You play the doctor in the loop and find out what you actually catch. You read a slice of Every Cure's real scores with Claude, and you check its reading against the numbers. Then the room debates whether a doctor should still sign off on a diagnosis, now that a major medical journal has argued AI alone might do better.

In class: Build a knowledge graph, then ask it for a cure (Every Cure's MATRIX, in miniature)

In Chapter 4 you read how Every Cure's MATRIX suggests old drugs for new diseases. It runs on a knowledge graph, and a knowledge graph is less mysterious than it sounds: it's a pile of short sentences, each one a thing, an arrow, and another thing. "Caffeine blocks adenosine." "Adenosine causes sleepiness." Stack enough of them and you can follow the arrows to answers nobody wrote down. Here you build a small one out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it questions, and find out what it's good at and what it can't tell apart.

    The takeaway

    A knowledge graph is a pile of sentences and a rule for walking the arrows. That rule found a real repurposing on its own: a blood pressure pill for hair loss. It told Benadryl from coffee only because each arrow said whether it causes or blocks, and it believed your false sentence as readily as the true ones, because every sentence looks the same to it. Remove one line and a discovery disappears. That cuts the other way too: the graph knows nothing that happened after its last sentence went in. A study published tomorrow, a new warning on a label, a finding that turns out to be wrong: none of it reaches the graph until someone adds it or takes it out. MATRIX is this machine with millions of sentences, which is why it can find what no person would. It's also why someone has to keep feeding it, and keep weeding it.

    2

    In class: You're the doctor in the loop

    Your knowledge graph in section 1 caught coffee on its own, because each arrow said which way it pushed. But it believed your false sentence as readily as the real ones, and it knows nothing about patients. It only knows facts. Nothing inside it could catch either problem. Something has to stand between an AI's answer and a patient, and that something is usually a person. Where that person stands is called the loop, and there are three places to put them.

    Every Cure works this way: its knowledge graph's matches go to people who decide which ones are worth a trial. The more of the loop a person covers, the more mistakes get caught, and the more doctor time it takes.

    Your turn: pretend you're the doctor

    For the next ten minutes you're playing a doctor at a clinic. Not a real one: you don't need any medical knowledge, and these patients are made up. Your clinic has a chat assistant hooked up to the knowledge graph you built. When a patient comes in for an appointment, you type their main complaint, like "trouble sleeping," and the assistant asks your knowledge graph what helps and suggests the top answer. It only sees the complaint you typed, not the rest of what the patient told you.

    That's where you come in. Read the patient's note, then approve the suggestion, and the patient gets it, or overrule it, and they don't. Every right call can be made from two things on your screen: the patient's note and what the knowledge graph knows. There are eight patients. Some suggestions are good and some aren't.

    The takeaway

    A person in the loop is only a safeguard if they look. You can fail in two directions. Approving a bad suggestion because checking takes effort is automation bias. Overruling a good one because it looks strange is algorithmic aversion. Chapter 4 names both in section 2.5. Minoxidil for hair loss looks strange, and it's the discovery your knowledge graph made in section 1. Real doctors fail the same two ways. Ask yourself the question every hospital has to answer: what would make the person in the loop actually catch things? Fewer cases, a reason written for every approval, the key fact shown next to each suggestion? Each one costs doctor time, and that's the price of standing in the loop. Chapter 4 calls the spot where you stood the human gate: the point where a person decides whether an AI's output goes forward.

    In class: Under the hood, how each one worked

    You just used two machines. Here's what was inside each one, and how it maps to the real thing.

    1 The knowledge graph

    Look at "Everything it knows." That list is the whole machine. Each line is one sentence with three parts, a thing, an arrow, and another thing, and the picture is just those sentences drawn as dots and lines. Asking a question is one move repeated: start from anything you can take, follow the arrows, and see what reaches the problem. That's how it found minoxidil for hair loss without anyone telling it. The blood pressure pill had a side effect, somebody wrote it down, and the arrows did the rest. That's drug repurposing, the same thing Every Cure does in section 2.2, and in real life it's how a blood pressure pill became Rogaine.

    Coffee connects to trouble sleeping just as surely as Benadryl does, so a graph that only checked for connections would suggest both. Yours read each arrow as a plus or a minus and multiplied along the path, which is how it knew coffee makes sleep worse and Benadryl makes driving worse. Real systems like MATRIX read far more than plus and minus, and weight each line by how strong the evidence is, but the idea is the same. And it believed "Candy reduces pain" as readily as anything on the cards, because it has no way to check a sentence. It only has the sentences.

    The real MATRIX is built in four layers, and you played three of them today:

    1. The facts. Most come from medical databases that people built by hand, one fact at a time. Some come from a computer program that read the summary of every study in the main medical research index and pulled out facts like "this drug treats that disease" on its own. There are far too many for anyone to check one by one, so people check samples and the top matches instead. You played this layer when you added cards, including the false one.
    2. The graph. Every Cure combines those databases into one giant graph. That part is careful bookkeeping, not AI.
    3. The ranking. This is where the AI is. Models score every possible drug and disease pair by the paths between them. You played a tiny version with the plus and minus scoring.
    4. The people. Experts read the studies behind the top matches and decide which ones are worth a trial. That's your job in section 2.

    So in the real system, a false fact doesn't need a student to add it. A program misreading one study summary can put it there, and unless that fact ends up in a top match someone reviews, the graph believes it as readily as "Candy reduces pain." The graph can only find what someone wrote down, and what gets written down depends on what someone paid to study.

    2 The doctor in the loop

    No model here either. The eight patients are made up, and every suggestion comes straight from the graph's own facts, plus one from the candy sentence somebody typed in. Each right call turns on a single fact, which is why the facts stayed on screen. The Approve all button shows up halfway through on purpose. It's the temptation every busy reviewer faces: the suggestions have mostly been fine, the line is long, and checking takes effort. The real version is the "on the loop" setting, where people only look at what gets flagged, and the question is always who decides what gets flagged.

    In class: Debate

    Should regulators stop requiring a doctor to sign off on an AI's diagnosis? Left half of the room is Side A, right half is Side B. In groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4: that number is your piece of evidence below. The Gilman Street rules apply.

    Monday's Every Cure case had a person at every gate. MATRIX ranked the drugs; Fajgenbaum's team read the ranking, Joseph's doctors decided to give the chemotherapy, and Joseph agreed to it. In August 2026, a viewpoint in JAMA, one of the biggest medical journals, argued that for thinking work like diagnosis, that person may be making the answer worse. Its authors wrote that "AI-alone medical care is likely to be better than physician-only or physician-AI hybrid care," and they asked regulators to stop requiring a doctor in the loop by default. In section 2 you were the doctor in the loop. Today you decide whether the rule that put you there should stay.

    Side A · left half of the room

    Yes, for diagnosis

    For the thinking work of medicine, like taking a history, making a diagnosis, and choosing tests, let the AI make the call. Move the doctor on the loop or out of it, and test the AI the way we test a drug. Start from the JAMA viewpoint and the 74, 76, and 90.

    The cost to name: when it's wrong, nobody is in the room, and the best evidence so far comes from cases on paper, not from real patients.

    Side B · right half of the room

    No, keep the doctor in the loop

    A licensed person signs every diagnosis, and the AI proposes. The doctor is the one who sorts out which facts matter, and the one who answers when it goes wrong. Start from the doctors' groups, Robert Wachter, and Every Cure's human gate.

    The cost to name: on the best numbers we have, adding the doctor dragged a 90 down to a 76, and every signature is doctor time that patients wait for.

    Nobody in this debate is arguing about a real patient's care. You're arguing about a rule, the way a regulator or a hospital board would.

    Your evidence: four pieces, one per number

    Every side gets all four. If your number hands you evidence that seems to help the other side, that's your job today: find what it means for yours, and what your side would rather nobody noticed.

    Evidence 1 · The JAMA viewpoint and the study behind it
    Four authors wrote it: Ezekiel Emanuel, an oncologist and bioethicist at Penn; Abe Baker-Butler, a research fellow; Neal Khosla, CEO of the AI health company Curai; and Vinod Khosla, an investor in Curai. They disclosed their interests. Their strongest evidence is a 2024 study in JAMA Network Open: 50 doctors got up to six hard patient cases, written up as summaries, and an hour to work them. Doctors alone scored 74 percent on a diagnostic reasoning scoresheet. Doctors using GPT-4 scored 76. GPT-4 alone scored about 90. The study's own authors cautioned against reading that as a reason to let AI diagnose without a doctor. Chapter 4, section 2.5.

    Evidence 2 · The pushback
    Six major doctors' groups, including the American Medical Association, answered that most of the evidence comes from written-up practice cases, not real patients in real hospitals. Robert Wachter, who runs UCSF's department of medicine and advises Curai, agreed that given a clean set of facts the AI often wins. Then he named two problems. First, garbage in, garbage out: the 90 was scored on cases a doctor had already boiled down. In an Oxford study he cites, when ordinary people described the same cases in their own words, the AI made the wrong call about two-thirds of the time. Second, the doorman fallacy: a person does a dozen other things that only look unimportant until you remove them. Chapter 4, section 2.5.

    Evidence 3 · Three hospital trials
    Three randomized trials moved the human on purpose, with real patients. In Sweden's MASAI trial, 105,000 women, an AI sorted mammograms by risk and one radiologist read the low-risk ones instead of two: 29 percent more cancers found, no more false alarms, 44 percent less reading work. In a fetal brain ultrasound trial at five hospitals in China, an AI beside the sonographer during the scan caught about nine points more real problems than the same AI used as a second look afterward. In England's PROMETHEUS trial, an AI that measured as the sonographer scanned didn't change accuracy in a way the trial could confirm, but scans took eleven minutes instead of twenty.     How the debate runs

    Prep, 15 minutes.

    1. Get your side (2 min). Left half Side A, right half Side B. Groups of 2 or 3, count off 1 to 4.
    2. Research with Claude (10 min). Your card, the two prompts, then your notes in your own words.
    3. Team up (3 min). Join one or two other groups on your side, so your team is 6 or 7 people: three teams per side. Pick your single best point, an opener, and a rebutter.

    Rounds, 22 minutes.

    1. Openings (6 min). A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3. About 45 seconds each: your claim in one sentence, your evidence, and the cost of your plan.
    2. Huddle (2 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    3. Rebuttals (6 min). Side B first this time. Answer one specific point, with evidence. No new arguments.
    4. Switch (4 min). Thirty seconds with your team: what was the other side's best point? Then someone new argues it in one sentence.
    5. What would change your mind? (4 min). Out loud. Name the specific thing you would have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

    To resolve it: the three trials put the human in three different places and got three different answers. Name one task where each side would move the doctor, how it should be tested first, and who answers when it's wrong.
