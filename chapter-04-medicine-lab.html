Inside the Graph

Chapter 4 said a predictive AI can only find what its record contains, and that where the doctor stands is a setting with a price. Today you work both halves with your own hands. You build a small knowledge graph out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it what might help, and find out what it can and can't tell apart. You play the doctor in the loop and find out what you actually catch. You have Claude map real research, and you check its work. Then the room works out what a doctor is actually for, now that a major medical journal has argued AI alone might do better.

From Chapter 4 · what this lab runs on

How today runs. Almost all of this lab happens in class, right now, at the machine in front of you. Work through the sections in order. At the end there are five questions to upload to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, and most people finish most of them before they leave, so answer them as you go rather than saving them for later.

The graph you'll build is section 2.2's knowledge graph, small enough to read in one screen. Every answer it gives is a claim about what's written in it, not a verdict about a patient (2.5). When you remove one sentence and a discovery disappears, you're watching section 2.3's gap: the system can only find what someone bothered to write down.

1

In class: Build a knowledge graph, then ask it for a cure (Every Cure's MATRIX, in miniature)

In Chapter 4 you read how Every Cure's MATRIX suggests old drugs for new diseases. It runs on a knowledge graph, and a knowledge graph is less mysterious than it sounds: it's a pile of short sentences, each one a thing, an arrow, and another thing. "Caffeine blocks adenosine." "Adenosine causes sleepiness." Stack enough of them and you can follow the arrows to answers nobody wrote down. Here you build a small one out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it questions, and find out what it's good at and what it can't tell apart.

Every fact on the cards is real, simplified to one line. Nothing here is medical advice.

Work in pairs. Keep notes as you go; Friday's writeup asks for what you saw, in your own words. (Rough notes are fine.)

1

Build it. Click the fact cards one at a time and read each one as it goes in. Watch the graph grow. The list under the graph, "Everything it knows," is the whole machine. There is nothing else inside.

2

Ask it. Ask about trouble sleeping, then drowsy driving. Both times, two things connect to the problem. The answer box shows which one helps and which one makes it worse, and the math behind it: every arrow either causes (+) or blocks (−), and it multiplies along the path. A graph that only checked whether things connect would have suggested both. Would you have taken both?

3

Let it discover. Ask about hair loss. What did it suggest, and what was that drug made for? Then find "Minoxidil causes hair growth" in the list, remove it, and ask again.

4

Feed it. Write your own sentence at the bottom of the tool. First a true one you know, then a false one, like "Candy reduces pain." Ask again. Can it tell which is which?

Each card is one fact about a drug or the body, cut down to a single sentence.     The takeaway

    A knowledge graph is a pile of sentences and a rule for walking the arrows. That rule found a real repurposing on its own: a blood pressure pill for hair loss. It told Benadryl from coffee only because each arrow said whether it causes or blocks, and it believed your false sentence as readily as the true ones, because every sentence looks the same to it. Remove one line and a discovery disappears. That cuts the other way too: the graph knows nothing that happened after its last sentence went in. A study published tomorrow, a new warning on a label, a finding that turns out to be wrong: none of it reaches the graph until someone adds it or takes it out. MATRIX is this machine with millions of sentences, which is why it can find what no person would. It's also why someone has to keep feeding it, and keep weeding it.

    2

    In class: You're the doctor in the loop

    Your knowledge graph in section 1 caught coffee on its own, because each arrow said which way it pushed. But it believed your false sentence as readily as the real ones, and it knows nothing about patients. It only knows facts. Nothing inside it could catch either problem. Something has to stand between an AI's answer and a patient, and that something is usually a person. Where that person stands is called the loop, and there are three places to put them.

    Every Cure works this way: its knowledge graph's matches go to people who decide which ones are worth a trial. The more of the loop a person covers, the more mistakes get caught, and the more doctor time it takes.

    Your turn: pretend you're the doctor

    For the next ten minutes you're playing a doctor at a clinic. Not a real one: you don't need any medical knowledge, and these patients are made up. Your clinic has a chat assistant hooked up to the knowledge graph you built. When a patient comes in for an appointment, you type their main complaint, like "trouble sleeping," and the assistant asks your knowledge graph what helps and suggests the top answer. It only sees the complaint you typed, not the rest of what the patient told you.

    That's where you come in. Read the patient's note, then approve the suggestion, and the patient gets it, or overrule it, and they don't. Every right call can be made from two things on your screen: the patient's note and what the knowledge graph knows. There are eight patients. Some suggestions are good and some aren't.

    The takeaway

    A person in the loop is only a safeguard if they look. You can fail in two directions: approving a bad suggestion because checking takes effort, and overruling a good one because it looks strange. Minoxidil for hair loss looks strange, and it's the discovery your knowledge graph made in section 1. Real doctors fail the same two ways. Ask yourself the question every hospital has to answer: what would make the person in the loop actually catch things? Fewer cases, a reason written for every approval, the key fact shown next to each suggestion? Each one costs doctor time, and that's the price of standing in the loop.

    3

    In class: Under the hood, how each one worked

    You just used two machines. Here's what was inside each one, and how it maps to the real thing.

    1 The knowledge graph

    Look at "Everything it knows." That list is the whole machine. Each line is one sentence with three parts, a thing, an arrow, and another thing, and the picture is just those sentences drawn as dots and lines. Asking a question is one move repeated: start from anything you can take, follow the arrows, and see what reaches the problem. That's how it found minoxidil for hair loss without anyone telling it. The blood pressure pill had a side effect, somebody wrote it down, and the arrows did the rest. That's drug repurposing, the same thing Every Cure does in section 2.2, and in real life it's how a blood pressure pill became Rogaine.

    Coffee connects to trouble sleeping just as surely as Benadryl does, so a graph that only checked for connections would suggest both. Yours read each arrow as a plus or a minus and multiplied along the path, which is how it knew coffee makes sleep worse and Benadryl makes driving worse. Real systems like MATRIX read far more than plus and minus, and weight each line by how strong the evidence is, but the idea is the same. And it believed "Candy reduces pain" as readily as anything on the cards, because it has no way to check a sentence. It only has the sentences.

    The real MATRIX is built in four layers, and you played three of them today:

    1. The facts. Most come from medical databases that people built by hand, one fact at a time. Some come from a computer program that read the summary of every study in the main medical research index and pulled out facts like "this drug treats that disease" on its own. Nobody checked those one by one. You played this layer when you added cards, including the false one.
    2. The graph. Every Cure combines those databases into one giant graph. That part is careful bookkeeping, not AI.
    3. The ranking. This is where the AI is. Models score every possible drug and disease pair by the paths between them. You played a tiny version with the plus and minus scoring.
    4. The people. Experts read the studies behind the top matches and decide which ones are worth a trial. That's your job in section 2.

    So in the real system, a false fact doesn't need a student to add it. A program misreading one study summary can put it there, and the graph believes it as readily as "Candy reduces pain." The graph can only find what someone wrote down, and what gets written down depends on what someone paid to study.

    2 The doctor in the loop

    No model here either. The eight patients are made up, and every suggestion comes straight from the graph's own facts, plus one from the candy sentence somebody typed in. Each right call turns on a single fact, which is why the facts stayed on screen. The Approve all button shows up halfway through on purpose. It's the temptation every busy reviewer faces: the suggestions have mostly been fine, the line is long, and checking takes effort. The real version is the "on the loop" setting, where people only look at what gets flagged, and the question is always who decides what gets flagged.

    4

    In class: Claude makes the map, you check it

    Your graph in section 1 believed "Candy reduces pain" because someone wrote it down. Claude can do the same thing with research: it can cite studies that sound real and aren't, or that exist but don't say what it claims. This time you're the doctor in the loop from section 2. Claude makes a map of real research on a question you actually care about, how to concentrate, and you check it before you trust it. About 20 minutes, in pairs.

    Keep it to the question on the page. Don't type anything about your own health or medications.

    The check · one map, three citations, one decision

    Before you start

    1

    Open Claude. Go to claude.ai and sign in with Google or your email. If you don't have an account, making a free one takes a minute.

    Build the map

    2

    Ask for the map. Paste this and send it. Claude will build a page you can click through. The last sentence is a dial, like the ones you set in Lab 2 & 3: you're telling it how careful to be.

    Build me a simple interactive web page that maps peer-reviewed research on what helps college students concentrate. Include four branches: substances like caffeine, sleep and exercise, social factors like phones and studying with other people, and the physical environment like noise, music, and a calm space. For each factor, show how strong the evidence is and list the key studies with authors, year, and journal. Only cite studies you're confident exist, and tell me when you're unsure about one.
    3

    Read the map like the graph. Which branches came back thick with research, and which came back thin? Write down one of each. A thin branch might mean nothing works there, or it might mean nobody paid to study it. The map can't tell you which.

    Check its work

    4

    Check three citations. Pick three studies from the map, at least one from a thin branch. Search each one in Google Scholar or the WWU library. For each, write down: does it exist, is it in a real journal, and does it say what Claude said it says? Then check the dial: did Claude flag any study as unsure, and were the flagged ones the ones that failed? Did a study it sounded sure about fail anyway?

    5

    Tell Claude what you found. Fill in the brackets and send it. Read how it answers being caught, or being confirmed.

    I checked three of your citations. Here's what I found: [what you found for each]. Which other claims on your map should I trust less, and why?
    6

    Decide. Based only on what survived your check, what is one thing you'll actually change about how you study? If nothing survived, say that.

    Then

    7

    Log out. These are shared machines, and Claude stays signed in for weeks. Click your initials in the bottom left and log out, or the next person in this seat gets your account.

    Rather not use AI, or can't sign in? Do it with a partner. Sit with someone who's running it, read the map with them, and do the checking in step 4 yourself. You never have to type a prompt.

    The takeaway

    The map looked like research, and some of it was. The parts that didn't survive your check were candy sentences: written down with confidence and never checked. The thin branches are Chapter 4's gap again, in your own life. The map shows what someone paid to study, not everything that's true. And checking cost you time, which is the price of standing in the loop.

    5

    In class: Discussion, what is the doctor for?

    We're at the very beginning of AI in medicine, and one thing it's already doing is exposing flaws in systems we trusted. A doctor double-checking the AI sounds like the safest setup there is. In section 2 you found out it only works if the doctor actually looks.

    In August 2026, a viewpoint in JAMA, one of the biggest medical journals, went further. For thinking tasks like diagnosis, its authors wrote that "AI-alone medical care is likely to be better than physician-only or physician-AI hybrid care." They point to studies like the one from section 2, where doctors alone scored 74, doctors with AI scored 76, and the AI alone scored 90, and they expect the gap to grow.

    So imagine it's five years from now, and they're right: AI is better at diagnosing than doctors. What is the doctor actually for?

    How it runs · about 30 minutes
    1. Think alone first (2 min). Before anyone talks, write down two things you think a doctor should still do, even if the AI diagnoses better.
    2. Form groups and pick a leader (1 min). Groups of 3 or 4. The leader keeps the list and speaks for the group.
    3. Make your list (10 min). What is the doctor for? List at least three things only a person should do, and next to each, what it costs to keep a person doing it. Then push your list with Claude, below: it argues with you, then suggests what you missed.
    4. Room share (8 min). Each leader reads one item the room hasn't heard yet. We build one list on the board, and anyone can push back.
    5. Now a professor (6 min). Same question, new setting. Imagine an AI can explain this course's material better than any professor. What is Professor Califf for? Make a second, shorter list in your group, then compare it to the doctor list. Which items show up on both? Those might be what a person is for.
    6. Step out of the future (3 min). Click through to the JAMA viewpoint and look at the authors. Two of the four are a venture capitalist who invests in AI health care and the CEO of an AI health care company. And six major doctors' groups, including the American Medical Association, pushed back that most of the evidence comes from written-up practice cases, not real patients. Is today's evidence enough to get us to that future, and who's telling us it is?
    Push your list with Claude

    Open claude.ai. The leader pastes your group's list into the prompt below and sends it. Read the answer together. Keep an item if you can answer Claude's objection, change it if you can't, and cross it off if Claude is right. Then look at what it says you missed. Add an item only if your whole group agrees with it and can say why in your own words. Then close Claude.

    One prompt per group
    We're discussing this in class: imagine that five years from now, AI is better than doctors at diagnosing. Here's our group's list of what a doctor is still for: [your list]. Argue against each item as hard as you can. Which ones could an AI, or a cheaper worker, do just as well? Then suggest two or three things a doctor is for that we missed.

    Rather not use AI? Skip Claude and work with a partner group instead. Trade lists, argue against each other's items, and each suggest two or three things the other group missed. The same rule applies: add an item only if your whole group agrees.

    6

    For homework: The writeup

    Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, October 16. Answer them as you go today. Written reflections, no screenshots.

    1. The knowledge graph. In your own words, explain what a knowledge graph is and how yours answered a question. Use two examples from today: a question where two things connected but only one helped, and how the graph told them apart, and what happened to the hair-loss answer when you removed one sentence.
    2. The doctor in the loop. How many bad suggestions did you approve, and how many good ones did you overrule? Did you press Approve all? Pick one patient you got wrong, or almost got wrong, and say what would have made you catch it. Then name what that fix would cost in a real hospital.
    3. The discussion. Which item on your group's doctor list would you fight hardest for, and what does it cost to keep a person doing it? Did Claude's pushback change anything on your list, and was it right to?
    4. Your sentences. The true sentence and the false sentence you fed it, and what it did with each. Then the citations you checked in section 4: how many held up? One sentence on what both mean for a system like MATRIX, or Claude, reading millions of sentences nobody checks one by one.
    5. Your commitment card. Pick a setting you know: a classroom, a job you've had, a team, a store, anything. Imagine an AI could do the main thinking part of that work better than the people there. What is the person still for? Name one thing, say why an AI shouldn't take it over, and what it costs to keep a person doing it. Three sentences. Hold onto it: Chapter 7 asks the same question about a car.
    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

    Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

    The knowledge graph (section 1)
    Self-contained, no network calls, nothing persists. The fourteen facts are standard pharmacology, simplified to one line each: diphenhydramine (Benadryl) is an antihistamine, and brain histamine promotes wakefulness, which is why it causes drowsiness and is the active ingredient in sleep aids such as ZzzQuil; caffeine blocks adenosine receptors; minoxidil is a blood-pressure drug that widens blood vessels, and hair growth seen in patients taking it led to its use for hair loss (Rogaine); ibuprofen reduces inflammation and can irritate the stomach. The plus-and-minus reading is a teaching simplification, not how MATRIX scores. No links yet; check each line against the drug's label before the next term.

    How MATRIX is built (section 3)
    Checked 3 October 2026. MATRIX's graph integrates RTX-KG2 and ROBOKOP (Every Cure, MATRIX documentation, docs.dev.everycure.org). RTX-KG2 integrates 70 knowledge sources, including UMLS, ChEMBL, DrugBank, and SemMedDB (Wood et al., BMC Bioinformatics, 2022). SemMedDB's facts were extracted automatically from the titles and abstracts of all PubMed citations by SemRep, a rule-based program (Kilicoglu et al., Bioinformatics, 2012). Every Cure's pair-scoring models include a transformer-based path classifier (MATRIX release notes, v0.12.0, November 2025). The section's "summary of every study" is SemMedDB's titles and abstracts, simplified.
