Inside the Graph

Chapter 4 said a predictive AI can only find what its record contains, and that where the doctor stands is a setting with a price. Today you work both halves with your own hands. First you run a small version of Every Cure's knowledge graph: pick a disease, see what the record connects it to, read the scores, and decide what you'd send to the science team. Then you run a hospital's AI program and decide where the doctor stands on four real tasks. Then the room argues about it.

Pick a disease · read the score · decide · set the gate

From Chapter 4 · what this lab runs on

The graph you'll click through is section 2.2's knowledge graph, the record turned into connections. Every score you see is a claim about the record, not a verdict about a patient (2.5). When you rewind history and watch a cure vanish, you're watching section 2.3's gap: the system can only find what someone bothered to write down. Your decisions are the analyst's, not the doctor's, which is where the chapter says the human belongs.

What to take away from Part 1

A score is a count of published paths, nothing more. The same number means different things depending on how much research sits behind it. And a disease nobody has studied scores zero against everything, not because no drug would work, but because the record has nothing to say. Your graph has 22 dots; MATRIX has about 8.9 million. Same mechanism. Nobody could run it by hand at that size, and that is why it's worth building even with everything you just saw about its blind spots.

What you just saw, in three sentences

Where the human stands is a setting, not a rule. The same kind of AI scored 90 alone and 76 with a doctor on clean notes, and got two in three wrong when the patient typed the story, so the right setting changed from card to card. Some settings have evidence and some don't. "Run the year" told you which of your four choices matched a real trial and which rested on nobody's data yet, and that is the external validation question from Chapter 4 applied to your own decisions. Some decisions have no cheaper setting. Joseph's chemotherapy could only be in the loop, with three people saying yes, and no budget changes that.

Discuss · Part 2 · 10 minutes, pairs then the room

The 2 a.m. patient, and the doctor as noise. On case notes a doctor had already written up, the AI scores about 90 percent. When ordinary people described the same illnesses in their own words, with no doctor to sort out which details mattered, it got about two in three wrong. The JAMA authors say adding a doctor to the clean-notes case makes the answer worse. Two questions, and your card settings are your evidence. As a patient, would you want the more accurate answer or the human one, and does your answer change between the clean-notes card and the 2 a.m. card? And if you ran the hospital, would you let patients use the 2 a.m. version at all, under what condition, and what would that condition cost?

4a

Inside the machine: how a knowledge graph scores a drug

The graph you clicked is a small version of Chapter 4's section 2.2, and the score next to each drug came from a rule you can state in a sentence: count the paths from this drug to this disease, weight each path by how strong its links are, and add. A drug that reaches the disease through a protein that many papers connect to it scores high. A drug that reaches it through one weak paper scores low. There's no biology in the machine, only a record of biology and the paths through it. That's why the rewind button is the lesson: erase the papers and the paths vanish, and so does the score, and so does the cure.

The real system, Every Cure's MATRIX, scores every approved drug against every disease the same way, at a scale no person could read, and then does the thing your gate in Part 2 did: hands a ranked list to a human who decides which ones are worth a trial. The score is a claim about the record (2.5). The gate is where the chapter puts the human, and Part 2 let you feel why moving it in either direction costs something.

4b

Debate: who should pay for the cures nobody profits from?

Debate · in class, about 20 minutes · pick a side, argue it, then switch

How it runs, every week. Sides are assigned, not chosen, so you're never defending your own belief. Two minutes with your partner to find your side's best point and its honest cost. Three minutes a side. Then everyone switches and does it again. Last five minutes: what would change your mind? Say it out loud. The person who names the evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were on.

The chapter's case exists because no drug company had a reason to build it. Argue the fix.

  • Side A: public money, public data. Governments should fund repurposing searches and the trials that follow, and require companies to release trial data so the graph has more to read. The record is thin where the profit is thin; only a non-market actor fixes that. Cost: public trials are slow, and a government that funds the search picks which diseases count.
  • Side B: fix the incentive, not the funder. Extend exclusivity or offer prizes for a proven second use of an old drug, so the market builds the graph itself. Cost: exclusivity raises prices on a cheap generic, which is the opposite of the case's happy ending.

Resolve it with the chapter's gap (2.3): whichever side you take, name the disease or group that still goes unread under your plan, because one always does.

The room · 10 minutes

One question to close, everyone in.

Who has to notice

Pick the card you set to out of the loop, or a disease no drug can reach on the graph. Something there will eventually go wrong: a case the AI misses, a cure nobody looks for. Name the one person who would find out first, and how long it would take them. Was that person in the room when the decision got made? That is the question Chapter 4 keeps asking, and it's the one to carry into the writeup.

The writeup (for homework)

Can you use AI on this writeup? Yes, with eyes open. Same line as Lab 1.

Use AI as a tutor: ask it to quiz you on in, on, and out of the loop, to argue against your settings so you can defend them, or to fix your grammar. Don't use it as the author: the paragraphs are yours, and a chatbot describing the blind spots of a system it never ran will sound sure and won't have looked. The calculator test: if the tool does the thing the assignment exists to teach, it isn't allowed on that part. And say so at the bottom: which tool, if any, and for what. "None" is fine. Silence isn't.
