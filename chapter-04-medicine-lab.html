Inside the Graph

Chapter 4 said a predictive AI can only find what its record contains, and that where the doctor stands is a setting with a price. Today you work both halves with your own hands. First you build a small knowledge graph out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it what might help, and find out what it can and can't tell apart. Then you run a hospital's AI program and decide where the doctor stands on four real tasks. Then the room argues about it.

a knowledge graph you build · the doctor's gate · under the hood · one debate · five questions on your own choices

From Chapter 4 · what this lab runs on

How today runs. Almost all of this lab happens in class, right now, at the machine in front of you. Work through the sections in order. At the end there are five questions to upload to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, and most people finish most of them before they leave, so answer them as you go rather than saving them for later.

The graph you'll build is section 2.2's knowledge graph, small enough to read in one screen. Every answer it gives is a claim about what's written in it, not a verdict about a patient (2.5). When you remove one sentence and a discovery disappears, you're watching section 2.3's gap: the system can only find what someone bothered to write down.

1

In class: Build a knowledge graph, then ask it for a cure (Every Cure's MATRIX, in miniature)

In Chapter 4 you read how Every Cure's MATRIX suggests old drugs for new diseases. It runs on a knowledge graph, and a knowledge graph is less mysterious than it sounds: it's a pile of short sentences, each one a thing, an arrow, and another thing. "Caffeine blocks adenosine." "Adenosine causes sleepiness." Stack enough of them and you can follow the arrows to answers nobody wrote down. Here you build a small one out of things from a medicine cabinet, ask it questions, and find out what it's good at and what it can't tell apart.

Every fact on the cards is real, simplified to one line. Nothing here is medical advice.

Work in pairs. Keep notes as you go; Friday's writeup asks for what you saw, in your own words. (Rough notes are fine. Precision is what the discussion is for.)

    The takeaway

    A knowledge graph is a pile of sentences and a rule for walking the arrows. That rule found a real repurposing on its own: a blood pressure pill for hair loss. It also suggested coffee for insomnia until you taught it to read "blocks" and "causes," and it believed your false sentence as readily as the true ones, because every sentence looks the same to it. Remove one line and a discovery disappears. MATRIX is this machine with millions of sentences, which is why it can find what no person would, and why it can only find what somebody wrote down.

    The takeaway

    Where the human stands is a setting, not a rule, and it has a price. Seven shifts could not cover eleven shifts of care, so every map in the room has a trade in it. Some settings have evidence and some don't. The striped tiles are settings nobody has tested, and the only measured setting for the 2 a.m. card is the one where the AI got two in three wrong. That is the external validation question from Chapter 4 applied to your own decisions. Some decisions have no cheaper setting. Joseph's chemotherapy could only be in the loop, with three people saying yes, and no budget changes that.

    3

    In class: Under the hood, how each one worked

    You just used two machines. Here's what was inside each one, and how it maps to the real thing.

    1 The knowledge graph

    Look at "Everything it knows." That list is the whole machine. Each line is one sentence with three parts, a thing, an arrow, and another thing, and the picture is just those sentences drawn as dots and lines. Asking a question is one move repeated: start from anything you can take, follow the arrows, and see what reaches the problem. That's how it found minoxidil for hair loss without anyone telling it. The blood pressure pill had a side effect, somebody wrote it down, and the arrows did the rest. That's drug repurposing, the same thing Every Cure does in section 2.2, and in real life it's how a blood pressure pill became Rogaine.

    When it only followed the arrows, it suggested coffee for insomnia and Benadryl for drowsy driving, because a connection is a connection. When you had it read the words, it multiplied pluses and minuses along each path, and the same two drugs flipped. Real systems like MATRIX read far more than plus and minus, and weight each line by how strong the evidence is, but the idea is the same. And it believed "Candy reduces pain" as readily as anything on the cards, because it has no way to check a sentence. It only has the sentences.

    MATRIX runs this on millions of sentences pulled from published research, and then does what your gate in section 2 did: hands a ranked list to people who decide which ones are worth a trial. The graph can only find what someone wrote down, and what gets written down depends on what someone paid to study.

    2 The gate

    The gate has no model at all. Each card's three results are written from the study or case linked on it, and "Run the year" reads back the one you picked. A dark tile means the linked study measured that setting; a striped tile means it didn't, and the tool refuses to make up a number for it. The shift costs are invented, but they keep one real proportion: MASAI's on-the-loop setting cut reading work by 44 percent, so on the loop costs about half of in the loop on that card. The gate is where the chapter puts the human, and section 2 let you feel why moving it in either direction costs something.

    4

    In class: Debate

    Chapter 4's case exists because no drug company had a reason to build it. Your job today: decide who should pay for the next one.

    The options: Four ways to pay for cures nobody profits from

    You saw the last step in section 1: remove one sentence and a discovery disappears. Each of these ideas is a real one people argue about. Your research finds out whether, and where, it has been tried.

    Copy the description of each idea (you'll paste one into Claude)
    1. Public funding: the government pays for searches that look for new uses of old, cheap drugs, and for the clinical trials that test what the searches find.
    2. Open trial data: drug companies are required to publicly release the results of their clinical trials, including trials that failed, so researchers and AI systems have more to read.
    3. Longer exclusivity: a company that proves an old drug works for a new disease gets extra years to sell the drug for that use without generic competition.
    4. Prizes: a cash reward, paid by a government or a foundation, to whoever proves an old drug works for a new disease.

    Topic: Who should pay for the cures nobody profits from?

    • Side A (left half of the room): public money, public data. The record is thin where the profit is thin, and only someone outside the market fixes that. Cost to name: public trials are slow, and a government that funds the search picks which diseases count.
    • Side B (right half): fix the incentive, not the funder. Pay for results, and the market builds the graph itself. Cost to name: exclusivity raises the price of a cheap generic, the opposite of the case's happy ending.
    How the debate runs · about 35 minutes
    1. Your side is where you sit (2 min). Groups of 2 or 3 on your side. Count off 1 to 4: that's your idea.
    2. Research with Claude (10 min). Follow the box below.
    3. Team up (4 min). Teams of 6 or 7, three per side: A1 to A3, B1 to B3. Pick an opener and a rebutter.
    4. Openings (5 min). Openers stand up where they are. About 45 seconds: claim, evidence, cost.
    5. Huddle (1 min). Rebutters pick one point the other side made.
    6. Rebuttals (5 min). Stand up and face the other side, Side B first. Answer one point. No new arguments.
    7. Switch (3 min). Someone new argues the other side's best point in one sentence.
    8. What did you hear? (3 min). Which argument from the other side was hardest to answer? No winner is declared.
    Research with Claude: take your idea apart · 10 minutes
    1. Open Claude. Paste your idea's description (from the box above) and say which side you're on.
    2. Ask: "What does this mean in practice? Who pays, and who decides which diseases get studied?"
    3. Ask: "Has this ever actually been tried? What happened?"
    4. Ask: "Who benefits from this, and who could still be left out?"
    5. Ask: "Would this get an old drug studied for a rare disease that no company would profit from? Why or why not?"
    6. Ask: "Based on all this, what are the strongest points for my side, and the best objection from the other side?"
    Sample prompt for the first step. Copy it, then swap in your own idea and side.
    I'm in an intro business class debating this topic: Who should pay for the cures nobody profits from, when an old, cheap drug might work for a rare disease but no drug company would make money testing it? I'm on Side B: fix the incentive, not the funder. My idea is longer exclusivity: a company that proves an old drug works for a new disease gets extra years to sell the drug for that use without generic competition. What does this mean in practice? Who pays, and who decides which diseases get studied?

    Then close Claude. Write four notes in your own words: your strongest point, the evidence behind it, the honest cost of your position, and what the other side will probably say. You cannot read from Claude.

    5

    In class: Discussion

    Pairs, then the room. Bring what you just saw on your own screen; your card settings are your evidence.

    Discussion questions · about 20 minutes

    1. The 2 a.m. patient. On case notes a doctor had already written up, the AI scores about 90 percent. When ordinary people described the same illnesses in their own words, with no doctor to sort out which details mattered, it got about two in three wrong. As a patient, would you want the more accurate answer or the human one, and does your answer change between the clean-notes card and the 2 a.m. card?

    2. The doctor as noise. The JAMA authors say adding a doctor to the clean-notes case makes the answer worse. If you ran the hospital, would you let patients use the 2 a.m. version at all, under what condition, and what would that condition cost?

    3. Who has to notice. Pick the card you set to out of the loop, or the false sentence you fed the graph. Something there will eventually go wrong: a case the AI misses, a bad suggestion nobody catches. Name the one person who would find out first, and how long it would take them. Was that person in the room when the decision got made? That is the question Chapter 4 keeps asking, and it's the one to carry into the writeup.

    In class: Run the five questions on your own choices

    Not on MATRIX in general. On the graph you built in section 1 and the four settings you chose in section 2. Pairs, about ten minutes.

    1

    What is the AI system, and what does it do? Name the two you ran today and their kind. For the graph, run the five parts: the data (the sentences, and what they leave out), the model (the rule for walking the arrows), the output, the feedback loop (what happens to its answers when someone adds a sentence), and the humans (you).

    2

    Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who benefits when an old drug turns out to have a new use, like minoxidil, and who benefits from each setting you chose on the gate?

    3

    What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Think of the coffee-for-insomnia answer, and the card you set to out of the loop. For each, who would ever notice a failure there?

    4

    Who's saying that, and how would we know? Which of your four settings rests on a real trial, and which on a guess? For the ones no trial tested, whose number would you be trusting, and who has checked it from outside? What evidence would change your least certain one?

    5

    Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Pick one of your settings and defend it with its price and the failure it accepts.

    For homework: The writeup

    Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, October 16. Answer them as you go today. Written reflections, no screenshots.

    1. The knowledge graph. In your own words, explain what a knowledge graph is and how yours answered a question. Use two examples from today: the question where following the arrows gave a bad answer and reading the words fixed it, and what happened to the hair-loss answer when you removed one sentence.
    2. The gate. List your final four settings and how many of your tiles were striped. What did you give up to stay inside seven shifts? Then one sentence on the 2 a.m. card: its only measured setting is the one where the AI got two in three wrong. What would you do with it, and what would that cost?
    3. The talk. The one setting you'd change after hearing the discussion in section 5, and why.
    4. Your sentences. The true sentence and the false sentence you fed it, and what it did with each. Then one sentence on what that means for a system like MATRIX reading millions of sentences nobody checks one by one.
    5. Your commitment card. For which one medical task would you accept a person out of the loop, what would you need to see first, and who answers when it's wrong? Three sentences. Chapter 7 asks this again about a car.
    Using AI on this writeup

    Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

    How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

    Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

    Sources & verification

    This lab's own outside claims have not yet had a dated verification pass; the gate cards' links and the figures on them are the first things to check. Everything else comes from Chapter 4, which carries its own dated note.

    The knowledge graph (section 1)
    Self-contained, no network calls, nothing persists. The fourteen facts are standard pharmacology, simplified to one line each: diphenhydramine (Benadryl) is an antihistamine, and brain histamine promotes wakefulness, which is why it causes drowsiness and is the active ingredient in sleep aids such as ZzzQuil; caffeine blocks adenosine receptors; minoxidil is a blood-pressure drug that widens blood vessels, and hair growth seen in patients taking it led to its use for hair loss (Rogaine); ibuprofen reduces inflammation and can irritate the stomach. The plus-and-minus reading is a teaching simplification, not how MATRIX scores. No links yet; check each line against the drug's label before the next term.

    No model. Each card's results are written from its linked source. The seven-shift budget and each setting's shift cost are invented to force a trade; only the roughly half-cost of on the loop for mammograms echoes a real figure, MASAI's 44 percent reduction in reading work.
