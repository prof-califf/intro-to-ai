About this book

A free, open textbook for MIS 201, Intro to AI in Business, at Western Washington University. Twelve chapters, nine labs, twelve real AI systems, and no cost to the student.

1

Who it is for

Anyone who will live with AI rather than build it. This is a 200-level general education course, open to any major, so the room is mixed on purpose. No technical background is needed and none is assumed.

You do not have to like AI to do well in this course. The approach here comes from Sam Illingworth, who writes about critical AI literacy: most courses teach you how to write a better prompt, and a critical one asks whether you should be writing the prompt at all. Knowing when not to use AI is the harder skill and the one nobody teaches. It also takes inspiration from technology ethicist Tristan Harris and his narrow path: not letting it rip, not locking it down, but matching power with responsibility one system at a time.

The book is written in plain language on purpose: short sentences, few idioms, every technical term defined in the sentence where it first appears.

2

What it argues

This book is not pro-AI and it is not anti-AI. It is pro-thinking critically about AI, and "critical" does not mean negative. It means looking closely instead of taking anyone's word for it: not the companies', not the critics', and not this book's.

Every chapter starts from the same place. "Is AI good or bad" is a question nobody can answer, because AI is not one thing: a spam filter and a robotaxi have almost nothing in common. What you can answer is a question about one AI system at a time: what is it, why does it exist and who benefits, what happens when it fails and who finds out, who is telling you that and how would you know, and should it be kept, fixed, or dropped, and at what cost. Those are the five critical AI questions, and they are the only thing in this book you have to memorize.

The second argument is quieter. Almost every failure in these twelve chapters was visible to someone before it mattered, and the difference between the good outcomes and the bad ones is usually whether anybody was looking. That is a claim about people and organizations, not about machines.

3

The road through the quarter

Twelve chapters, one real AI system at the center of each, in an order that builds.

Getting your footing (Weeks 2 and 3). Chapter 1 is what AI actually is, told through five moments, and hands you the five questions. Chapter 2 asks what it means that these systems seem human, with a therapy chatbot that worked. Chapter 3 is the AI you already live inside: recommendation systems, and Spotify Wrapped.

What it costs and what it can do (Weeks 4 and 5). Chapter 4 is medicine, and a nonprofit finding cures already sitting on the pharmacy shelf. Chapter 5 is the physical bill: energy, water, and the disclosure nobody wants to publish.

Who it decides about (Weeks 6 and 7). Chapter 6 is bias in hiring and lending, where a system can beat the humans it replaced and still discriminate. Chapter 7 is robotaxis, and who gets to keep the track record.

The hardest cases (Weeks 8 and 9). Chapter 8 is ethics and war, taught in Veterans Day week. Chapter 9 is the economy: where the boom is, and what is happening to entry-level work.

Who decides (Week 10). Chapter 10 is truth, and why verification is expensive when generation is free. Chapter 11 is who makes the rules, with license-plate cameras in Bellingham. Chapter 12 is the future, and it comes last because by then you can tell a scenario from a sales pitch.

If you take one thing from the map: the first half is about understanding systems, and the second half is about who is accountable for them.

4

How to use it

Every chapter follows the same shape. Learning objectives, then the concepts with a diagram for each, then a short try-it quiz, then the main case run through the five questions, then how to make that system more beneficial, then key terms and questions for Monday's discussion.

  • Read the chapter over the weekend. Monday is the case and the discussion, and both assume you have read it.
  • Do the try-it boxes as you read. None of them is graded. All of them are the kind of question the weekly Canvas quizzes ask.
  • Treat the discussion questions as positions, not lookups. None can be answered from the text alone. That is deliberate. Feel free to use AI to help sharpen your thinking before class; what you say in the room is yours.
  • The labs are where the ideas become something you did. Everything runs in the browser. Nothing installs, nothing saves, and you are never required to use an AI tool to finish one.
  • Every page prints. Use the print button at the top of any chapter and choose Save as PDF. The navigation and the interactive parts drop out; the try-it answers print open, so print after you have tried them.

How I used AI to write this

A book that asks you to disclose how you use AI should be honest about its own use of it. I used AI here in three ways: to research, chasing sources and checking figures; to draft, turning my outlines, notes, and lectures into first versions of sections that I then rewrote; and to edit, as a reader that never tires of going over a paragraph. The cases are based on real-life examples that I researched, and every argument and every judgment about what matters is mine. Many of the sentences began as the machine's and ended as mine, and where a phrase of its earned its place I kept it. The thinking I did myself, and that is what this course asks of you too.

Two things I owe you beyond that, because this book is about AI and not merely written with it.

The conflict of interest. The model I used is Claude, made by Anthropic, and Anthropic is a company that appears in this book's cases, including Chapter 1's. That is a conflict of interest, plainly stated. You are allowed to hold it against the book, and you should read the passages about Anthropic more skeptically than the rest.

How the claims were checked. This book asks you to check claims, so here is how its own were checked: every load-bearing fact is linked to a source I went and read, and every chapter records the date its facts were verified. An AI model will produce a confident sentence about a study that does not exist. That is Chapter 10's entire subject, and it is the reason the verification is mine rather than the machine's.

Where you find an error, tell me. Corrections are course content, not embarrassment, and I will say what changed and when.
