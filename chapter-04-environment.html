The Machine Has a Body

Every prompt we send runs somewhere physical: a building full of hot chips, drawing power from a real grid, cooled by real water, next to a real town. This chapter follows the answer back to the building — and to the strangest fact of all: nobody will tell us exactly what it costs.

1

Where the answer comes from

The cloud is a marketing word for other people's buildings. When any of us sends a prompt, it travels to a data center — a warehouse of specialized chips running hot enough that cooling them is an industrial engineering problem of its own. The response feels weightless. It isn't. It has a wattage, a water bill, and a street address.

Most of this book asks what AI systems do to people. This chapter asks what running them does to everything else — and, just as important for our critical training, why that question turns out to be so strangely hard to answer.

2

The case: the buildout

The AI industry is in the middle of one of the largest infrastructure buildouts in history. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, and OpenAI are collectively committing hundreds of billions of dollars to new data centers — spending on a scale usually associated with national highway systems, not software companies. The energy appetite is reshaping the power industry itself: Microsoft signed a deal to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island — yes, that Three Mile Island — to feed its data centers, and Google has agreements with Kairos Power to build small nuclear reactors for the same purpose. When software companies start commissioning nuclear plants, the machine has stopped being a metaphor.

The buildout also has neighbors. Data centers cluster where land and power are cheap, and the surrounding communities inherit the load: strained grids, rising electricity rates, and water draws in places that don't have water to spare. The costs of the boom don't stay on the companies' books — some flow outward onto people who never sent a prompt. Economists call that an externality, and it's a concept we'll reuse in half the chapters ahead.

Externality

A cost (or benefit) of an activity that lands on someone who wasn't part of the transaction. Your prompt is free to you and profitable to the provider; the neighboring town's higher power bill is an externality. Once you have the word, you'll see the pattern everywhere in this course.

3

Two costs: training and inference

To think clearly about AI's footprint, split it into its two very different pieces.

Training is the one-time (per model) cost of teaching the system — weeks or months of enormous compute, an energy bill that for frontier models runs into the gigawatt-hours. It's a big number, but it's paid once and then amortized across everything the model ever does.

Inference is the cost of answering — every single prompt, from every single user, forever. Each individual query is tiny: current public estimates put a typical text prompt on the order of a few tenths of a watt-hour, with a few drops of water for cooling. The number that matters isn't the query. It's the multiplication: hundreds of millions of daily users, dozens of queries each, every day, growing. Small × billions × forever is how inference quietly overtakes training as the real bill.

Training vs. inference · right-sizing · waste factor

Training: the one-time cost of building the model. Inference: the recurring cost of every answer. Right-sizing means matching the model to the task — a small model for simple jobs costs a fraction of a frontier model. Waste factor is everything we run that we didn't need: the regenerated answer we didn't read, the giant model summoned to fix a comma.

This framing also tells us where the real leverage is — and it mostly isn't personal guilt. An individual's yearly chatbot use plausibly costs less energy than a few hot showers. The decisions that move gigawatts are made elsewhere: which model a company deploys by default, whether products are designed to invoke AI on every keystroke, where the data centers get built and what feeds them. We'll still audit our own use in the lab — but as evidence for judging the system, not as a shame exercise.

4

The disclosure gap

Now the part of this chapter that trains the skill we keep sharpening. Try to find the actual per-query energy cost of the AI product you use most. Go ahead — the lab will send you looking. What you'll find is a fog: a couple of companies have published carefully framed per-prompt estimates, researchers publish independent estimates that differ from the companies' and from each other, and for most products there is simply no number at all. Estimates for the same basic question can differ by an order of magnitude.

Why? Because disclosure is voluntary, the accounting rules are unsettled, and the companies hold the data. What counts — just the chips, or the cooling too? The idle servers waiting for peak load? Measured on which model, at what length of prompt? Every one of those choices moves the number, and the parties making the choices have preferences about what the number should look like. This is the disclosure gap, and it should feel familiar: in Chapter 3 we saw that a graph can only predict what research recorded. Here, the public can only judge what companies disclose — and the record has a shape someone chose.

The sentence to carry out of this chapter

The environmental debate about AI is not mostly a debate about facts. It's a debate conducted in the absence of facts that specific parties could publish and don't. When we see wildly conflicting claims — "AI is boiling the oceans" vs. "a prompt is nothing" — the critical move isn't picking a side. It's asking who benefits from the fog.

5

Question it & decide

The Monday questions, applied
1

What kind of AI is this? All of it — this chapter is about the substrate every kind runs on. But note that model size varies enormously, which means the footprint of "AI" isn't one number. That's what right-sizing exploits.

2

Where is the human? Far from the machine. The people who bear the externalities — the town next to the data center — were never in any loop at all. Ask what mechanism could put them in one.

3

Who is accountable when it fails? When a grid strains or a water table drops, the chain runs through the AI company, the utility, and the local officials who approved the deal. Who among them answered to the residents?

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Environmental costs surface slowly, in utility bills and water reports, long after the ribbon-cutting. And the disclosure gap means even then, attribution is contestable. This is our clearest case yet of a failure designed to be hard to see.

Deciding here means resisting the two easy exits. Exit one: "AI is an environmental catastrophe, log off" — which doesn't survive the actual per-query numbers or an honest comparison to the rest of our digital lives. Exit two: "a prompt is a rounding error, nothing to see" — which doesn't survive the multiplication, the buildout, or the neighbors' power bills. The defensible position lives in the middle and has two parts: right-size what we control, and demand disclosure for what we don't. The lab makes both concrete.

6

Key terms

Data center

The physical building where AI runs: chips, power, cooling, and a street address with neighbors.

Training vs. inference

The one-time cost of building a model vs. the forever cost of every answer. Inference wins the long game through multiplication.

Externality

A cost that lands on someone outside the transaction — the course's most reusable economics concept.

Right-sizing

Matching model size to task. The biggest efficiency lever that product designers — and users — actually hold.

Waste factor

Compute spent on outputs nobody needed: unread regenerations, oversized models on trivial tasks.

Disclosure gap

The absence of standardized, published footprint data — a fog with beneficiaries. The reason honest estimates disagree by 10×.
