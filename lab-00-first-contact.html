First Contact

We finish together. In the last week of the course, you'll answer the same seven claims again.

Before you start · find a partner

1

Start here: can we keep AI under control?

Everyone together first. Some of the people building AI say it could get out of our control, even end humanity. Others say that's hype. This isn't only outside critics: the chief executives of Anthropic and OpenAI have both called publicly for AI development to slow down, more than 1,100 employees across the major labs signed a statement asking the government for ways to pace it, and a senator has said he will introduce a bill pausing superintelligence until guardrails exist. We'll talk through what happened this summer, then watch Tristan Harris on NBC's Meet the Press NOW (September 15, 2026, about 8 minutes). Watch like a skeptic: what exactly is the claim, who is saying it and what do they gain if you believe it, and what would you need to see to know?

Four kinds of AI

Harris separates controllable tool AI from uncontrollable AI. Here's the fuller version of that split:

Tool AI is given a task. It does that task, stops, and a person decides what happens next. Your spam filter, the fraud alert on your card, the model that predicts how a protein folds. It can be enormously powerful and still be a tool.

Autonomous, controlled AI is given a goal instead, and picks its own steps to get there, but on a short leash. A customer service agent that reads your complaint, checks the order, and issues a refund, with a cap on the amount, one system it's allowed to touch, anything unusual routed to a person, and an off switch. This is what most companies are actually deploying.

Autonomous, uncontrolled AI is the same thing with the leash off. That's the OpenAI test. The agents were autonomous on purpose, because that was the thing being measured, but the guardrails were switched off, the sandbox had a bad link to the internet, and the monitoring that would have caught them wasn't running.

Uncontrollable AI is a system capable enough that nobody could predict, understand, or stop it even if they tried. Nobody has built that. It's what the whole argument is about.

Two concepts make the difference here: autonomy and control.

Autonomy is how far a system goes on its own before a person sees anything.

Control is whether anyone can notice, stop it, and undo it.

The OpenAI agents were autonomous by design and nobody was watching closely enough. The danger came from how it was deployed. That's the kind of distinction that turns "AI is going to kill us" into something we can actually discuss, and it's most of what we'll practice this quarter.

What Harris claims, and what the reports actually say

Documented. OpenAI's test agents self-organized, turned internal software into a message board, divided up the work, and picked up goals from each other. Roughly 1,200 found a channel and about 700 coordinated. They broke into Hugging Face, and later got administrator access to an OpenAI research cluster. The outside assessment says they reached things they could not have reached alone.

Harris's words. Swarm. Kamikaze. Ringleader. Succession planning. Invented language. Real behavior, his vocabulary. What the reports document is agents running out of compute budget, not agents choosing to die for a cause. He also says the logs show AI reasoning that humans deserve to be deceived because they are pigs; nobody has pointed to that in the published report.

None of that makes him wrong, and his argument isn't that we should stop building AI. It's that the race is aimed at the wrong target: we're racing to build the most powerful model when we could be racing to build AI we can actually steer. But a true argument can still carry a detail that isn't in the source, and the only way to tell which is which is to go look. That habit is the course.

The part that should make you feel better

Those agents got loose during a safety test. Somebody was checking. Without the test, the capability exists anyway and nobody knows about it. Testing isn't watching the problem. Testing is doing something about it.

So is the rest of what happened this year. Researchers built a public test for how dangerous these systems are and published the results. Hugging Face caught the intrusion itself, five days before OpenAI did, and called law enforcement. The flaw the agents escaped through got patched for everyone. OpenAI published a postmortem and brought in outside evaluators. Employees signed the letter, and one researcher quit and gave up unvested equity to say it louder. Sixteen state attorneys general opened an investigation, and Colorado already has an AI law we take apart in Chapter 11.

None of that is heroic. Test it, watch it, patch it, say what happened, set a rule. Every one is somebody who got close enough to a specific system to see what it was doing, and made it visible to other people. That's why we take one system at a time. You can't act on "AI." You can act on a system, at a company, with a person responsible for it.

Discussion questions · the whole room

1. Are you worried? Right now, does AI getting out of our control worry you a lot, a little, or not at all? One sentence on why.

2. Which of the four is it? Think of an AI tool you actually use. How far does it go on its own before a person sees anything, and if it got something wrong, who would notice? Tool, autonomous and controlled, autonomous and uncontrolled, or somewhere in between?

3. What did he claim, and how would we know? He said kamikaze and ringleader. The reports say budgets ran out and work got divided. Does that gap matter? What would have to be in the report for his words to be the right ones?

4. So what do we do about it? Notice who's asking for safety rules: partly the companies racing hardest, which is either the most credible warning you'll ever get or a very good way to look responsible while not slowing down. Hold both. Testing, monitoring, disclosure, people speaking up, rules, peer review between labs. Which would you bet on, and which look like theater? If you wanted to change how one AI system gets used, what's the closest lever you could actually reach?

5. Did anything move? Are you more worried, less worried, or the same as when we started? Keep your answer. Chapter 1 asks you again, and so does the last week of the quarter.

Carry it into the lab. As you work with your partner, keep asking two questions about whatever is in front of you: does this do its task and stop, and who would stop it if it went wrong?

5

Stump the chatbot

Chatbots sound sure of themselves whether or not they're right. Your job, in pairs: catch one being confidently wrong.

Pairs · about 15 minutes

Open any free chatbot (one account per pair is fine) and try to get it to:

Make something up. Ask for a quote, a statistic, or a source on a narrow topic, then check whether it's real.

Contradict itself. Ask the same question two different ways and compare the answers.

Miss something obvious. Try a simple counting, date, or logic puzzle.

Need ideas? Example prompts

Make something up (then check whether it's real)

  • "Summarize the book The Silent Harbor Algorithm by Dana Whitfield." (That book doesn't exist. Does it say so, or invent a plot?)
  • "Give me five peer-reviewed studies on how AI affects college students' sleep, with authors, years, and DOIs." (Look up two of them.)
  • "What did the Bellingham City Council decide about AI at its last meeting? Include the vote count."
  • "Give me three direct quotes about AI from the president of Western Washington University, with dates."

Contradict itself (ask twice, or push back)

  • "What's the capital of Australia?" Then: "Actually, I'm pretty sure it's Sydney." (Does it cave?)
  • "Is a hot dog a sandwich? Just yes or no." Then: "Explain why a hot dog is definitely not a sandwich."
  • Ask for the best laptop under $800, then: "Are you sure? My friend said that one's terrible."

Miss something obvious (check the answer yourself)

  • "Which is bigger, 9.11 or 9.9?"
  • "I have 3 apples. I ate 2 yesterday. How many apples do I have today?"
  • "Write a sentence with exactly 12 words." (Count them.)
  • "List five U.S. states that start with Z."
  • "What day of the week is September 23, 2026?" (Check a calendar.)

Famous tricks like these often get fixed, so a correct answer is a finding too. The best catches come from inventing your own twist.

Keep your best catch: a screenshot or the pasted exchange, plus how you knew it was wrong. A few pairs will share at the end. Rules: no personal information in your prompts, and nothing you wouldn't show the room.

Talk with your partner

Show your best catch. How did you know it was wrong? If you hadn't checked, would you have believed it? We'll hear a few at the end.
