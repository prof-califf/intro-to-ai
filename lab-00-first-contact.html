First Contact

Nothing to read before class. We start with the biggest question in the headlines: can we keep AI under control? Then, with a partner, you'll guess which everyday things are secretly AI, call eight claims on instinct, try to catch a chatbot being confidently wrong, and browse what real people are building with AI. We finish together. In the last week of the course, you'll answer the same eight claims again.

one big question · AI or not · eight claims · what people are building

Before you start · find a partner

Sit next to someone you don't know yet and introduce yourself: your name, your major or what you're thinking about, and one AI tool you've used lately. You'll work with this partner for the rest of today.

1

Start here: can we keep AI under control?

Everyone together first. Some of the people building AI say it could get out of our control, even end humanity. Others say that's hype. This isn't only outside critics. In July, more than 1,100 employees across the major labs signed a statement asking the government for ways to pace AI development. Two weeks ago, the heads of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and xAI all said publicly that the pace should slow. The same day, a senator announced a bill to pause it. None of this is history yet; most of it happened while you were registering for this class. We'll talk through it, then watch Tristan Harris on NBC's Meet the Press NOW (September 15, 2026, about 8 minutes). Watch like a skeptic: what exactly is the claim, who is saying it and what do they gain if you believe it, and what would you need to see to know?

Four kinds of AI

Harris separates controllable tool AI from uncontrollable AI. Here's the fuller version of that split:

Controllable Tool AI is given a task. It does that task, stops, and a person decides what happens next. Your spam filter, the fraud alert on your card, the model that predicts how a protein folds. Most generative AI right now. It can be enormously powerful and still be a tool.

Autonomous, controlled AI is given a goal instead, and picks its own steps to get there, but on a short leash. A customer service agent that reads your complaint, checks the order, and issues a refund, with a cap on the amount, one system it's allowed to touch, anything unusual routed to a person, and an off switch. This is what most companies are actually deploying.

Autonomous, uncontrolled AI is the same thing with the leash off. That's the OpenAI test. The agents were autonomous on purpose, because that was the thing being measured, but the guardrails were switched off, the sandbox had a bad link to the internet, and the monitoring that would have caught them wasn't running.

Uncontrollable AI is a system capable enough that nobody could predict, understand, or stop it even if they tried. Nobody has built that. The OpenAI case matters because it's the closest anyone has come, and because it shows how much of the risk comes from how a system is governed rather than from the AI itself.

Two concepts make the difference here: autonomy and control.

Autonomy is how far a system goes on its own before a person sees anything.

Control is whether anyone can notice, stop it, and undo it.

The OpenAI agents were autonomous by design and nobody was watching closely enough. The danger came from how it was deployed. That's the kind of distinction that turns "AI is going to kill us" into something we can actually discuss, and it's most of what we'll practice this quarter.

Let's pause and think: what we can do now

Those agents got loose during a safety test. Somebody was checking. Without the test, the capability exists anyway and nobody knows about it. Testing isn't watching the problem. Testing is doing something about it.

So is the rest of what happened this year. Researchers built a public test for how dangerous these systems are and published the results. Hugging Face caught the intrusion itself, five days before OpenAI did, and called law enforcement. The flaw the agents escaped through got patched for everyone. OpenAI published a postmortem and brought in outside evaluators. Employees signed the letter, and one researcher quit and gave up unvested equity to say it louder. Sixteen state attorneys general opened an investigation, and Colorado already has an AI law we take apart in Chapter 11.

None of that is heroic. Test it, watch it, patch it, say what happened, set a rule. Every one is somebody who got close enough to a specific system to see what it was doing, and made it visible to other people. That's why we take one system at a time. You can't act on "AI." You can act on a system, at a company, with a person responsible for it.

Discussion questions · the whole room

1. Are you worried? Right now, does AI getting out of our control worry you a lot, a little, or not at all? One sentence on why.

2. Which kind is it? Think of an AI tool you've used, or one you've seen a friend, a family member, or a company use. How far does it go on its own before a person sees anything, and if it got something wrong, who would notice? Controllable tool AI, autonomous and controlled, autonomous and uncontrolled, or somewhere in between?

3. Who is telling us this, and how would we know? Harris runs an organization built on this warning. The researchers who quit worked inside the companies. The CEOs asking for rules also sell the product. None of that makes any of them wrong. So what would you need to see, not hear, before you believed any of them?

4. So what can we actually do? Look back at the list above: testing, monitoring, patching, disclosure, people speaking up, governments writing rules. Which one do you think does the most good? Which one sounds good but probably changes nothing?

5. Did anything change? Are you more worried, less worried, or about the same as you were before class? It's okay to be worried. Keep your answer. Chapter 1 asks you again, and so does the last week of the quarter.

Carry it into the lab. As you work with your partner, keep asking two questions about whatever is in front of you: does this do its task and stop, and who would stop it if it went wrong?

4

Talk it out

Now pair up with the person next to you and compare your eight answers. Don't change them, just talk through where you disagreed.

Pairs · about six minutes · before the reveal

1. Find the two claims you disagreed on most. Each of you gives one reason, in one sentence. No winning, just the reasons.

2. Pick the one claim you most want to be false. Say why in one sentence. Write it down; we'll collect these on the board.

3. For that claim: what would you need to see, not hear, to believe it? A document, a number, a person, a demo? Be specific.

Then press reveal. Read your result, then read the room's.

6

Explore the AI Workflow Hub

Everything so far today has been about systems somebody else built and somebody else decided about. This part is a live feed of workflows real people built with AI, for real work, this week. Your final project grows out of this, so keep an eye out for ideas.

The Hub · about 15 minutes · with your partner

Before you open it, write one sentence with your partner: what do you think people are actually using AI for at work this week? Be specific enough to be wrong. You will check it against the feed in a minute.

Now open the Rundown AI Workflow Hub and browse until you find two workflows. Then note the following:

One you'd actually use. Not "cool." Use. For your classes, your job, your side hustle, this week.

One that made you hesitate. Something in it felt off, and you may not have words for why yet. That hesitation is worth keeping.

For each one, write down three things: what it does in one sentence, where the human is in it, and what you'd want to check before trusting it.

Then talk with your partner. Is the workflow you'd use a tool that does its job and stops, or is it handed a goal and left to pick its own steps? Who, if anyone, double-checks its work? Be ready to tell the class about one of them at the end, and to say whether your sentence from before you opened the feed was close.

7

Come together

Everyone back in. A few voices on each.

Wrap-up discussion · about 12 minutes

1. Hidden AI. Which "AI or not?" answer surprised you most?

2. The claims. Which claim split your group, and what would you need to see to believe it?

3. Use it or skip it. Tell us about one Hub workflow: what it does, who would use it, whether you'd trust it, and who checks its work.

4. Back to the start. After today, does AI getting out of our control worry you more, less, or the same? What changed, if anything?

Every question you asked today is one of the five critical AI questions this course is built on. On Monday, we name them and run them on the real incident behind the headlines: OpenAI's agents that broke out of their test.
