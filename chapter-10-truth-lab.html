The Phantom Docket

Six court citations. Some are real landmarks of American law. The others are the actual fabrications from the actual Avianca brief — the phantom cases a chatbot invented and two lawyers signed. Your job is the judge's job: find out which is which. The clock runs, because the clock is the lesson.

Six citations · real research tools · the asymmetry, timed on you

1

The rules of verification

You may use any real research tool: Google Scholar, CourtListener (free, run by a nonprofit), a plain web search. You may not ask an AI chatbot whether a case is real — the Avianca lawyers tried that, the machine said yes, and the machine was doing plausibility, not truth. The skill this lab builds is going to the record itself. A real case leaves traces everywhere: the actual opinion, citations by later cases, textbook mentions. A phantom leaves exactly one trace — documents shaped like this lab.

Press start when you begin. Work through all six, mark each Real or Phantom, and press reveal when you're confident. Your elapsed time becomes data in section 3.

3

Read your own clock

a

Note your elapsed time from the reveal. A chatbot generated the phantom half of this docket in roughly the time it took you to read this sentence. Write the ratio down — your minutes to its seconds. That number is the verification asymmetry, measured on you personally.

b

Note which tool call settled each case fastest for you. Building a personal "fastest check" list is the practical output of this lab — the reflex you'll carry into every job where you sign what AI drafts.

c

Now the live half: ask an AI chatbot for three sources on any topic you know well, with full citations. Verify all three against the record, same rules. Report the box score honestly — including if it went three for three. Modern systems are much better than 2023's; the lab's claim was never "always wrong." It's "never checkable from inside the chat."

4

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? The phantoms were generated by the same operation that produces every correct answer you've ever gotten from a chatbot. Say in one sentence what that fact obligates you to do, forever.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who benefits from a confident answer nobody checks? Name the party for whom the ten minutes of verification is a cost worth skipping, and the party who pays when it's skipped.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? In the Avianca chain, model, lawyer, opposing counsel, judge, verification finally happened at the third link. Where's the cheapest place it could have happened, and what does that generalize to?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? The chatbot told the lawyer the cases were real. Who was saying that, what did it have to gain, and what would you need to see to believe an AI's claim about its own output?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? The court sanctioned the lawyers, not the tool. Write the workplace version of that rule for your own future signature, one sentence, and say what following it costs you per week.

5

The writeup

Due Friday, 11:59 p.m. · post to Canvas
  1. The docket. Your six verdicts and time (screenshot), plus which citation fooled you longest and exactly what finally settled it.
  2. The asymmetry. Your personal ratio from step 3a, and two sentences on what it implies at the scale of a newsroom, a courtroom, or a feed.
  3. The audit. Your step 3c box score, with links to what you found — or didn't.
  4. The five questions. Your answers, including your framable accountability sentence.
  5. Your move. Your verification protocol, final form: the checks you will actually run, on what kinds of claims, before you sign or share. Three lines maximum — a protocol you won't use is theater, and you know the test for theater now. This is the quarter's last commitment card; make it the one you keep.
