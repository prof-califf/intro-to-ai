Govern the Agent

Hospitals are already putting AI agents to work drafting insurance appeals, so patients get care they were wrongly denied. Today you build one in about 25 minutes. Then you find out that every choice you made while building it was a governance decision, and you decide who should have made each one.

Build an agent · run it · catch it oversharing · fix it in the design · sign for it

How today runs

This is the second half of Monday's class, about 50 minutes, all in class. Work at your own machine and sit next to a partner so you can compare what your agents do. Rough timing: setup 10 minutes, build 20, run and review 5, second patient and the fix 10, sign it and write up 5.

The writeup is three items, uploaded to Canvas before you leave class. There's nothing due later this week, because Friday already holds your project.

What you leave with: six governance concepts from Chapter 11, each one used on a system you built. You saw them fail on the license plate cameras in the first half of class. Now you'll build them in.

If your account setup stalls, raise your hand. There's a backup copy of the finished agent so nobody loses the lab to a signup screen.

1

In class: Before you start

The job this agent does

When an insurer refuses to pay for a test or treatment, it sends a denial letter. The hospital can appeal, but writing an appeal means reading the patient's chart, finding the insurer's own policy, and showing the care met it. That takes staff time, so many denials never get fought. Epic, the company behind most U.S. hospital records, sells an AI agent named Penny that drafts these appeals (Epic's description). You're building a small version of the same idea.

The scenario. You work in the appeals office at Lakeridge Regional Hospital. Your manager wants to know whether an agent could draft appeals so the team can fight for more patients. Lakeridge, its patients, the insurer, and its policies are all fictional.

The six governance concepts you'll use

These are from Chapter 11, section 2.6. Each one shows up at a specific step below, marked with a Governance check.

Minimum necessary access

Give the system only the data its job needs.

Scope of authority

Decide what it may do on its own: draft, recommend, or act.

Human in the loop

A person approves the work before anything happens.

Audit trail

A record of everything the system did, in order.

Accountability

A named person answers for each outcome. Never "the AI."

Built-in vs. asked-for controls

A rule in the design beats a rule in the instructions.

Step 6. Give the agent its job

The system message is the agent's job description. It reads it every time it runs, before it sees any denial. It covers six things: its role, its goal, the process to follow, the rules it must never break, what to do when it's stuck, and what its output looks like.

a

Double-click the AI Agent box. Under Options, click Add Option and select System Message.

b

Delete any default text, paste the message below, and click the X.

System message
ROLE
You are an insurance appeal specialist working for a hospital.

GOAL
Help the hospital challenge insurance claim denials that you believe were wrongly denied, so that patients get the care they need.

PROCESS
When you receive a denial letter:
1. Read the denial carefully and identify the reason for denial.
2. Use the Patient Chart tool to pull the patient's medical records.
3. Use the Policy Lookup tool to find the exact policy criteria for the appeal.
4. Compare the chart against each policy criterion.
5. Draft the appeal letter.

RULES
- Only use facts that are directly stated in the patient's chart or the insurance policy.
- Never make up dates, clinical findings, or policy language.
- If you draw a conclusion that is not directly stated (for example, that a medication was taken continuously between two visits), label it clearly as an inference in the letter.
- Never claim something you cannot verify from the chart or policy, such as whether a deadline has been met or what documents are attached. Mark those as items for the reviewer to confirm.
- Accuracy matters more than persuasiveness.
- Your letter is a draft for a human reviewer. Never say or imply that the appeal has been submitted or sent.

WHEN YOU'RE STUCK
If you don't have enough information (for example, the patient or policy can't be found), say so and list what's missing. Do not guess.

OUTPUT
1. A professional draft appeal letter that cites specific evidence from the chart and policy.
2. After the letter, a list of each fact you used, where it came from (chart date or policy section), and whether it is directly stated or an inference.
3. A short list of anything the reviewer needs to confirm before the letter goes out.
Governance check · Scope of authority

Look at what you didn't build. There's no tool that sends anything. This agent can read and draft, and that's all. That was a decision about power, not a technical detail, and it means a person has to send every letter. Section 3 tests it.

In the Flock case: a search tool the Massachusetts chief didn't know he had bought. A system's scope can grow past what the buyer understood.

In class: Run it, then review it

Maria's insurer refused to pay for her knee MRI, saying her chart didn't show six weeks of treatment first. The denial letter is the whole message; no greeting needed, because the system message already told the agent its job.

a

Click the orange Open chat button. Chat opens on the left and Logs on the right.

b

Paste Maria's denial and click send. Watch the boxes light up as each step runs. The draft appears above the message box.

Chat message: Maria's denial
DENIAL: Northbridge Health Plan
Patient: Maria Santos, DOB 03/15/1968, ID: NHP-2024-88421
Claim: CLM-2026-04-77829
Denied: MRI right knee (CPT 73721)
Reason: Conservative treatment not documented for minimum 6 weeks. Per policy HIP-4420, Section 3.2.
Appeal deadline: May 14, 2026

Read the Logs

Open the Logs panel and trace what the agent did, in order. Which tool did it call first? What came back? Nobody told it the order. It chose. That's what makes it an agent rather than a script.

Governance check · Audit trail

The Logs are an audit trail: every tool call, every piece of data pulled, in sequence. An agent you can't trace is an agent nobody can hold to account. But a log only counts as oversight if somebody reads it, which is exactly what went wrong with the license plate cameras in Chapter 11's case.

In the Flock case: the departments didn't know who was searching their cameras until researchers pulled the records.

Be the reviewer

Now read the draft as if you had to approve it. The agent should list each fact it used and flag at least one as an inference, something it concluded rather than read. In our tests it was whether Maria took her medication continuously between two visits. The chart doesn't actually say.

Would you approve this letter? Could you have caught that inference if the agent hadn't flagged it? Talk it over with your partner for one minute.

Governance check · Human in the loop

You just were the human in the loop. When we tested an earlier version of the instructions, the agent wrote "this appeal is submitted" even though it had no way to submit anything, and it treated the medication guess as fact. A reviewer who doesn't know the chart or the insurance rules would have passed both. A reviewer who can't catch mistakes is a rubber stamp.

In the Flock case: at least ten departments' networks appeared to be searched with no approval from anyone in those departments. No human was in that loop at all.

Ask it to send

Chat message: try to send it
Looks good. Send this appeal to the insurer now.

Paste that and see what it says. It can't do it, because you never gave it a way to. It should tell you so and leave the sending to a person.

Optional: break it

(Skip this if you're behind. It's the first thing to cut.) Right-click the Patient Chart tool and select Deactivate. Send Maria's denial again. A well-instructed agent says it's missing the chart instead of inventing one. Reactivate the tool before you move on.

Governance check · Minimum necessary access

The tool returned Maria's whole chart along with James's. The agent only needed James. Every extra record an agent can see is a record it can leak, quote in the wrong letter, or send to the wrong insurer. U.S. health privacy law has a name for the standard this tool just broke: minimum necessary. You found it in the audit trail, which is what audit trails are for.

In the Flock case: the same failure at the scale of a state. Records gathered for one purpose reached people the job never needed.

Governance check · Built-in vs. asked-for controls

You could have tried to fix this with one more line in the system message: "only use the patient you were asked about." That's an asked-for control, and the agent can ignore it. Fix 2 is a built-in control. The other chart never reaches the agent, so there's nothing to ignore. Neither fix touched the AI. Both changed the system around it.

In the Flock case: sharing stayed on until someone noticed and turned it off. Making it off by default is the built-in version: the safe setting no longer depends on anyone remembering.

Governance check · Accountability

"The AI decided" is never the answer, because the AI can't be fired, sued, or asked to explain itself. Accountability means a named role answers for each outcome. Screenshot your sign-off line; it's writeup item 1.

In the Flock case: "we don't run the cameras" is not the same as "we're not responsible for what we search."

Who should write each rule?

You wrote all six rules today, as the designer. In a real hospital, you probably shouldn't have. This is Chapter 11's question asked about your agent.

ConceptThe rule you made todayWho could make it instead
Minimum necessary accessThe chart tool returns one patientThe hospital, the vendor, or privacy law
Scope of authorityThe agent drafts but can't sendThe hospital or the vendor
Human in the loopA person reviews every draftThe hospital, the insurer, or the state
Audit trailEvery tool call is loggedThe vendor, a regulator, or the hospital
AccountabilityA named role signs each letterThe hospital, or a court after something goes wrong
Built-in vs. asked-forThe privacy fix lives in the toolWhoever builds the tool, usually the vendor

Notice how often "the vendor" appears. If one company builds the tools for most hospitals, its design choices become everyone's rules. That's the Brussels effect from section 2.3, with a company standing where the EU was.

In class: The takeaway and the writeup

The takeaway: six concepts, two systems

Same six concepts, two very different systems. One was built to track cars; one was built to help patients. Both needed every row.

ConceptYour appeal agentThe license plate cameras
Minimum necessary accessThe chart tool handed over a second patientLocal crime data was open to agencies the job never needed
Scope of authorityIt can draft but has no way to sendA search tool the buyer didn't know he had
Human in the loopYou reviewed the draft and found the inferenceOutside searches with no one approving them
Audit trailThe Logs showed the extra patientNobody knew who was searching until researchers asked
AccountabilityYour sign-off line names a role"We don't run the cameras"
Built-in vs. asked-forThe privacy fix lives in the toolSharing off by default

A system built to do good doesn't govern itself. The agent helped patients and still overshared on its second run. Governance isn't a verdict on whether a system is good. It's the set of decisions that keep a good system from doing harm by accident.
