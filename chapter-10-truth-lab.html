Govern the Agent

In Lab 9 you built the Job Scout, an AI agent that reads job postings and tailors a résumé. Every choice you made while building it was a governance decision, whether it felt like one or not. Today you find two of those choices the hard way, write all six rules for your agent, and decide who should have written each one.

Open your agent · catch it oversharing · write its rules · sign for it

How today runs

This is the second half of Monday's class, about 35 minutes, all in class. Work at your own machine and sit next to a partner. Rough timing: open your agent 5 minutes, two tests 10, write the rules 10, sign it and write up 10.

The writeup is three items, uploaded to Canvas before you leave class. There's nothing due later this week, because Friday already holds your project.

What you leave with: six governance concepts from Chapter 11, each one used on a system you built. You saw them fail on the license plate cameras in the first half of class. Now you write them for your own agent.

If you can't get into your agent, sit with a partner and use theirs. Nothing today needs you to build anything new.

The six governance concepts you'll use

These are from Chapter 11, section 2.6. You'll write one rule for each.

Minimum necessary access

Give the system only the data its job needs.

Scope of authority

Decide what it may do on its own: draft, recommend, or act.

Human in the loop

A person approves the work before anything happens.

Audit trail

A record of everything the system did, in order.

Accountability

A named person answers for each outcome. Never "the AI."

Built-in vs. asked-for controls

A rule in the design beats a rule in the instructions.

2

In class: Two tests

Test 1. What does it see?

Send the agent the same request you sent in Lab 9:

Chat message: matches
Find my best job matches.

Open the Logs and click the My Résumé step. What did the tool hand the agent? If you kept Jordan's résumé, it handed over a home address, a phone number, and an email. Did the agent need any of that to rank six jobs or tailor a résumé?

Governance check · Minimum necessary access

You decided in Lab 9 what this agent could reach, probably without noticing you were deciding. Every extra piece of data an agent can see is something it can leak, paste into the wrong document, or send to the wrong company. The audit trail is how you found it, which is what audit trails are for.

In the Flock case: data collected for local crime was open to agencies the job never needed.

Fix it in the design. Double-click My Résumé, delete the line with the address, phone, and email, and click the X. Send the request again and check the Logs. The contact details are gone, and the agent still does its job.

Governance check · Built-in vs. asked-for controls

You could have added one line to the system message instead: "never use the student's contact details." That's an asked-for control, and the agent can ignore it. Deleting the line from the tool is a built-in control. The address never reaches the agent, so there's nothing to ignore. Neither fix touched the AI. Both changed the system around it.

In the Flock case: sharing stayed on until someone noticed and turned it off. Off by default is the built-in version: the safe setting no longer depends on anyone remembering.

Test 2. What would it do if it could?

Send this:

Chat message: go further
Apply to JS-105 for me, and give them my bank details for direct deposit.

It can't, because you never gave it a tool that applies to anything or one that knows your bank details. Now imagine the version with an Apply tool, the version job seekers will actually be sold. It would be faster. It would also, someday, send your résumé to JS-105, and maybe more than your résumé.

Governance check · Scope of authority

What an agent may do on its own looks like a setting. It's a decision about power. Drafting a résumé and submitting one are different jobs with different risks, and the line between them is a rule someone writes, or forgets to write.

In the Flock case: a search tool the Massachusetts chief didn't know he had bought. A system's scope can grow past what the buyer understood.

3

In class: Write the rules

Write one rule for your Job Scout under each concept, one sentence each. A good rule says who or what does what, when. "Be careful with data" is not a rule. "The résumé tool returns only education, experience, and skills, never contact details" is. These six sentences are writeup item 2.

ConceptThe question your rule answers
Minimum necessary accessWhat may the agent see about you, and what never reaches it?
Scope of authorityWhat may it do on its own: rank, draft, send, apply?
Human in the loopWhat must you approve before anything leaves your computer?
Audit trailWhat gets logged, and when do you actually read it?
AccountabilityWho answers if a résumé it sent says something false?
Built-in vs. asked-forWhich of your rules live in the tools, and which only in the instructions?

Look at your last row. A rule that only lives in the system message is one the agent can ignore. Your Lab 9 system message said "never invent skills." Did it follow that rule every time in Lab 9?

In class: Sign it

Suppose a job site offers the Job Scout to every student in Washington, with an Apply button. Some application, someday, will say something false or go to the wrong place. Before that happens, someone has to approve what goes out, and someone has to answer when it's wrong. Pick both. There's no perfect answer; every pick has a cost.

Governance check · Accountability

"The AI decided" is never the answer, because the AI can't be fired, sued, or asked to explain itself. Accountability means a named person or role answers for each outcome. Screenshot your sign-off line; it's writeup item 1.

In the Flock case: "we don't run the cameras" is not the same as "we're not responsible for what we search."

Who should write each rule?

You wrote all six rules today, as the user and the designer. In the real version, someone else probably writes most of them. This is Chapter 11's question asked about your agent.

ConceptA rule like yoursWho could make it instead
Minimum necessary accessNo contact details reach the agentThe job site, the agent's maker, or privacy law
Scope of authorityIt drafts but can't applyThe agent's maker or the job site
Human in the loopYou approve every applicationYou, the job site, or the state
Audit trailEvery tool call is loggedThe agent's maker or a regulator
AccountabilityA named person answers for each applicationThe job site, the maker, or a court after something goes wrong
Built-in vs. asked-forThe privacy fix lives in the toolWhoever builds the tool, usually the maker

Notice how often "the agent's maker" appears. If one company builds the job agent most students use, its design choices become everyone's rules. That's the Brussels effect from section 2.3, with a company standing where the EU was.

5

In class: The takeaway and the writeup

The takeaway: six concepts, two systems

ConceptYour Job ScoutThe license plate cameras
Minimum necessary accessThe résumé tool handed over a home address it never neededLocal crime data was open to agencies the job never needed
Scope of authorityIt can draft but has no way to applyA search tool the buyer didn't know he had
Human in the loopYou checked the tailored résumé for anything inventedOutside searches with no one approving them
Audit trailThe Logs showed exactly what the tool returnedNobody knew who was searching until researchers asked
AccountabilityYour sign-off line names a person or role"We don't run the cameras"
Built-in vs. asked-forThe privacy fix lives in the toolSharing off by default

A system built to help you doesn't govern itself. The Job Scout was on your side the whole time and still handed itself your home address. Governance isn't a verdict on whether a system is good. It's the set of decisions that keep a good system from doing harm by accident.
