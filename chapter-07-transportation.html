Transportation: Two Answers to One Question

This week we look at the same industry giving two opposite answers to our second Monday question. In an airline's dispatch center, AI advises and a human decides. On the streets of ten American cities, the human is gone from the driver's seat entirely. Both are running, at scale, right now.

1

Two rides

Ride one: you're on an Alaska Airlines flight. Somewhere in a Seattle operations center, an AI system called Flyways has studied the weather, the traffic, and thousands of past flights, and suggested a better route for your plane. A human dispatcher looked at the suggestion and decided whether to take it. You never knew any of this happened.

Ride two: you're in Phoenix, or San Francisco, or Austin, and you hail a taxi. It arrives with no one in it. The steering wheel turns by itself. There is no human to look at anything, decide anything, or grab anything. You know exactly what's happening — that's the product.

Same industry. Same safety-critical stakes. Opposite designs. This chapter is a controlled experiment that reality ran for us: hold the domain constant, move the human, and watch what changes — in trust, in accountability, and in what failure looks like. By Friday, the lab hands the whole question to you, wearing a city council member's badge.

2

Case one: the advisor in the dispatch center

Airlines route flights through some of the most complicated conditions in business: shifting weather, congested airspace, fuel costs, connection times. Alaska Airlines worked with a company called Air Space Intelligence to build Flyways, an AI system that continuously evaluates routes and flags better options to the airline's human dispatchers — a few minutes saved here, a smoother ride there, fuel not burned. Multiplied across an airline's schedule, small percentages become real money and real emissions — Alaska says tens of thousands of flight hours and roughly a million gallons of fuel a year. And the model just scaled: the FAA recently awarded Air Space Intelligence an $875 million contract to help modernize the nation's entire airspace — the dispatcher's advisor, promoted to the national level, with the same design question riding along.

Notice the design decision, because it's the whole case: Flyways recommends. Licensed human dispatchers accept or reject every suggestion, and the legal responsibility for each flight plan stays with humans. This is predictive AI in the advisor's seat — the same species of system as EveryCure's, pointed at airspace instead of medicine.

And the human story inside it is the honest part. Dispatchers are experienced professionals; when the system first arrived, many didn't trust it, and some suggestions got rejected simply because a machine made them. What changed minds wasn't an explanation of the algorithm — dispatchers can't see inside it any more than we can. What changed minds was a track record. The suggestions kept being good. Trust arrived through results, not understanding. Hold that distinction; it's about to matter enormously.

Results-based vs. understanding-based trust

We trust some things because we understand how they work, and others because they keep working. Almost all trust in AI systems — including the dispatchers', including ours — is results-based. The question this chapter keeps asking: how many good results should it take, and who's keeping the count?

3

Case two: the empty driver's seat

For years, fully self-driving taxis were the technology that was always five years away. Quietly, that stopped being true. Waymo — born as Google's self-driving car project — now operates paid robotaxi service in more than ten U.S. cities, from Phoenix and San Francisco to Austin, Atlanta, Miami, and across Texas, with Denver, Washington D.C., and London announced next. At last count the company reported over 400,000 paid rides per week, on freeways in some cities, to and from major airports. In China, Baidu's Apollo Go and rivals like Pony.ai run their own large fleets. The future arrived on a rolling basis, city by city — which is itself a lesson: transformative technology doesn't switch on everywhere at once. It has a coverage map.

Here the human is out of the loop by design. No safety driver, no remote joystick pilot steering your car (remote operators can give high-level guidance when a vehicle gets confused, but no one is driving). The company's safety argument is statistical: its published data compares its crash rates favorably against human-driver benchmarks over tens of millions of miles. Critics answer that the company grades its own homework, that its cities and routes are chosen carefully, and that human-driver statistics include drunk, distracted, and exhausted drivers — a low bar to clear.

And failure, when it comes, is public in a way Flyways' could never be. Waymo recently issued a software recall after reports that its vehicles failed to stop properly for school buses — the kind of error that is rare, visible, filmed, and instantly national news. A dispatcher rejecting a bad route suggestion is invisible. A driverless car doing the wrong thing near a school bus is a headline. Same industry; completely different failure economics.

4

The spectrum: in, on, and out of the loop

Our second Monday question — where is the human? — has been binary so far. This chapter upgrades it to a spectrum with three main positions.

In the loop: the AI proposes, a human disposes, nothing happens without human sign-off. Flyways. EveryCure. The human is the gate.

On the loop: the AI acts on its own, and a human supervises, ready to intervene. This sounds like the best of both worlds. It's often the worst seat in the house — humans are famously bad at staying vigilant while monitoring a system that's almost always right. Attention decays; the takeover comes precisely at the hardest moment. Ask anyone who has studied a driver "supervising" a car that mostly drives itself.

Out of the loop: the AI acts; no human is positioned to intervene in the moment. Waymo, deliberately. The design bet is that removing the unreliable human beats keeping a badly-positioned one.

The handoff problem

The middle of the spectrum hides a trap: systems reliable enough to lull their supervisors but not reliable enough to be left alone. The handoff — machine to human, mid-crisis, no warning — is where "on the loop" designs break. It's a major reason Waymo skipped the middle entirely rather than a step it hasn't reached yet.

Now run trust across the spectrum. Flyways had to earn the trust of a few hundred professional dispatchers with a track record they could personally verify. Waymo has to earn the trust of everyone — riders, other drivers, pedestrians, parents at bus stops — most of whom will never read a safety report. Surveys still find a majority of Americans say they'd be afraid to ride in a self-driving car. The technology's coverage map is expanding faster than the trust map. Which one decides how this goes? That's not a technical question. That's Wednesday's lab.

5

Question it & decide

The five questions, applied to both cases at once
1

What is it, and what does it do? Two systems. Flyways: predictive AI recommending routes to a licensed dispatcher at Alaska Airlines. Waymo: a stack of perception, prediction, and control acting in the physical world, embodied AI. Neither is a chatbot; neither could hallucinate a citation.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Flyways exists to save fuel and minutes; the airline benefits, and so does anyone who breathes. Waymo exists to sell rides without paying drivers and, by its own account, to crash less than people do. Who benefits: riders, the company, and, if the numbers hold, everyone on the road.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Flyways' failures are invisible: a worse route, a rejected good one, nobody outside ever knows. Waymo's are filmed, recalled, and reported to regulators. The twist worth arguing: the visible-failure system may improve faster precisely because it can't hide.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? "Fewer injury crashes per mile than humans" is Waymo's own analysis of its own data. "A robot ran into a school bus" is a video. Which is evidence, and of what? What would settle it is independent crash data, and the NHTSA reports are the closest thing we have.

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Keep the dispatcher in the loop at Alaska: cheap, and it works. Fix Waymo's permit with conditions you can price (Wednesday's lab). Drop driverless cars from your city and the cost is the crashes humans will keep causing. Whoever keeps the track record holds the power; decide who that should be.

Deciding, this week, is literal: cities and states actually vote on this. Robotaxis need permits, and communities set conditions — or refuse. That means the question "should driverless cars operate here?" belongs to city councils, not just companies, and refusal or conditional approval are live options, not obstruction. Wednesday's lab seats you on that council for a city you know: Bellingham. Bring your Monday positions; you'll have to convert them into permit conditions with costs attached.

6

Key terms

Advisory (decision-support) AI

AI that recommends while humans retain the decision and the responsibility. Flyways' design.

Embodied AI

AI that acts in the physical world through sensors and actuators — where failure has mass and momentum.

In / on / out of the loop

The three human positions: gatekeeper, supervisor, absent. The middle seat is harder than it sounds.

The handoff problem

Machine-to-human transfers under pressure fail exactly when they matter most — the case against "on the loop."

Results-based trust

Trust earned by track record rather than understanding — the only kind most of us can have in AI, which makes the track record's keeper powerful.

Operational design domain

The mapped conditions — cities, streets, weather — a driverless system is approved to handle. The future arrives with a coverage map.

7

Discussion questions

For Monday's discussion · listen to both NPR pieces first: the Flyways story and the robotaxi story (about 12 minutes total)
  1. The Flyways dispatchers came to trust a system they don't understand because it kept being right. Name one thing you trust the same way — and what it would take to lose that trust. How many bad results?
  2. Waymo skipped "human supervises the robot" and went straight to "no human." Using the handoff problem, explain why the middle seat might genuinely be the most dangerous of the three.
  3. A driverless car and a human-driven car cause identical minor crashes on the same street. Walk through who is accountable in each case — and name one way the driverless version is actually an improvement, and one way it's worse.
  4. Waymo's safety numbers are Waymo's numbers. What specific, independent evidence would you want before letting a robotaxi drive your younger sibling to school? Be concrete — name the data, not the vibe.
  5. If robotaxis applied to operate in Bellingham next year: your instinct right now — approve, deny, or approve with conditions? One sentence. Keep it; Wednesday you'll find out whether the evidence moves you.
