Transportation: The Empty Driver's Seat

This chapter is about AI that acts in the physical world, where a wrong answer has mass and momentum, and about the one question that decides whether we trust it: where is the human? We'll learn how a robotaxi actually drives and how it learned to, the three places a person can stand relative to a machine, why the middle one is a trap, and how to read a safety claim. Then the case: Waymo, the driverless taxi that arrived city by city while everyone was still saying it was five years away.

Learning objectives
  1. Walk through the loop a robotaxi runs every fraction of a second, and name which steps are predictive AI and which make it embodied.
  2. Explain in plain language how a driverless car learned to see and to decide: labeled examples for perception, scored outcomes for driving, and simulation for practice.
  3. Read a reward function as a statement of business strategy, and name a trade-off encoded in one of its numbers.
  4. Place any AI system on the in, on, or out-of-the-loop spectrum, and explain why the middle position is often the most dangerous.
  5. Explain results-based trust, and why whoever keeps the track record holds the power.
  6. Take apart a per-mile safety claim: what it compares, what it leaves out, and who counted.
  7. Explain the operational design domain and the long tail, and why together they make the coverage map the safety design rather than a limitation.
  8. Argue both sides of the robotaxi question using evidence rather than instinct, and explain why diffuse benefits and concentrated costs decide permit votes.
  9. Run the five critical AI questions on Waymo and turn a position into permit conditions with costs attached.
1

Two rides

Ride one: you're on an Alaska Airlines flight. Somewhere in a Seattle operations center, an AI system called Flyways has studied the weather, the traffic, and thousands of past flights, and suggested a better route for your plane. A human dispatcher looked at the suggestion and decided whether to take it. You never knew any of this happened.

Ride two: you're in Phoenix, or San Francisco, or Austin, and you hail a taxi. It arrives with no one in it. The steering wheel turns by itself. There is no human to look at anything, decide anything, or grab anything. You know exactly what's happening; that's the product.

Same industry. Same safety-critical stakes. Opposite designs. This chapter is a controlled experiment that reality ran for us: hold the domain constant, move the human, and watch what changes in trust, in accountability, and in what failure looks like. By Wednesday, the lab hands the whole question to you, wearing a city council member's badge.

2

The concepts: the loop that drives, and where the human stands

2.1 How a robotaxi drives

Start with what's actually happening in that empty driver's seat, because most of the public argument skips it. A robotaxi runs the loop from Chapter 1's agent section, about ten times a second, for the whole ride. Sensors first: cameras, lidar (a spinning laser that measures distance), and radar, producing a firehose of pixels and points. Then perception: models trained on millions of labeled scenes turn that firehose into objects with names, a pedestrian here, a cyclist there, a school bus, a lane line. Then prediction: for every object, the car estimates what it will do over the next few seconds, a fan of possible futures with weights on them. Then planning: pick a path and a speed that keeps a safe margin from all of those futures. Then act: steer, brake, throttle. And again.

Two things to notice. Steps two and three are predictive AI, Chapter 1's Moment 1 at highway speed, and they're where almost every hard problem lives: a bus that looks like a truck, a child who might step off the curb, a construction cone in a spot the map didn't expect. Step five is what makes the system embodied: a wrong label in step two becomes a wrong turn of the wheel in step five, with the car's mass behind it. A chatbot that mislabels something writes a wrong sentence. A car that mislabels something has momentum.

And notice what isn't there: a step where a person is asked. Waymo does have remote assistants, but they answer a stuck car's questions ("is this construction zone passable?"); they don't drive it. That's a design choice, and the rest of this chapter is about it.

Embodied AI

AI that acts in the physical world through sensors and actuators. What people get wrong: they think the hard part is the driving. The hard part is perception and prediction, the same pattern-matching as every other predictive system, with the difference that a mistake here has mass and momentum.

2.2 How the driver learned to see

Section 2.1 said perception uses "models trained on millions of labeled scenes." That sentence is carrying most of the weight in this chapter, and the same sentence sits under almost every AI system in this book, so it is worth opening up.

Start with why the obvious approach fails. You could try to write the rules for recognizing a person: upright, about so tall, two legs, moving at about walking speed. Then comes someone in a wheelchair, someone carrying a mattress, a kid in a dinosaur costume, a cyclist half hidden behind a parked van, a mannequin in the back of a truck. Every rule needs an exception and every exception needs an exception. Researchers spent decades on that approach, which is a large part of why Chapter 1's quiet decade was quiet.

What works instead is learning the rules from examples. A neural network is a stack of layers made of very simple units. Each unit takes in some numbers, does a small calculation, and passes a result to the next layer. The first layer sees raw input, the pixel values of a camera frame. The last layer produces an answer: pedestrian, cyclist, school bus. The layers in between are where the work happens, and "deep" learning just means there are many of them.

Every connection between units is a dial. A modern network has billions of them, and before training they are set randomly, so the network's answers are random too. Training fixes that one nudge at a time: show it a labeled example, compare its answer to the label, and adjust every dial slightly in the direction that would have made the answer a little less wrong. Do that across millions of examples. Nobody tells the network to look for a red octagon or for legs. The early layers end up detecting edges and patches of color, the middle layers detect wheels, limbs, and lettering, and the later layers combine those into whole objects. A network built to scan an image in small overlapping patches, hunting for the same pattern anywhere it appears, is called a convolutional network. It is the direct descendant of Chapter 1's first moment, the 2012 ImageNet result, now running at thirty miles an hour.

Deep learning

Pattern recognition learned from labeled examples by adjusting billions of numerical dials inside a many-layered network. What people get wrong: they assume someone wrote the rules and the machine follows them. Nobody wrote the rules. That is the source of the power, and of the two problems below: the system cannot explain itself, and it is only as good as what it has already seen.

One camera is not enough, and neither is one kind of sensor. The camera reads color, text, and brake lights, and it is the only sensor that can tell you a traffic light is red, but it is poor at distance and it struggles in glare and darkness. Lidar measures the exact shape and distance of everything around the car and does not care whether it is midnight, but it sees no color and thins out in heavy rain, fog, and steam. Radar measures speed directly and punches through weather, but it is coarse enough that a manhole cover and a stopped car can look uncomfortably similar. Sensor fusion is the step that merges these three imperfect witnesses into one list of objects, each with a confidence attached.

The interesting part is what happens when the witnesses disagree. If lidar reports something in the lane and the camera reports empty road, the car has to decide which to believe, and both answers have a price. Believe the lidar every time and you get phantom braking, which is uncomfortable, unpopular, and its own kind of hazard in traffic. Believe the camera every time and you eventually drive through something. That threshold is not a fact about the world. It is a setting, chosen by people, with a cost attached to each direction, and it is the first of several places in this chapter where an engineering decision is really a business decision.

Sensor fusion

Combining cameras, lidar, and radar into a single picture of what is around the car. What people get wrong: they think more sensors means more certainty. More sensors means more disagreements, and someone has to decide in advance who wins, knowing that both kinds of error have a cost.

Two consequences follow from learning this way, and the rest of the chapter keeps running into them. First, the system cannot tell you why. What it learned is spread across billions of numbers, not stored as a sentence, so when a network misreads a stop sign in an odd shadow, there is no line of reasoning to inspect. Second, it is only as good as what it has seen. Both of those come back in section 2.8.

2.3 How the driver learned to decide

Perception can be taught with labeled examples because each image has a right answer. Driving does not work that way. What exactly should the car have done at this intersection, with this pedestrian half off the curb, this delivery van double parked, this cyclist arriving on the left? There are several defensible answers and an unlimited supply of situations. You cannot label your way out of that.

So the deciding part of the car, the planner, is taught a different way. It tries something, gets a score for how the attempt went, and adjusts toward whatever scores well. That is reinforcement learning, and it is how Chapter 1's second moment worked: AlphaGo was not shown a library of correct moves, it played itself millions of times and learned from winning and losing. The driving version swaps the win for a score that rewards arriving smoothly and punishes everything from a jerky stop to a collision.

Reinforcement learning

Learning from scored outcomes instead of labeled answers: try, get feedback, adjust. What people get wrong: they assume the system was taught what good driving is. It was taught what scores well, which is only as close to good driving as whoever wrote the scoring got it.

That scoring sheet is called the reward function, and it is the most quietly important document in the company. It is a literal list of behaviors and points. Waymo does not publish its own, but a simplified version looks like this:

Read those numbers again as a business student rather than an engineer, because this is where the two jobs meet. The ratio between one point per second of waiting and fifteen points for a close call is a decision about how much passenger patience a margin of safety is worth. Nobody votes on that ratio. It is set in a product meeting, written into a training run, and then executed ten times a second on a street where people live. Strategy usually arrives as a slide. Here it arrives as a number that moves a two-ton object.

The proof that these numbers are strategy is that the company changes them. Early Waymo vehicles were famously timid: always yielding, always waiting for a clean gap. That turned out to have costs of its own, because an overly polite car blocks traffic and other drivers learn they can cut it off forever. In 2025 the company deliberately tuned its cars to be more assertive. The visible results included vehicles changing lanes without signaling, and one memorable illegal U-turn in San Bruno, where a police officer pulled over a car with nobody in it. The window came down and a remote employee apologized over the speaker. Somebody turned a dial toward "take the gap," and the street felt it.

The reward function

The list of scored outcomes a learned system is trained to maximize. What people get wrong: they treat it as a technical detail. It is the company's priorities in numbers, it encodes who is asked to wait and who is protected, and changing one line of it changes behavior on public streets the same week.

One problem remains: you cannot learn to drive by crashing a few hundred thousand times. So most of the learning happens in simulation. Every real mile becomes a scenario that can be replayed with the variables moved: the same intersection, but the child steps out a half second earlier, or the light changes, or it is raining. Waymo has built a generative model of driving, trained on its own fleet's video, that produces scenes which never happened at all, which is the same technology as Chapter 2's chatbot pointed at traffic instead of text. The fleet's real driving is measured in hundreds of millions of miles, and the simulated driving in tens of billions.

Two things follow, and they matter well beyond cars. The first is competitive. Real miles feed the models, the models feed the simulator, the simulator tests the fix, the fixed car drives more real miles: a loop that compounds, and one a competitor cannot shortcut by buying the same sensors or hiring the same engineers. That is why this is a strategy story and not only an engineering story, and it is the same flywheel Chapter 9 will describe in less dramatic industries. The second is a limit. A simulator can only imagine variations on what it has already seen, and a simulated pedestrian is the company's own model of pedestrians, which means the practice is honest exactly where the data was and silent where it was not.

2.4 Where the human stands: in, on, and out of the loop

Our second question, where is the human?, has been binary so far. This chapter upgrades it to a spectrum with three positions.

In the loop: the AI proposes, a human disposes, and nothing happens without sign-off. Every Cure's doctors. And the first ride in this chapter: Alaska Airlines worked with a company called Air Space Intelligence to build Flyways, an AI system that evaluates routes and flags better options to the airline's human dispatchers. Alaska says it saves tens of thousands of flight hours and roughly a million gallons of fuel a year, and the FAA has since hired the same company, on an $875 million contract, to help modernize the national airspace. The design decision is the whole point: Flyways recommends. Licensed dispatchers accept or reject every suggestion, and responsibility for each flight plan stays with humans.

On the loop: the AI acts on its own, and a human supervises, ready to intervene. This sounds like the best of both worlds. It's often the worst seat in the house, because humans are famously bad at staying vigilant while monitoring a system that's almost always right. Attention decays. The takeover comes precisely at the hardest moment. Ask anyone who has studied a driver "supervising" a car that mostly drives itself.

Out of the loop: the AI acts, and no human is positioned to intervene in the moment. Waymo, deliberately. The design bet is that removing the unreliable human beats keeping a badly positioned one.

In, on, and out of the loop

The three human positions: gatekeeper, supervisor, absent. What people get wrong: they assume more human involvement is always safer. The supervisor seat can be the least safe of the three, because it asks a person to do the one thing people are worst at: pay full attention to something that almost never needs it.

2.5 The handoff problem

The middle of the spectrum hides a trap with a name. A system that's reliable enough to lull its supervisor but not reliable enough to be left alone produces the handoff problem: the moment the machine gives up and hands control to a person is, by definition, the hardest moment of the drive, and the person has had nothing to do for the last forty minutes. Reaction time, situational awareness, and the sense of what the car was about to do are all at their lowest exactly when they're needed most.

This is why Waymo skipped the middle rather than passing through it. It's also why "a human is always monitoring" is not the reassurance it sounds like. The question to ask about any on-the-loop design is not whether a person is watching but what happens in the three seconds after the machine says "your turn."

The handoff problem

Machine-to-human transfers under pressure fail exactly when they matter most. What people get wrong: they picture the handoff as a safety net. It's a cliff edge, and the person standing at it has been asleep.

2.6 Results-based trust, and how to read a safety claim

When Flyways arrived, many dispatchers didn't trust it. What changed their minds wasn't an explanation of the algorithm, which they can't see inside any more than we can. It was a track record. The suggestions kept being good. That's results-based trust: we trust some things because we understand how they work, and others because they keep working, and almost all trust in AI, including ours, is the second kind. Hold that, because it has a consequence: if trust comes from the track record, then whoever keeps the track record holds the power.

Now scale it up. Flyways had to earn the trust of a few hundred professionals who could check its work personally. Waymo has to earn the trust of everyone: riders, other drivers, pedestrians, parents at bus stops, most of whom will never read a safety report. The company's argument is statistical, and it deserves to be read carefully rather than accepted or dismissed. "Fewer crashes per mile than human drivers" compares the robotaxi's crashes, on the roads it chose, in the weather it handles, counted by the company, against a human average that includes drunk, distracted, and exhausted drivers, counted by regulators. Every clause in that sentence is a Question 4. None of it makes the claim false. All of it makes the claim smaller than the headline.

Surveys still find a majority of Americans say they'd be afraid to ride in a self-driving car. The technology's coverage map is expanding faster than the trust map, and which one decides how this goes isn't a technical question. It's Wednesday's.

Results-based trust

Trust earned by track record rather than understanding. What people get wrong: they treat "it's been safe so far" as the end of the argument. It's the start: safe compared to what, where, and according to whom?

2.7 The coverage map: operational design domain

Chapter 9 will say that a general-purpose technology arrives through reorganization, not a switch. Here's what that looks like on the ground: a robotaxi company doesn't launch everywhere. It defines an operational design domain, the set of conditions the system is approved to handle, and earns it one piece at a time. Which roads: mapped in detail, to the centimeter, and updated constantly, which is why a service launches in one city and not the next one over. Which speeds: surface streets first; freeways came years later. Which weather: rain, mostly yes; fog and snow, mostly not yet. Which hours, expanded as the track record grows.

At the edge of the domain the car does something people find either reassuring or maddening: it stops, or it asks. This is where remote assistance actually lives. A car that meets a construction zone its map doesn't know can pull over and put a question to a person in an operations center, "is this passable?" The person answers; the car drives. That's a human on the loop for one decision, not a human driving, and the distinction matters for Chapter 6's accountability question: the remote assistant is not a driver, and the company has said so in court and in filings.

The coverage map is also why the case can't be settled by a single statistic. Waymo's crash rate is a crash rate inside its domain, on roads it chose, in weather it handles. That's not cheating; it's what the domain means. But it's why "safer than humans" and "safer than humans on these streets, in this weather, by this count" are different sentences, and section 2.6 is about the difference.

Operational design domain

The mapped conditions, cities, streets, weather, hours, that a driverless system is approved to handle, with defined behavior at the edge. What people get wrong: they read a small domain as a failure. It's the safety design. The failure would be a domain the car doesn't know it's left.

2.8 The long tail: why the last few percent is the hard part

If the system is excellent at lanes, lights, and pedestrians, what's left? The long tail: the rare situations that make up almost none of the miles and almost all of the failures. A school bus with its stop arm out at an odd angle. A person in a costume that no training image looked like. A flooded street that reads as pavement. A police officer directing traffic by hand, contradicting the light. Each is rare. Together, at hundreds of thousands of rides a week, they happen every day.

The school-bus recall is a tail event, worked. Perception, step two of section 2.1, has to recognize a bus, read whether its stop arm is out, and understand that the rule is different for that vehicle than for any other: you stop, in both directions, no matter what the light says. The system had seen many buses. It had not handled every angle and every partial view of a stop arm the way a human driver who grew up riding buses does without thinking. Reports came in, the company found the gap, and the fix was a software update that added the case to the left side of the curve. That's how the tail gets shorter: one filmed failure at a time.

Two things follow. First, "99.99 percent" is not a finish line for embodied AI, because the remaining fraction is multiplied by every mile, and every failure is public. Second, this is the strongest argument for the coverage map and against on-the-loop designs: the tail is exactly where the handoff would happen, and exactly where the supervising human, lulled by ten thousand ordinary miles, is least ready.

The long tail

The rare situations that hold almost none of the miles and almost all of the failures. What people get wrong: they judge a system by the common case, where it's excellent. Ask what it does with the costume, the flood, and the hand signal, because that's where the recall comes from.

2.9 Who's counting: the track record's keeper

Put sections 2.6 through 2.8 together and one question is left over: who keeps the count? Right now, mostly the company. It logs every mile, every disengagement, every contact. Some of that goes to regulators, who publish crash reports on a schedule; some goes into the company's own safety analyses, which compare its rates to human benchmarks; and some stays inside. Independent researchers work with what's published, which is why the public argument runs on a mix of company statistics and bystander video, neither of which is the dataset you'd want.

This is Chapter 11's question arriving early: whether the keeper of the track record should be the company, the state, or a third party with access to the raw data. It's also the most concrete permit condition a city can write, and Wednesday's lab prices it: require crash and disengagement reporting to the city, on the city's schedule, in a format someone can check. It costs the company privacy about its failures. It buys the city the only thing that turns "trust us" into a track record.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five systems: is the human in, on, or out of the loop? Decide, then click to check.
1. A radiologist reads every scan; software highlights spots she might have missed.

In the loop. The software proposes, the radiologist disposes. Nothing leaves the room without her sign-off.

2. A warehouse robot moves pallets on its own; a technician on the floor can hit a stop button.

Out of the loop in the moment, with a person on the loop for emergencies. The robot decides each move; the stop button is the handoff, and it works only if someone is looking.

3. Highway driving mode steers and brakes; the manual says to keep your hands on the wheel and eyes on the road.

On the loop, and the textbook handoff problem. The car does the work until it can't, and then it's yours, mid-crisis.

4. A spam filter moves messages to a folder; you never see them unless you go looking.

Out of the loop. Low stakes, so nobody minds. Notice that "out of the loop" isn't scary by itself; it's scary when the action has mass.

5. An AI drafts replies to customer emails; an agent clicks send on each one.

In the loop, on paper. In practice, if the agent clicks send 400 times a day without reading, it's out of the loop with a human-shaped rubber stamp. The position is where the attention is, not where the button is.

3

The case: the empty driver's seat

Background The future arrived city by city

For years, fully self-driving taxis were the technology that was always five years away. Quietly, that stopped being true. Waymo, born as Google's self-driving car project, now operates paid robotaxi service in more than ten U.S. cities, from Phoenix and San Francisco to Austin, Atlanta, Miami, and across Texas, with Denver, Washington D.C. and London announced next. At last count the company reported over 400,000 paid rides per week, on freeways in some cities, to and from major airports. In China, Baidu's Apollo Go and rivals like Pony.ai run their own large fleets. The future arrived on a rolling basis, city by city, which is itself a lesson: transformative technology doesn't switch on everywhere at once. It has a coverage map.

Here the human is out of the loop by design. No safety driver, no remote joystick pilot steering your car (remote operators can give high-level guidance when a vehicle gets confused, but no one is driving). The company's safety argument is statistical: its published data compares its crash rates favorably against human-driver benchmarks over tens of millions of miles. Critics answer that the company grades its own homework, that its cities and routes are chosen carefully, and that human-driver statistics include drunk, distracted, and exhausted drivers, a low bar to clear.

And failure, when it comes, is public in a way Flyways' could never be. Waymo recently issued a software recall after reports that its vehicles failed to stop properly for school buses, the kind of error that is rare, visible, filmed, and instantly national news. A dispatcher rejecting a bad route suggestion is invisible. A driverless car doing the wrong thing near a school bus is a headline. Same industry; completely different failure economics.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

An embodied, agentic system: the sense-perceive-predict-plan-act loop from section 2.1, running on a fleet. The five parts: data, millions of miles of driving plus hand-labeled scenes; model, perception and prediction networks, plus a planner that mixes rules with learned behavior; output, a steering angle and a speed, ten times a second; feedback loop, every mile driven becomes training data, and every recall becomes a rule; humans, the riders, the remote assistants who answer questions but don't drive, the engineers, and everyone else on the road, who never agreed to be part of the test. It isn't a chatbot; it couldn't hallucinate a citation. Its failures have mass. Compare Flyways, which only recommends, and where the human stays the gate.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

Waymo exists to sell rides without paying drivers and, by its own account, to crash less than people do. Who benefits: riders, the company, and, if the numbers hold, everyone on the road. Who doesn't: the drivers it replaces, whose jobs don't come back; the city that absorbs the surprises, from blocked intersections to the school-bus recall; and the pedestrians and cyclists who are part of the experiment without having signed up for it. The drivers deserve one more sentence, because the case usually skips them: ride-hail driving is one of the largest sources of flexible income in American cities, and every city a robotaxi enters is a city where that income shrinks for people who had few other options. The benefit is real, and so is the bill, and they land on different people.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

Waymo's failures are filmed, recalled, and reported to regulators, because the failure is a car doing something wrong in public. Flyways' failures would be invisible: a worse route, a rejected good suggestion, nobody outside ever knows. The twist worth arguing: the visible-failure system may improve faster precisely because it can't hide. The school-bus recall was a bad week for the company and a public correction for everyone else.

Business implications What this means for a company that puts AI in the physical world

Most businesses won't build a car, but a growing number are putting AI where it can act: warehouse robots, delivery drones, automated forklifts, kitchen equipment, farm machinery. The case gives them three lessons. First, choose the loop position on purpose. The default many companies drift into, "the machine does it and someone keeps an eye on it," is the handoff problem with a payroll line, and it fails at the worst moment. Either keep a person as a real gate, or engineer the system to be safe without one, and know which you chose. Second, the track record is the product. A robotaxi company sells safety statistics as much as rides, and whoever counts them, and how, is a business decision that customers, regulators, and juries will read. Third, the coverage map is the rollout plan. Waymo didn't switch on everywhere; it expanded one operational design domain at a time, which is slower and much cheaper than one national mistake. The costs: slower launches, independent auditing you don't control, and a person on the floor even when the robot rarely needs one. The alternative cost is the video.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

"Fewer injury crashes per mile than humans" is Waymo's own analysis of its own data. "A robot ran into a school bus" is a video. Which is evidence, and of what? Both are, and both are partial: one is a statistic without an outside auditor, the other is an anecdote with a camera. What would settle it is independent crash data on matched roads, and regulators' reports are the closest thing we have. Underneath the numbers is a question no dataset answers: who gets to decide whether the experiment runs on a given street? Right now the answer is a company's expansion plan plus a state permit, and the people at the bus stop aren't consulted. That's Chapter 11's question arriving early.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it where its track record is long and independently checked. The cost: you're trusting a count someone else keeps.
  • Fix it with a permit whose conditions you can price: crash reporting to the city, speed limits near schools, a cap on fleet size until the numbers are audited, a plan for the drivers it replaces. Each costs the company money and the city staff time, and each is Wednesday's lab.
  • Drop it from your city, and the cost is the crashes humans will keep causing, which is a real number too.

Whoever keeps the track record holds the power. Decide who that should be.

How to make it good

Keep robotaxis where the track record is long and someone independent keeps the count. Fix the permit so its conditions can be priced: crash and disengagement reporting to the city, limits where the long tail lives (school zones, weather the car hasn't proven), a fleet cap until the numbers are audited, and a plan for the drivers it replaces. Drop the on-the-loop design that asks a person to stay alert for a handoff that comes mid-crisis. The cost is slower expansion for the company, staff time for the city, and trusting a count that someone other than the company now has to keep.

  3. Q3 · What happens when it fails, and who finds out? The school-bus recall happened because the failure was filmed. Name a failure a robotaxi could have that nobody would film, say who would find out and how, and say whether the coverage map makes it more or less likely.
  4. Q4 · Who's saying that, and how would we know? Waymo's safety numbers are Waymo's numbers. What specific, independent evidence would you want before letting a robotaxi drive your younger sibling to school? Be concrete: name the data, who would keep it, and why they don't now.
  5. Q5 · Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? If robotaxis applied to operate in Bellingham next year: approve, deny, or approve with conditions? Write one condition with its cost to the company and its cost to the city. Keep it; Wednesday you'll find out whether the evidence moves you.
The takeaway from the case

Same industry, two designs, opposite failure economics. The dispatcher's invisible mistake and the robotaxi's filmed one are both real; only one of them gets fixed in public. The question the case leaves you with isn't whether driverless cars are safe. It's who gets to say so, and what you'd need to see to believe them.

4

The argument in the street

Section 3 ran the five questions on the system. This section is about the fight over it. The fight is where a technology's future actually gets settled, and this case is the book's clearest example of a real public good and a real harm landing on completely different people.

4.1 The case for: this is a public health argument

More than 38,000 people die on American roads every year. That is the equivalent of a full airliner going down every day, year after year, and we have arranged our lives around treating it as weather. A trauma surgeon who reviewed Waymo's safety data put the point in one line: "If this was a disease, we would have declared war". What he saw in the data was not a gadget. It was a large reduction in exactly the crashes that fill a trauma bay: pedestrians struck, and cars hit from the side at intersections.

The company's own analysis, covering more than 220 million driverless miles through March 2026, reports roughly 94 percent fewer crashes causing serious or worse injuries than its human benchmark on the same roads, and about 82 percent fewer injury crashes of any kind. Section 2.6 already taught you what to do with a number like that: ask what it compares, where it was collected, and who counted. It is the company's count, on its own coverage map. It is also a very large effect, it has held as the fleet expanded into harder cities, and at least one insurer, which loses money by being wrong about risk, has published analysis pointing the same direction. None of that makes it settled. It does mean the burden of proof is no longer all on one side, and a critic who waves the numbers away is doing the same thing the company is accused of.

Take the argument seriously and it gets uncomfortable fast. If the effect is anywhere near that size, then a city that delays robotaxis for a year is choosing a number of crashes it will never have to see, count, or explain. That is the strongest version of the case for, and it is why this chapter does not let "wait and see" be a free option.

4.2 The other case for: what riders are actually buying

The surgeon's argument is about the population. There is a quieter one about the individual, and it is easy to miss because it sounds like a small thing until you ask who is saying it. A meaningful share of riders say the appeal is not the novelty of the empty seat. It is that there is no stranger in the front seat: nobody who knows where you live, nobody to talk to you when you have been crying, nobody to size you up at one in the morning. Rideshare drivers are overwhelmingly fine. The point is about the worst case, and in a driverless car the worst case is a software fault rather than a person.

There is an access story too. For someone who is blind, or cannot drive because of a disability, or has outlived their license, point-to-point travel has always meant arranging it around somebody else: a family member, a volunteer, a paratransit booking made days ahead. Being able to decide at 7 p.m. to go somewhere is not a small upgrade for that rider. It is a different life.

Here is the uncomfortable part, and it is the sentence to bring to Wednesday. Some of what riders value exists because the driver is gone. That means "just keep the human drivers" is not a clean answer either, because for some riders the human driver was part of what they were trying to avoid. The labor cost in 4.3 is real. So is this. Any position worth having has to hold both.

4.3 The case against: whose value, exactly

The people most affected by robotaxi expansion are not reading crash studies. They are driving for a living. Roughly four and a half million Americans work professionally behind a wheel. Ride-hail driving in particular has been one of the largest sources of flexible income for people with few other options: new immigrants, students, parents working around a school day, workers between jobs. Surveys find that a large majority of Americans expect driverless vehicles to cost jobs. They are not wrong.

The response has been organized and local, which is the form this argument almost always takes. Drivers have rallied in San Francisco and Seattle. Boston's city council held hearings and moved to require a human in the vehicle. New York's governor pulled a robotaxi proposal after taxi driver opposition, with a spokesperson citing insufficient stakeholder support, which in plain language means the drivers had votes. In London, a drivers' union declared an emergency as Waymo prepared to launch, warning that tens of thousands of licensed private hire drivers were exposed.

The counterargument is the one you will hear in every AI conversation for the rest of your life: we said this about elevator operators, bank tellers, and automatic looms, and the work moved rather than vanished. That is historically true, and Chapter 9 takes it apart properly. It is also not a plan. The displaced driver is a person with a lease payment this month, and "the economy will adjust" is a sentence with no address on it. Both of those things stay true at the same time, which is why this argument does not resolve, only get managed.

4.4 When the argument stops being an argument

In February 2024, a driverless car in San Francisco's Chinatown was surrounded during Lunar New Year, somebody threw a firework inside, and it burned. In June 2025, during protests against immigration raids in downtown Los Angeles, people summoned several cars with the app and set five of them on fire. Some protesters called them spy cars, which is not a metaphor: the vehicles record continuously, and police have requested that footage. That is Chapter 11's Bellingham camera case arriving in a different body.

Notice the mechanism of the burning, because it is the chapter's own lesson turned inside out. The cars did not drive away. They could not: they are built never to push through a crowd of people, so surrounded, they stopped and waited. The safety property that makes them worth defending is exactly what made them defenseless. A system's greatest virtue and its exposure are frequently the same property, seen from two sides.

These are not technology stories. A burned car is what a political dispute looks like when the people on the losing end have nowhere to file a complaint. Read it the way you would read a strike or a recall petition: as information about who was not consulted.

4.5 What a business is supposed to do with this

The useful term here is value capture. A technology can create enormous value and still cause a political crisis, if the gains and the costs land on different people. Waymo's case is the clean example: the gains go to riders, to the company, and to everyone who is not hit by a car they would otherwise have been hit by, and that last group never knows who they are. The costs go to a named, countable, organized group of drivers, and they know exactly who they are. Diffuse benefits, concentrated costs. That asymmetry, not the crash statistics, is what decides permit votes.

For a manager, that turns into three unglamorous jobs. Know the arithmetic of who gains and who pays before the launch, not after the hearing. Bring a transition plan, and put a real number on it: a per-ride fee that funds retraining is real money, and every dollar of it is a reason a competitor launches in the friendlier city next door. And expect to negotiate locally, forever, because the permit is the product's license to exist and it gets re-argued every time something is filmed.

Value capture

Who keeps the gains a technology creates, and who absorbs its costs. What people get wrong: they argue about whether a technology is good, when the fight is almost always about the split. A system can be a public health win and a political disaster at the same time, and usually is.

The takeaway from the argument

The crashes it prevents are invisible and statistical; the jobs it ends have names and addresses. Both are real, neither cancels the other, and the only honest position is one that names what its own side costs. Bring that position to Wednesday, where you will have to write it down as permit conditions with prices on them.

5

Decide: the badge is real

Deciding, this week, is literal: cities and states actually vote on this. Robotaxis need permits, and communities set conditions, or refuse. That means the question "should driverless cars operate here?" belongs to city councils, not just companies, and refusal or conditional approval are live options, not obstruction. Wednesday's lab seats you on that council for a city you know: Bellingham. Bring your Monday positions; you'll have to convert them into permit conditions with costs attached.

6

Key terms

Embodied AI

AI that acts in the physical world through sensors and actuators, where failure has mass and momentum.

Perception and prediction

The two predictive steps inside a robotaxi: name what's around the car, then guess what each thing will do next. Where the hard problems live.

Deep learning

Pattern recognition learned from labeled examples by tuning billions of dials in a many-layered network. Nobody writes the rules, which is why the system cannot explain itself.

Sensor fusion

Merging camera, lidar, and radar into one picture of the world. The interesting part is what the car is told to do when the sensors disagree.

Reinforcement learning

Learning from scored outcomes rather than labeled answers. How the planner, and AlphaGo before it, learned to act where there is no single right answer.

The reward function

The list of scored outcomes the system is trained to maximize: company priorities written as numbers, executed ten times a second in traffic.

Value capture

Who keeps a technology's gains and who absorbs its costs. Diffuse benefits and concentrated costs are what decide permit votes, not crash statistics.

Advisory (decision-support) AI

AI that recommends while humans retain the decision and the responsibility. Flyways' design.

In / on / out of the loop

The three human positions: gatekeeper, supervisor, absent. The middle seat is harder than it sounds.

The handoff problem

Machine-to-human transfers under pressure fail exactly when they matter most, the case against "on the loop."

Results-based trust

Trust earned by track record rather than understanding, the only kind most of us can have in AI, which makes the track record's keeper powerful.

Operational design domain

The mapped conditions, cities, streets, weather, hours, a driverless system is approved to handle, with defined behavior at the edge. The future arrives with a coverage map.

The long tail

The rare situations that hold almost none of the miles and almost all of the failures. Where every recall comes from.

Remote assistance

A person in an operations center who answers a stuck car's question. On the loop for one decision, not driving, and the company says so in court.
