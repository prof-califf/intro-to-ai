Transportation: The Empty Driver's Seat

This chapter is about AI that has a body. When AI acts in the physical world, a wrong answer has mass and momentum, and one question decides whether we trust it: where is the human? First we learn the ideas underneath: how a machine learns to see, how it learns to decide, how it practices in worlds that never happened, and why the rare cases are the hard ones. Then we put them to work on one case: Waymo, the driverless taxi that arrived city by city while everyone was still saying it was five years away.

Context: two rides

Ride one: you're on an Alaska Airlines flight out of Seattle. Somewhere in an operations center, an AI system called Flyways has studied the weather, the traffic in the sky, and thousands of past flights, and it has suggested a better route for your plane. A human dispatcher looked at the suggestion and decided whether to take it. You never knew any of this happened.

Ride two: you're in Phoenix, or San Francisco, or Austin, and you hail a taxi. It arrives with no one in it. The steering wheel turns by itself. There is no human to look at anything, decide anything, or grab anything. You know exactly what's happening, because that's the product.

Both rides are transportation. In both, a mistake can hurt people. But the two companies made opposite choices about one thing. In the first ride, a person checks every suggestion the AI makes before anything happens. In the second, no person is in the car at all. That makes the pair a useful comparison: same kind of business, same kind of risk, and the only big difference is where the human stands. So we can watch what changes when you move the human. How much do people trust it? Who gets blamed when it goes wrong? What does a failure even look like? On Wednesday, the lab puts you on the other side of the table: you're a strategy analyst at a robotaxi company, pitching Bellingham's city council.

The point of this chapter, in one sentence: when AI gets a body, the hard part is not the driving but the seeing and the rare surprises, and the question that decides whether we trust it is where the human stands.

What this chapter teaches:

  • Concepts: embodied AI and its loop, neural networks and deep learning, how machines see (convolutional networks and sensor fusion), how they learn to decide (reinforcement learning), world models and the simulation flywheel, the long tail, in, on, and out of the loop, and responsible AI.
  • The case: Waymo, run through the five critical AI questions, including what happens when the car is wrong and the fight in the street over who it helps.
  • The lab: as an analyst at a robotaxi company, you build a pitch to Bellingham's city council, including the conditions you'd accept and what each one costs.

The concepts: AI with a body

Most of the AI in this book so far lives on a screen. A chatbot writes a sentence. A feed picks a video. A classifier sorts a résumé. If any of them gets it wrong, the mistake is words or numbers. This chapter is about AI that gets it wrong with a two-ton car. Before we meet the company, let's learn the ideas, using examples from everyday life. Section 3 then runs every one of them on Waymo.

2.1 Embodied AI: when AI gets a body

Embodied AI is AI that senses the physical world and acts in it. It has sensors, like cameras, instead of a keyboard, and actuators, the motors and brakes that make things move, instead of a text box. You may already own one. A robot vacuum maps your living room, notices the couch, guesses where the dog will wander, picks a path, and turns its wheels. Warehouse robots that carry shelves, delivery drones, farm machines, and self-driving cars all work the same way. (You'll also hear this called physical AI. Same idea, different label.)

Remember the loop from Chapter 1's section on agents: look at the situation, decide, act, check what happened, and go again. Embodied AI runs that same loop, except the "tools" are wheels and brakes, and the loop runs many times a second for as long as the machine is moving. Engineers usually split it into five steps. Let's walk through them with one scene: a car is coming up on a corner where a child is chasing a ball toward the street.

  1. Sense. Cameras, lidar (a spinning laser that measures distance), and radar collect raw data. Nothing has a name yet.
  2. Perceive. Software names what's there: a child, a ball, a parked van. This is the step that "sees."
  3. Predict. For each thing, it guesses the next few seconds, with odds, like a weather forecast: the child probably stops at the curb (60 percent) but might chase the ball (40 percent).
  4. Plan. It picks a speed and path that's safe in every likely future: slow down early, leave room.
  5. Act. Brake, steer, accelerate. Then the loop starts over with fresh data.

Step 2 (perceive) and step 3 (predict) are predictive AI, the same pattern-matching you met in Chapter 1, and they're where almost every hard problem lives: a bus that looks like a truck, a shadow that looks like a person. Step 5 (act) is what gives the system a body. A chatbot that mislabels something writes a wrong sentence you can delete. A car that mislabels something turns the wheel, and you can't take that back.

That's what people usually get wrong about embodied AI. They assume the hard part is steering and braking, which machines have done well for decades. The hard part is steps 2 and 3, and the rest of section 2 is about them: how a machine learns to see (2.2 and 2.3), to decide (2.4), to practice (2.5), and where it still fails (2.6).

2.2 How a machine learns to see: neural networks

So how does step 2 actually work? How does a grid of colored dots from a camera become "a child"? The answer sits under almost every AI system in this book, so it's worth slowing down.

You could try writing rules for spotting a person: upright, two legs, walking speed. Then comes someone in a wheelchair, a kid in a dinosaur costume, a cyclist half hidden behind a van. Every rule needs an exception, and every exception needs another. Researchers tried that for decades.

What works instead is letting the machine learn the rules from examples. That idea is older than you might think. In the 1990s, a researcher at Bell Labs named Yann LeCun built a system that learned to read handwritten numbers by studying thousands of examples, and banks used it to read the dollar amounts on paper checks. Nobody wrote a rule for what a "7" looks like. The system worked it out from the examples. Remember LeCun's name; he comes back in section 2.5 with a very different bet.

The tool that does this learning is called a neural network. The name comes from a loose comparison to brain cells. A real neuron collects signals from its neighbors and, if the signals are strong enough, passes a signal on. An artificial "node" does a cartoon version of the same thing with numbers.

Stack those nodes in layers and you have a network. The first layer takes in raw input, like the pixel values from a camera. The last layer gives an answer, like "pedestrian" or "cyclist." The layers in between are called hidden layers, because nobody looks at them directly. That is where the work happens: early layers react to simple things like edges and patches of color, middle layers combine those into parts like wheels and legs, and later layers combine parts into whole objects. A network with many hidden layers is called a deep neural network, and learning with one is called deep learning. "Deep" just means many layers. It doesn't mean wise.

Students always ask: neural network vs. deep learning?

A neural network is the thing: nodes connected by weighted lines, in layers. Deep learning is using a neural network with many hidden layers. So all deep learning uses neural networks, but a small network isn't "deep." The toy below has two hidden layers, so it's a small neural network. The one in a self-driving car has dozens, so that's deep learning. Both sit inside machine learning (any system that learns from data instead of following written rules), which sits inside the big umbrella of AI.

Here's what makes it learning instead of programming. Every line in that picture has a weight, and you can think of each weight as a dial. Before training, the dials are random, so the answers are random. Training shows the network an example with the right answer attached (a photo labeled "cyclist"), lets it guess, and turns every dial a tiny bit in whichever direction would have made the guess less wrong. Repeat millions of times and the dials settle where the network is usually right. (The formal name is backpropagation. You only need the idea.)

That toy shows the two facts the rest of this chapter keeps running into. First, the network can't tell you why. What it learned is spread across the dials as numbers, not written down as a rule, which is why people call it a "black box." Second, it's only as good as what it has seen. Plenty of walkers and cyclists in training means it's great at both. A person walking a bike, rarely seen, gets a confident wrong answer. Nobody wrote the rules; the machine found them in the examples. That's the source of the power and of the trouble.

2.3 Seeing in practice: convolutional networks and sensor fusion

Images are a special problem. A photo from one camera is millions of numbers, and a cyclist can show up anywhere in the frame, big or small, near or far. So the networks that handle images use a trick. Instead of looking at the whole picture at once, they slide many small "pattern spotters" across the image, a little square at a time, each one hunting for one simple pattern wherever it appears: a vertical edge here, a curve there, a patch of red. Early layers find edges. The next layers combine edges into shapes like a wheel or a leg. Later layers combine shapes into whole objects. A network built this way is called a convolutional neural network, or CNN ("convolution" is the math word for the sliding). LeCun's check reader was an early one.

You use CNNs every day: when your phone unlocks by looking at your face, when your photo app finds every dog you've photographed, when hospital software flags a spot on a scan. They took over in 2012, when a deep network crushed a famous photo-labeling contest called ImageNet, Chapter 1's first big moment.

One camera isn't enough for a car, either. Your phone already knows this: it finds you by combining GPS, Wi-Fi, and its motion sensors, because each one fails somewhere. Combining imperfect sources into one better answer is called sensor fusion. A self-driving car fuses three: cameras (great at color, signs, and traffic lights, poor at distance and darkness), lidar (exact shape and distance, day or night, but no color and weak in fog), and radar (measures speed through any weather, but blurry). The hard part is when they disagree. If lidar sees something in the lane and the camera sees an empty road, which one wins? Trust the lidar every time and the car slams the brakes for steam from a manhole. Trust the camera every time and someday it hits something the camera missed. That tie-breaker is a setting chosen by people, with a cost in each direction. More sensors means more disagreements, not more certainty, and it's the first place in this chapter where an engineering decision turns out to be a business decision.

2.4 How a machine learns to decide: reinforcement learning

Seeing can be taught with labeled examples because every photo has a right answer. Deciding doesn't. What should a car do at a four-way stop with a pedestrian half off the curb and a cyclist coming up on the left? There are several reasonable answers and endless situations. You can't label your way out of that.

So the deciding part, the planner, learns differently. It tries something, gets a score, and adjusts toward whatever scores well. That's reinforcement learning: learning from rewards and penalties instead of labeled answers. AlphaGo, from Chapter 1, learned this way, playing itself millions of times until it found Move 37. Warehouse robots learn to grab odd-shaped boxes the same way.

The list of what earns points and what costs points is called the reward function, and it may be the most quietly important document a company like this writes. Companies don't usually publish theirs, but a simplified one for a self-driving car might look like this:

2.5 Practicing in worlds that never happened: simulation and world models

Reinforcement learning learns by failing, and you can't let a car fail a hundred thousand times on real streets. Airlines solved a version of this long ago: the pilots on your Alaska flight practiced engine failures and ice storms in a flight simulator first. Embodied AI does the same at enormous scale, practicing in simulation, a computer-built world where crashing costs nothing.

The newest simulators are world models: AI that has learned enough about how the physical world behaves to generate realistic new situations and predict what happens next. A chatbot predicts the next word; it knows "the glass fell and…" usually ends with "shattered." A world model predicts the next moment: the glass falling and breaking. It predicts consequences, not words. An engineer can type "a mattress falls off a truck on the freeway at night, in the rain" and get a scene no real car has recorded. The catch is the sim-to-real gap: a simulator can only imagine variations on what it learned from, so practice there isn't proof.

Put real driving and simulation together and you get a flywheel: a loop that's hard to start but keeps its own momentum. Real use produces data, the data trains better models (including the world model), the world model invents rare situations to practice, and the better system gets used more. Each lap makes the next cheaper, which is why an early leader can be hard to catch even when its methods are public.

World models may matter far beyond cars. Remember Yann LeCun, who taught a network to read checks? In late 2025 he left his job running Meta's AI research lab to start a company called AMI Labs, which raised $1.03 billion in March 2026 to build world models, before it had a product. His argument: chatbots learn from text, but a four-year-old learns that cups fall and balls roll behind couches by watching the world, and AI will need to learn that way too. The biggest AI companies are still putting most of their money into bigger language models. Both sides could be partly right, and the self-driving car is where world models got tested first.

2.6 The long tail: why the last few percent is the hard part

The toy in 2.2 showed it: a network is only as good as what it has seen. In almost any real activity, a few common situations make up most of what happens, and a huge number of rare ones each happen almost never. Plot them and you get a tall spike and a long, thin tail: the long tail.

You've met it outside cars. A spam filter catches the usual scams, then a new kind slips through. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol four games to one, and in the game it lost, one very unusual move threw it off for a stretch. For driving, the tail is a school bus with its stop arm at an odd angle, a person in a costume, a flooded street that looks like pavement. Each is rare, but a fleet driving millions of miles a week meets some every day.

So "right 99.99 percent of the time" isn't a finish line, because the leftover fraction is multiplied by every mile. Don't judge a system by the green light. Ask what it does with the costume and the flood, because that's where the next recall comes from.

2.7 Where the human stands: in, on, and out of the loop

Back in 2.1 we noticed that the loop has no step where a person is asked. Every embodied AI makes a choice about that, and there are three places a person can stand. People call this question human in the loop, and it has three answers.

In the loop: the AI suggests, a person decides, and nothing happens without that person's sign-off. This is your Alaska flight. Alaska Airlines worked with a company called Air Space Intelligence to build Flyways, an AI system that studies routes and flags better ones for the airline's human dispatchers. Alaska says it saves tens of thousands of flight hours and roughly a million gallons of fuel a year, and the FAA has since hired the same company, on an $875 million contract, to help modernize the national airspace. The design choice is the whole point: Flyways recommends. Licensed dispatchers accept or reject every suggestion, and responsibility for each flight plan stays with people.

On the loop: the AI acts on its own, and a person watches, ready to take over. The cruise-control-plus-steering features in many new cars work this way: the car drives, and the manual tells you to keep your hands on the wheel and your eyes on the road. This sounds like the best of both worlds. It's often the worst seat in the house, because people are bad at staying alert while watching a system that's almost always right. Attention fades. And the moment the machine gives up and hands control back is, by definition, the hardest moment of the drive, arriving to a person who has had nothing to do for forty minutes. That trap has a name: the handoff problem. The question to ask about any on-the-loop design is not "is a person watching?" but "what happens in the three seconds after the machine says 'your turn'?"

Out of the loop: the AI acts, and no person is in position to step in at the moment it matters. A spam filter works this way, and nobody minds, because the stakes are low. A driverless taxi works this way too, on purpose. The bet is that removing an unreliable human beats keeping a badly placed one.

More human involvement isn't automatically safer. A person who really checks each decision is a strong safeguard; a person who clicks "approve" 400 times a day without reading is out of the loop wearing a name tag. The human's real position is wherever their attention is.

2.8 Responsible AI: what a company owes the street

Responsible AI means building and running AI so that its benefits are real, its risks are known and limited, and someone answers when it fails. Put a machine on a public street and that turns into four plain questions, each tied to an idea from this section.

Can anyone explain a mistake? Deep networks can't say why (2.2), so a responsible company relies on testing, records, and the ability to replay a failure and fix it.

Does it know where it's allowed to be confident? Because of the long tail (2.6), a responsible system has a proven zone called its operational design domain: which streets, speeds, weather, and hours. Outside it, the system should stop or ask for help. A small domain isn't a failure. Not knowing you've left it is.

Who keeps the score? Most of us trust these systems because they keep working, not because we understand them, the way we trust elevators. That's results-based trust, and it means whoever keeps the track record holds the power.

Who answers when it's wrong? There's no driver to ticket or sue. The reward function (2.4) and every setting after it were chosen by people, and someone, the company, the city that issued the permit, or both, has to own them.

1. A radiologist reads every scan; software highlights spots she might have missed.

In the loop. The software suggests, the radiologist decides. Nothing leaves the room without her sign-off.

2. A warehouse robot moves shelves on its own; a technician on the floor can hit a stop button.

Out of the loop in the moment, with a person on the loop for emergencies. The robot decides each move. The stop button is the handoff, and it only works if someone is looking.

3. Highway driving mode steers and brakes; the manual says to keep your hands on the wheel and eyes on the road.

On the loop, and the textbook handoff problem. The car does the work until it can't, and then it's yours, in the middle of the hard part.

4. A spam filter moves messages to a folder; you never see them unless you go looking.

Out of the loop. Low stakes, so nobody minds. "Out of the loop" isn't scary by itself; it gets scary when the action has mass.

5. An AI drafts replies to customer emails; an agent clicks send on each one.

In the loop, on paper. In practice, if the agent clicks send 400 times a day without reading, it's out of the loop with a human-shaped rubber stamp. The position is where the attention is, not where the button is.

The case: Waymo and the empty driver's seat

Background The future arrived city by city

For years, fully self-driving taxis were the technology that was always five years away. Then, quietly, they weren't. Waymo started in 2009 as Google's self-driving car project and is now a company owned by Google's parent, Alphabet. In September 2026 it opened service to the public in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa, which brought it to 14 U.S. cities where anyone with the app can ride with no one in the driver's seat. The company says it now gives more than 500,000 paid rides a week, and its stated goal is a million a week by the end of 2026. It's testing in London, too. In China, Baidu's Apollo Go and others run large fleets of their own.

It arrived one city at a time, often employees first, then a waitlist, then everyone. Here the human is out of the loop by design: no safety driver, no one steering from a distance. Let's take it apart with the five critical AI questions.

The five critical AI questions, run on this case:

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

The system is the Waymo Driver, Waymo's name for the software and sensors that drive its cars. It's embodied AI running the five-step loop from section 2.1, many times a second, on a fleet of thousands of cars. Every idea from section 2 shows up inside it.

Seeing (2.2 and 2.3). Each car fuses cameras, lidar, and radar. Deep networks, including CNNs, turn that into a live 3D picture with every object labeled and placed, and prediction networks attach likely futures to each one, all learned from millions of labeled examples collected since 2009.

Deciding (2.4). The planner is trained largely with reinforcement learning in simulation, against a reward function the company doesn't publish. We know the scoring matters because Waymo changes it. Early Waymos were famously timid, which blocked traffic, so in 2025 the company made them more "confidently assertive," as NPR reported. Soon after, a police sergeant in San Bruno, California, watched a Waymo make an illegal U-turn. Somebody turned a dial toward "take the gap," and the street felt it.

Practicing (2.5). In February 2026, Waymo introduced the Waymo World Model, built on Genie 3, a world model from Google DeepMind (AlphaGo's lab) that learned how the world looks and moves from huge amounts of ordinary video. It generates both camera and lidar views of scenes that never happened, controlled with plain-language prompts.

The flywheel, Waymo's version. Put it together and you get section 2.5's flywheel running at full speed. The real fleet drives millions of miles a week. In Waymo's own description, a separate AI it calls the Critic flags moments where the Driver did something less than ideal, like braking a beat late or hesitating too long. Engineers and AI systems produce better alternatives, and the Driver practices them in simulation against the World Model. A safety check has to confirm the new version is actually better before it ships to the fleet, which then drives more real miles. This is the real answer to "why can't a competitor just copy them?" The methods are published. The laps aren't.

The coverage map (2.8). Waymo defines an operational design domain for each city and earns it a piece at a time: which roads, speeds, weather, and hours. At the edge, the car stops, or it asks.

Where the human is (2.7). When a car meets something it can't sort out, like a construction zone its map doesn't know, it can pull over and send a question to a person Waymo calls a fleet response agent: "is this lane passable?" The person answers, and the car drives. These agents give advice. They don't steer. In February 2026, Waymo's chief safety officer told a Senate committee that some of those agents work overseas, in the Philippines, which set off a congressional inquiry. So the honest picture is "out of the loop while driving, on the loop for one question at a time."

Now the AI systems model from Chapter 1, run on Waymo:

  • Data: hundreds of millions of real driverless miles, millions of hand-labeled scenes, and billions of simulated miles, including scenes the World Model invents.
  • Model: deep networks that see and predict, plus a planner trained with reinforcement learning against a reward function the public never sees.
  • Output: a steering angle, a speed, a path, many times a second.
  • Feedback loop: every mile becomes training data, every flagged mistake becomes practice, every recall becomes a software update. That's the flywheel.
  • Humans: riders, fleet response agents who answer questions but don't drive, engineers who set the reward function, regulators, and everyone else on the street, who never agreed to be part of the test.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

Waymo exists to sell rides without paying drivers and, by its own account, to crash far less than people do. Whether that's a public health breakthrough or a job killer depends on whom you ask, and the honest answer is that it's both, for different people.

The case for: a public health argument. About 36,600 people died on U.S. roads in 2025, by NHTSA's early estimate, roughly a full airliner every day. A trauma surgeon who reviewed Waymo's safety data put it in one line: "If this was a disease, we would have declared war". Waymo's own analysis of about 271 million driverless miles through June 2026 reports 95 percent fewer crashes causing serious or worse injuries than human drivers on the same roads, and 82 percent fewer crashes with any injury. That's the company's count on its own coverage map, and Question 4 takes it apart. It's also a very large effect. If it's anywhere near right, a city that delays robotaxis for a year is choosing crashes it will never see or count, so "wait and see" isn't a free option.

The other case for: what riders are buying. NBC News interviewed women who switched to robotaxis after uncomfortable rides. What they describe buying is the absence of something they can't check before getting in: who is driving tonight, and who now knows their address. By Uber's own count, about one in five of its U.S. drivers are women, and in 2025 it launched a feature letting women riders ask for women drivers. That isn't an insult to a workforce; millions of rideshare trips happen safely every day. The rider on the curb at 1 a.m. just can't tell one trip from another ahead of time. There's an access story too: at Waymo's San Diego launch, the local president of the National Federation of the Blind said rideshare's unpredictability usually makes her anxious. The uncomfortable part: some of what riders value exists because the driver is gone, so "just keep the human drivers" isn't a clean answer either.

The case against: whose value, exactly? The people most affected aren't reading crash studies. They drive for a living, and ride-hail has been one of the biggest sources of flexible income for people with few other options. In Boston, councilors debated an ordinance requiring a "human safety operator" in every driverless car while drivers packed City Hall. In February 2026, New York's governor withdrew her proposal to allow robotaxis outside New York City. In London, a drivers' union declared a "state of emergency". Notice, using section 2.7, that Boston's idea puts the human back on the loop, the seat with the handoff problem. It may protect jobs. Whether it protects riders is a separate question.

You'll hear the counterargument for the rest of your life: we said this about elevator operators and bank tellers, and the work moved instead of vanishing. That's historically true (Chapter 9 takes it apart). It's also not a plan for a driver with rent due this month.

When the argument stops being an argument. In February 2024, a crowd in San Francisco's Chinatown set a driverless Waymo on fire with a firework. In June 2025, during protests against immigration raids in Los Angeles, people summoned several cars and burned five of them, and some called them spy cars, since the cars record constantly and police have asked for footage. Look at how it happened: the cars didn't drive away, because they're built never to push through a crowd. The safety feature nearly everyone agrees is right is exactly what left them defenseless. These were crimes (prosecutors brought arson charges in Chinatown), and they're also evidence about who felt they had nowhere else to complain. A fair account holds both.

The business term here is value capture: who keeps the gains a technology creates, and who absorbs its costs. With Waymo, the gains go to riders, the company, and everyone not hit by a car they otherwise would have been, people who never find out who they are. The costs land on a named, organized group of drivers who know exactly who they are. Spread-out benefits and concentrated costs decide permit votes more than any crash statistic does.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

This is where responsible AI (section 2.8) becomes real events. Flyways' failures would be invisible. Waymo's happen in public, get filmed, and get reported, which is bad for the company's week and good for everyone else's information. Three recent cases:

The school buses: the long tail, worked. In fall 2025, Waymo cars were caught on video driving past stopped school buses, which is illegal because children may be crossing. The Austin school district documented 19 such incidents in a few months. Waymo filed a voluntary software recall, and its chief safety officer said that "holding the highest safety standards means recognizing when our behavior should be better." No one was hurt. In January 2026, the National Transportation Safety Board opened its own investigation. A stopped school bus with its stop arm out is rare in the data and very high in stakes, which is the long tail in one picture. The system had seen many buses. It hadn't learned every angle of a stop arm the way a person who rode buses as a kid knows it without thinking. The fix was a software update that moved one more case out of the tail.

The assertive update: the specification problem. The 2025 change that made the cars more "confidently assertive" (Question 1) fixed one problem, cars too polite to merge, and created another: lane changes without signaling and that illegal U-turn in San Bruno. As NPR's reporting noted, police can't hand a ticket to a car with nobody in it. That's section 2.4's specification problem on a real street: the company scored what it measured, and the car found a way to score well that nobody intended.

Santa Monica: the hardest kind of case. On January 23, 2026, during morning drop-off near an elementary school in Santa Monica, a child ran into the street from behind a double-parked SUV, outside the crosswalk, and was struck by a Waymo. The child had minor injuries, stood up, and walked to the sidewalk, and Waymo called 911. According to the company, the car braked hard from about 17 miles per hour to under 6 before contact. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened an investigation into whether the car was cautious enough given where it was: two blocks from a school, at drop-off time, surrounded by children. Hold both halves of that. Hard braking almost certainly made the injury smaller. And the question regulators asked is the right one: in a place where a child might appear from behind any parked car, should the car have been going slower before anyone appeared? That's a judgment about the operational design domain, not about perception.

Waymo in the wild. Not every failure is dangerous. Some are just strange, and they teach something the serious ones don't: the world is weirder than any training data, and the people in it are weirder still.

"Why is this thing going in a circle?"

In December 2024, a passenger headed to the Phoenix airport watched his Waymo loop a parking lot again and again while he filmed and talked to support. When he asked the support agent to just take over, she said she didn't have an option to control the car. He made his flight. A software update fixed the bug.

The lesson: out of the loop means out of the loop. The person on the phone could advise, not drive.

A parking lot that honked all night

In August 2024, neighbors of a San Francisco lot where Waymos park between rides were woken at 4 a.m. by the cars honking at each other. The honk came from a sensible rule: beep if another car backs toward you slowly. Fill a lot with cars that all follow that rule and they set each other off.

The lesson: a behavior that's right for one machine can be wrong for a fleet. Scale is its own kind of long tail.

Pulled over, with nobody to ticket

The San Bruno U-turn ended with a police officer, flashing lights, and an empty car. Traffic tickets are written to drivers. There wasn't one.

The lesson: our laws were written around a human in the driver's seat. Take the human out and the question "who answers for this?" (section 2.8) needs a new answer.

Notice what these three have in common. None was a failure of the car's vision. The car wasn't confused about what a parking lot is. They were failures of system design: the rules, the defaults, and the assumptions about how a product behaves in the whole messy world. Deep learning makes the car able to drive. It doesn't automatically make the product ready for everything.

Business implications What this means for a company that puts AI in the physical world

Most of you won't build a car, but more companies are putting AI where it can move things: warehouse robots, drones, forklifts, farm machines. Three lessons:

Choose the loop position on purpose. The default many companies drift into, "the machine does it and someone keeps an eye on it," is the handoff problem with a payroll line, and it fails at the worst moment. Either keep a person as a real gate, like Flyways, or engineer the system to be safe without one, like Waymo, and know which you chose.

The track record is the product. A robotaxi company sells safety statistics as much as rides. How you count, and who gets to check the count, is a business decision that customers, regulators, and juries will all read.

Roll out by coverage map, and let the flywheel do the rest. Waymo expanded one operational design domain at a time, which is slower and far cheaper than one national mistake. And its methods are mostly public; its laps aren't. Every year of real driving makes the simulator better, which makes the next year cheaper.

This playbook isn't just for cars. BMW has been testing humanoid robots from a company called Figure on its Spartanburg, South Carolina, factory floor, starting with simple, repeatable jobs: a small operational design domain. Homes are much harder, because a home is all long tail. When the startup 1X began selling its NEO home robot, reviewers found the hard chores were done with a company employee in a VR headset steering it remotely, seeing through its cameras. That's a person in the loop, inside your house. Run Question 4 on that before Question 5.

The costs of doing it this way: slower launches, outside checks you don't control, and staff on the ground even when the robots rarely need them. The alternative cost is the video.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

Start with the headline number. "Fewer crashes per mile than humans" is Waymo's analysis of Waymo's data. Read it the way section 2.8 taught you. It compares the robotaxi's crashes, on the roads it chose, in the weather it handles, counted by the company, against a human average that includes drunk, distracted, and exhausted drivers, counted by regulators. Every clause in that sentence is a Question 4. None of it makes the claim false. All of it makes the claim smaller than the headline.

Santa Monica shows the same problem in a single event. Waymo said its own computer model showed that a fully attentive human driver in the same spot would have hit the child at about 14 miles per hour instead of under 6. Maybe so. But who built that model, who checked it, and could anyone outside rerun it? A comparison against an imaginary human, built by the company being investigated, is exactly the kind of claim this question exists for.

Then ask who keeps the count. Mostly, the company. In February 2026, Senator Ed Markey asked seven self-driving companies how often their cars need help from remote assistants. According to his office's report, every one declined to say. That's the most basic number for knowing how "driverless" a driverless car is, and the public doesn't have it.

And the public is not convinced. In AAA's 2025 survey, six in ten U.S. drivers said they'd be afraid to ride in a self-driving vehicle, and only 13 percent said they'd trust one. The coverage map is growing faster than the trust map. Underneath the numbers is a question no dataset answers: who gets to decide whether the experiment runs on a given street? Right now the answer is a company's expansion plan plus a state permit, and the parents at the bus stop aren't asked. That's Chapter 11's question arriving early.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep it where its track record is long and independently checked. The cost: you're trusting a count someone else keeps.
  • Fix it with a permit whose conditions you can price: crash and remote-assistance reporting to the city, lower speeds near schools at drop-off time, a fleet cap until the numbers are audited, a plan for the drivers it replaces. Each costs the company money and the city staff time, and Wednesday's pitch has to decide which ones to offer.
  • Drop it from your city, and the cost is the crashes humans will keep causing, which is a real number too, just one nobody films.
How to make it good

Keep robotaxis where the record is long and someone independent keeps the count. Fix the permit so its conditions can be priced: crash, disengagement, and remote-assistance reporting to the city; limits where the long tail lives (school zones at drop-off, weather the car hasn't proven); a fleet cap until the numbers are audited; and a transition plan for the drivers it replaces. Drop the on-the-loop design that asks a person to stay alert for a handoff that comes mid-crisis. The cost is slower expansion for the company, staff time for the city, and a count that someone other than the company now has to keep.

Thinking critically The five critical AI questions, on this case, by you

Thinking critically · for Monday's discussion · one question each, answered by you, not by the chapter
  1. What is it, and what does it do? Pick one embodied AI you've seen in person (a robot vacuum, a self-checkout camera, a delivery robot, a car's lane-keeping). Walk it through the five steps of the loop in section 2.1, and say which step you think fails most often, and why.
  2. Why does it exist, and who benefits? A driverless car and a human-driven car cause identical minor crashes on the same street. Who benefits from each design, who is accountable in each case, and name one way the driverless version is better and one way it's worse.
  3. What happens when it fails, and who finds out? The school-bus recall happened because the failure was filmed. Name a failure a robotaxi could have that nobody would film. Who would find out, how, and does the coverage map make it more or less likely?
  4. Who's saying that, and how would we know? Waymo's safety numbers are Waymo's numbers, and no company would tell a senator how often its cars need human help. What specific, independent evidence would you want before letting a robotaxi drive your younger sibling to school? Name the data, who should keep it, and why they don't now.
  5. Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? If robotaxis applied to operate in Bellingham next year: approve, deny, or approve with conditions? Write one condition with its cost to the company and its cost to the city. Keep it; on Wednesday you'll find out whether the evidence moves you.
The takeaway from the case

Same industry, two designs, opposite failure economics. The dispatcher's invisible mistake and the robotaxi's filmed one are both real; only one gets fixed in public. Under the hood, the car is section 2 come to life: networks that learned to see, a planner that learned to decide from a scoring sheet, a world model that rehearses the tail. The question the case leaves you with isn't whether driverless cars are safe. It's who gets to say so, and what you'd need to see to believe them.

4

Decide: the pitch is real

Deciding, this week, is literal: cities and states actually vote on this. Robotaxis need permits, and communities set conditions or say no, so "should driverless cars operate here?" belongs to city councils, not just companies. Wednesday's lab flips your seat. You're a strategy analyst at a robotaxi company, and your job is to pitch Bellingham's city council. A good pitch can't just repeat the safety numbers. It has to answer the hard questions from this chapter before the council asks them: who keeps the count, what happens near schools, and what you'll do for the drivers. Bring your Monday positions; you'll need to decide which conditions to offer and what each one costs the company.

Three takeaways.

The hard part is seeing, not driving. Embodied AI runs a simple loop, but steps 2 and 3, naming what's out there and guessing what it will do, carry almost all the difficulty. They are learned from examples by networks nobody can fully read.

The tail is where it breaks. A system can be excellent at the common case and still fail every day at scale on the rare one. Simulation and world models are how companies practice the tail, and the sim-to-real gap is why practice isn't proof.

Where the human stands is a choice with a price. In, on, or out of the loop, each seat costs something. The middle one looks safest and often isn't. And whoever keeps the track record holds the power, so decide who that should be.

Key terms

Embodied AI

AI that senses the physical world and acts in it through sensors and motors. Also called physical AI. A mistake has mass and momentum.

Neural network

Layers of simple nodes connected by weights. Each node weighs its inputs, adds them up, and passes one number on.

Deep learning

Learning with a neural network that has many hidden layers. Nobody writes the rules, which is why the system can't explain itself.

Convolutional neural network (CNN)

A network for images that slides small pattern spotters across a picture, building edges into shapes and shapes into objects.

Reinforcement learning

Learning from scored outcomes instead of labeled answers: try, get a score, adjust. How AlphaGo learned Go and how planners learn to drive.

World model

AI that has learned how the physical world behaves well enough to generate new situations and predict what happens next. It predicts consequences, not words.

Flywheel

A loop where real use produces data, data improves the system, and the better system gets used more. Each lap makes the next one cheaper.

The long tail

The rare situations that hold almost none of the miles and almost all of the failures. Where every recall comes from.

In / on / out of the loop

The three places a person can stand: gatekeeper, supervisor, absent. The middle seat is harder than it sounds.

The handoff problem

Machine-to-human handoffs fail exactly when they matter most, because the person has had nothing to do until the hardest moment.

Responsible AI

Building and running AI so the benefits are real, the risks are known and limited, and someone answers when it fails.

Operational design domain

The roads, speeds, weather, and hours a system is approved to handle, with set behavior at the edge. The future arrives with a coverage map.

Value capture

Who keeps a technology's gains and who absorbs its costs. Spread-out benefits and concentrated costs decide permit votes.
