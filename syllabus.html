2

What this course is

AI is already in your life: the spam filter on your inbox, the bank watching your card, the software reading your résumé, the feed deciding what you see next. This course is not about building it. It's about seeing it clearly enough to decide what to do about it, as a worker, a customer, and a citizen. The question the whole quarter carries: can we build a hybrid society, one where humans and AI systems are tangled together, that still works for the humans in it?

The course runs on a free digital textbook written for it: this website. Every chapter is anchored on a real system at a real moment (EveryCure's drug search, Amazon's scrapped hiring tool, Waymo's crash data, the lawyer who cited phantom cases, the New Hampshire robocall, the "canaries" study on entry-level jobs), and every chapter reads its case through the same method: See it, question it, decide. See it means naming the five parts of any AI system: data, model, output, feedback loop, humans. Question it means asking the same five questions of every system, every week: what is it and what does it do; why does it exist and who benefits; what happens when it fails and who finds out; who's saying that and how would we know; and keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost. Decide means taking a position you can defend: keep it, fix it, or drop it.

This course takes a critical approach to AI, and "critical" doesn't mean negative. It means looking closely instead of taking anyone's word for it: not the companies', not the critics', not this book's. Critical AI literacy is the skill of knowing what an AI system is, what it's for, how it fails, who benefits from your believing what you're told about it, and when to use it and when not to. That last part is the one nobody teaches, and it's the one this course is built around. The book is not pro-AI and not anti-AI. It's pro-thinking critically about AI, and it says so on page one.

The labs are where you do it yourself: train a tiny neural network on your own drawings, audit a résumé screener, sit on a city council deciding whether to permit robotaxis, run a firm through an AI reorganization, and verify a docket of court citations against a chatbot's inventions.

One disclosure, made on the first page of the book and repeated here: this textbook was drafted with the help of Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, a company that appears in the book's cases. That is a conflict of interest. Chapter 8 explains the choice; you are allowed to hold it against the book while reading closely.

Learning outcomes and how they are assessed

D = Monday discussion · L = labs · Q = reading quizzes · E = midterm and final · P = the Prototype project. By the end of the course you should be able to:

  1. Explain what makes software "AI" rather than a hand-written recipe, and classify any system as predictive, generative, or embodied/agentic, saying why the kind changes how it fails (D, Q, E).
  2. Name the five parts of any AI system and locate a failure in the right part (D, L, E).
  3. Run the five questions on a system you have never seen before (D, L, E, P).
  4. Tell a slogan from a claim: identify what a warning or a promise about AI would need to be checkable, and check it (D, L, P).
  5. Explain, without math, how a system learned what it knows: training versus use, why the data's gaps become the system's gaps, and why confidence is not accuracy (Q, E).
  6. Audit an AI system for bias, error, or harm using a controlled test, and report what you found in plain language (L).
  7. Defend a decision about an AI system, keep it, fix it, or drop it, with the costs of each option named (D, L, P).
  8. Use AI tools as a tutor and a subject of study without outsourcing your judgment, and say plainly when and how you used them (L).

The weekly rhythm

The quarter starts on a Wednesday, so Week 1 has one session: Lab 0, a hands-on start with no reading due, and a short Lab 0 assignment on Canvas that Friday. From Week 2 on, every week has the same shape (midterm week and Veterans Day week are the exceptions, marked on the schedule):

Weekend: read the chapter. That's the whole ask. Come Monday with one thing you didn't buy.

Monday, 110 minutes: the chapter argued: the case, how the system works, the five critical AI questions in groups, and where you land.

Wednesday, 110 minutes: a short recap of Monday, then the lab, in class, in pairs at the lab computers, then a debate about the system you just ran.

Friday, 11:59 p.m.: the lab writeup and the week's reading quiz, both on Canvas. The writeup's last line is your commitment card, one sentence you actually keep.

Nothing is due on a weekend. The midterm is Wednesday, October 21. The last class is Wednesday, December 2. The final exam is Thursday, December 10, at 10:30 a.m.

Mondays: discussion, and why attendance counts

Attendance is required, and here is why, since you deserve a reason and not just a rule. This course is built on discussion: instead of listening to a lecture about AI, you spend most of every session arguing about a real system with other people. Monday opens with the case, told rather than read, then groups of four run the five questions on the board, and then the room takes a position and names its cost. None of it can be made up from the book, because the learning happens in the arguing.

That design is not a preference. The largest review of college teaching in science and business, 225 studies published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2014, found that students in active-learning classes scored about half a letter grade higher and were a third less likely to fail than students in traditional lectures. So the attendance points aren't a reward for showing up. They pay for the part of the course that works best.

Attendance is credited by your notes. Each class day has a Canvas assignment that opens when class starts and closes right after class ends. Before you leave, upload whatever notes you took: a photo of the page, a few typed lines. A single word counts. What matters is that it was uploaded from the room while class was happening. Seventeen class days, full credit at fifteen, so two absences cost nothing and need no explanation. A documented absence longer than three days is the one case handled by hand; email me within a week of returning.

House rules, borrowed from 924 Gilman Street. The Berkeley club where Green Day started has run since 1986 on a hand-painted sign of rules, each four words or fewer, changed only by vote at the members' meeting. This room argues about jobs, politics, war, and whether the machines are coming for us, so it has the same rules, with each line translated for a classroom:

  • NO RACISM
  • NO SEXISM
  • NO HOMOPHOBIA
  • NO TRANSPHOBIA
  • NO FIGHTING Attacking someone's position without evidence. "That's wrong" is fighting; "that fails because" is arguing, and arguing is the point.
  • NO STAGEDIVING The sudden lunge: interrupting, piling on, or ambushing the one person holding a position. They get room, and they get the room's best counter, not its loudest.
  • NO MAJOR LABEL BANDS No selling out to a company because you like its product. Liking a system is not evidence about it. If you work for or own stock in a company we discuss, say so first; the book does.

The first four are Gilman's rules, verbatim, and they line up with the university's equal-opportunity policy in section 14. If something comes up that the rules don't cover, let me know and we'll add to them. The rules and the full text live on the Rules page.

Each chapter's case ends with the five critical AI questions for you to answer, and they can't be answered from the text. They are never submitted. They are what Monday and Wednesday are for. You are welcome to use AI to prepare: to test an argument, to find the counter-argument, to check a fact. What you say in the room is yours.

8

Wednesdays and the labs

Chapters 1 through 9 have labs, plus Lab 0 on the first day; Lab 2 covers Chapters 2 and 3. Week 10 is the project's week. Week 3 covers Chapter 2 on Monday and Chapter 3 on Wednesday; Lab 2 covers both chapters, the chatbot that seems to care and the feed that learns what holds you. The labs run on Wednesdays in class, in pairs at the lab computers: a short recap of Monday, then about an hour of work, then a debate: each lab ends with a real argument about the system you just ran, two sides, the cost of each, and you switch sides halfway. Lab 8 (The Red Lines) starts on Monday of Veterans Day week as the discussion itself and is finished at home, with the writeup due that Friday. One lab runs take-home: Lab 5 (The Footprint Machine) in midterm week. Week 10 has no lab; the Prototype project is that week's work. Each lab ends with a verdict you screenshot and a commitment card, one sentence about what you'll do differently, that the next session asks about.

For the nine graded lab writeups, the writeup is due Friday at 11:59 p.m. on Canvas: exactly five items, listed at the end of every lab, the first of which is always your screenshot. Credit is for a complete, on-time writeup that answers what was asked; the lab's own verdict already told you how you did. Writeups are individual even though the lab is done in pairs: same screenshots, your own words.

Keep everything you make. A hiring audit, a robotaxi permit with priced conditions, and a citation docket you verified by hand are all work you can describe on a résumé.

9

Quizzes, exams, and the Prototype project

Reading quizzes. Five questions on Canvas, open book, covering that week's chapter, due Friday at 11:59 p.m. along with the lab writeup. Every question is a situation to classify with the chapter's core distinction: seems or is, predictive or generative, engagement or wellbeing, proxy or pretext. If you read the chapter and came to class, you'll get them. Nine quizzes, lowest dropped, no extensions.

Midterm. Wednesday, October 21, in class on Canvas, closed book, no AI. Chapters 1 through 4 and Labs 0 through 4, in multiple choice plus four short-answer case questions. The multiple-choice questions are short scenarios, not definitions; each case question gives you a small system you haven't seen and asks you to run the course's tools on it. It covers the five moments, the five parts, the five questions, and what each case actually showed. That Monday is Chapter 5's case plus about 30 minutes of review, and Lab 5 is take-home that week.

Final exam. Thursday, December 10, 10:30 a.m., in our classroom, cumulative. Chapters 5 through 12 and their labs in full, plus the whole course at the level of its main ideas: the five moments, the AI systems model, the five critical AI questions, and what each case company got right or wrong. Roughly half new material and half looking back. Same format as the midterm: in class on Canvas, closed book, no AI, multiple choice plus four short-answer case questions.

The Prototype project. In teams of four, formed by Friday, October 30 (the four names and a one-paragraph rough idea go on Canvas that day), design your own AI system. Browse the Rundown AI Workflow Hub for inspiration, pick a real problem, and write a spec using the five parts: data, model, output, feedback loop, and humans. Then run the five questions on your own design and decide what to keep, fix, or drop, with the cost of each. Building a working version is optional, with Claude, another tool, or no AI at all. In the last week, each team gives a five-minute pitch with four slides or fewer, plus two minutes of questions: half the teams on Monday, November 30, after the Chapter 10 and 11 cases, and half on Wednesday, December 2. Pitch order is announced in Week 9, and every team's slides should be ready by Monday. The three-page paper and slides are due on Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, December 4, along with a short individual reflection and a rating of your teammates. It is graded on whether the design survives the questions, not on whether you built it. The full guide is on the Prototype project page.

The last class. Wednesday, December 2: the last five Prototype project pitches, then Chapter 12 and the closing conversation, where you re-answer the seven claims from Week 1. Your Lab 0 answers come back out.

AI policy: it depends on what the assignment is for

This is a course about AI, so you'll use it constantly, and the rule changes with what an assignment is for. One principle sits under all of it: learn it first, then use AI. In your career you'll use AI all the time, and you should. But while you're learning how something works, do the thinking yourself first. Once you understand it, AI can speed you up. If you use it before you understand, you can't tell when it's wrong. Three settings:

SettingYou mayYou may not
TutorExplain a concept, quiz you, find the counter-argument, check a fact, help you prepare for discussion.Hand it the deliverable. What you submit is yours.
SubjectUse the AI as the lab tells you to: probe it, audit it, argue with it, verify it. The lab states its own rule at the top.Ask it the thing the lab exists to teach you to check yourself (Chapter 10's rule: never ask a chatbot whether a case is real).
OffNothing.Any use: the midterm and the final.

Where each applies: reading the chapters, the reading quizzes, and preparing for discussion are Tutor (the quizzes are open book; a chatbot that answers them for you is the calculator test failed). Every lab is Subject, with its rule printed in the lab. The lab writeups and the Prototype project are Tutor for thinking and Off for writing: the words are yours. Building a Prototype project prototype is the one place any tool is fine. The exams are Off.

The test throughout is the calculator test: if the tool does the thing the assignment exists to teach, it isn't allowed on that assignment. Whenever you use AI on anything you submit, say so in the submission: which tool, for what part. Undisclosed AI use where it is restricted is academic dishonesty. The book discloses its own AI use on page one; you're held to the same standard.

The fine print

The rules that settle most arguments before they start.

Absences. The two dropped days are your excused absences; use them for whatever comes up, no documentation needed. A documented absence longer than two days is handled by hand; email me within a week of returning.

Arriving late, leaving early. The notes window is open for the whole class and closes right after it ends, so arriving late still earns the point as long as you upload before you go. Leaving early means missing the window. Uploading from outside the room is academic dishonesty, and Canvas records where and when submissions arrive.

Late work is measured by Canvas. Everything is due at 11:59 p.m. on its due date, except your class notes, which are due right after class. The Canvas timestamp is the submission time. Days late are counted in 24-hour blocks from the deadline, weekends included.

Exam conditions. In class, closed book, closed notes, no devices. Arrive on time; a student who arrives after the first student has finished cannot sit the exam. A documented emergency is the only makeup; talk to me before the exam if at all possible. The final's date and time are set by the university and cannot be moved.

Regrades. Ask within seven days of the grade being posted, in writing, saying which item and why. A regrade reviews the whole item, so the grade can go down as well as up.

Rounding. Final percentages are rounded to the nearest whole number once, at the end (89.5 rounds to 90; 89.4 does not). There is no extra credit and no end-of-quarter negotiation.

Devices in class. Laptops and tablets are welcome for the labs and for notes. Phones go away for discussion. Do not record class without permission.

Email. Use your WWU address, put MIS 201 in the subject, and expect a reply within one business day.
