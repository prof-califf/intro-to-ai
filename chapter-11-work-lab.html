The Exposure Audit

The chapter told us where the pressure lands: automating work built on codified knowledge. This lab turns that finding into an instrument — and points it at the job you actually want.

One real job posting · your own classification · a live check of the data

1

Pick a real job

Not a job category. A job. Go to a job board and find one real posting for a role you would genuinely want in the next few years. Copy the title and the employer. Then read the posting closely and pull out six to eight concrete tasks the role actually involves — not the buzzwords, the work. "Prepare monthly variance reports." "Run client onboarding calls." "Respond to vendor escalations."

Why a real posting

The Canaries study measures occupations. But nobody is hired into an occupation — we're hired into a specific role at a specific company, and the same job title can sit on either side of the automate/complement line depending on how that employer built it. The posting is the evidence. The title is just the label.

2

Classify the tasks

For each task, make two calls — the same two distinctions the researchers found in the data.

Knowledge type. Is this task built on codified knowledge (you could learn it from documentation, a textbook, a standard procedure) or tacit knowledge (you'd only get good at it through practice, mentorship, and real situations)?

AI's likely role. If this employer applied AI to the task, would it automate it (the system does the work, a person maybe checks) or complement it (the system extends what the person can do, but the person's judgment stays central)?

Use the tool below. Your calls are yours to defend — that's the point. The tool doesn't save anything, so screenshot your finished audit for the writeup.

What the profile means — and doesn't

Codified + automating tasks are where the study found young workers' employment falling. Tacit + complementing tasks are where it held or grew. But this is your classification of one posting, not a verdict on your future. Exposure measures overlap, not destiny — and a role heavy on exposed tasks today can be rebuilt around complement work tomorrow. The question is whether this employer is doing that.

3

Check the instrument

Now compare your read against the live data. Open the Canaries Dashboard — it updates monthly with the same payroll data behind the chapter.

a

Find the occupation group closest to your job. Note its AI exposure level and what employment for workers 22–25 has done since late 2022.

b

Write down the current month's numbers. In week 11 we check them again as a class — the gap was 15% in mid-2025 and 19% in mid-2026, and part of this lab is watching whether it moves while we're watching.

c

Compare: does the dashboard's picture of your occupation match your task-level audit? If they disagree, don't assume the dashboard wins. Occupations average over thousands of very different roles. Figure out which one you trust more here, and why.

3½

Hold the rival hypothesis

Before you write your verdict, do what the economists in this week's NPR story do to each other: attack your own reading. Suppose the pattern you just checked on the dashboard is real but AI isn't the cause — the New York Fed's remote-work explanation, for instance. Write one sentence for each: what your job's exposure profile predicts if AI is the driver, and what it predicts if remote work is. Then name the single piece of future dashboard evidence that would tell the two apart. If you can't name one, that's a finding too — it means the honest answer this month is "we don't know yet," and you'll be one of very few people in any room who can say exactly why.

4

Run the four questions

Same ritual as every week — this time aimed at the role you chose.

1

What kind of AI is this? What AI, if any, does the posting mention — and what kind is it actually describing?

2

Where is the human? In this role, is the person in the loop, on the loop, or being designed out? Use your task audit as evidence.

3

Who is accountable when it fails? If AI in this role produces a bad output that ships, who answers for it — the new hire reviewing it, or someone above them?

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Would a failure in this role be visible immediately, or surface quietly months later? What does that mean for the person holding the job?

5

The writeup

Due Friday afternoon · post to Canvas
  1. The posting. Title, employer, link, and your task list with classifications (screenshot of your audit is fine).
  2. The profile. What did your exposure profile show, and where were your two hardest classification calls? Defend them.
  3. The instrument. What did the dashboard show for your occupation this month, and did it match your audit? Which did you trust more, and why?
  4. The four questions. Your answers, with the posting as evidence.
  5. Your move. One specific thing you will do before graduation to build tacit knowledge for this role — something with real situations in it, not another credential. Name it. This becomes your commitment card Wednesday.

One honest note to close on. This lab points a measuring tool at something personal, and the numbers may not be comforting. That's not a reason to skip the measurement. The whole argument of this book is that seeing a system clearly beats being surprised by it — and that goes double when the system is the one deciding whether to open the door.
