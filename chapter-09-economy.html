AI and the Economy

Hundreds of billions invested, hundreds of millions of users, and productivity numbers that have barely moved. Meanwhile, the first jobs to feel AI are the entry-level ones this room is about to apply for. This chapter asks two questions with one answer: where is the boom, and where are the jobs?

The paradox on the receipt

Here's the puzzle this chapter lives in. By any adoption measure, generative AI is one of the fastest-spreading technologies in history, with hundreds of millions of weekly users within a few years of launch. By investment measures, it's a once-in-a-generation buildout; we saw the data centers and nuclear deals in Chapter 5. And by the measure economists care about most, productivity, the needle has barely moved.

This isn't a new kind of embarrassment. In 1987 the economist Robert Solow joked that the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The economist Paul Krugman, whose essay "AI and the Pitfalls of Innovation" anchors this week, calls it a recurring pitfall: a transformative technology and a transformed economy are two different events, sometimes decades apart.

There's a second question in the room, and most of us walked in carrying it: what does all this mean for the jobs we're about to go get? This chapter treats the two questions as one. If AI's payoff depends on how work gets reorganized, then who gets hired is part of that reorganization, and the first place to look is the entry level.

Productivity (and why economists obsess over it)

Output per hour worked. In the long run it's close to everything: wages, living standards, and whether the next generation lives better than the last all ride on it. What people get wrong: they judge a technology by how many people use it or what its stock is worth. Economists judge it by this number, and this number is slow.

The concepts: why a technology pays late, and who feels it first

2.1 Productivity, and the paradox

Economists judge a technology by one number, and it isn't users or market value. Productivity is output per hour worked, and in the long run it's close to everything: wages, living standards, whether the next generation lives better than the last. The paradox in section 1 is that AI is racing on every measure except this one. That's not new. In 1987 Robert Solow said the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics, and he was right for roughly a decade before the number moved. The question this chapter asks is whether we're in that decade again, or in something else.

Productivity paradox

Transformative technology, flat statistics. What people get wrong: they resolve it too early, in either direction. "It's coming" and "it's a bubble" are both predictions about the same flat line, and the line hasn't told us yet.

2.2 General-purpose technologies, and the dynamo's forty years

The best history lesson for this moment is a hundred years old, and Krugman's essay leans on it for good reason. The economic historian Paul David studied what happened when American factories got electricity, and found that for decades, almost nothing happened.

Here's why. A steam-powered factory was built vertically around one giant engine, with power carried by belts and shafts through the whole building. When electric motors arrived, owners did the obvious thing: swapped the steam engine for a big electric one and kept everything else. Same building, same layout, same workflow, new power source. The gain was small, and it stayed small for about a generation.

The payoff came only when a new generation of managers stopped retrofitting and started redesigning: small motors on each machine, single-story factories laid out around the flow of work instead of the flow of power, assembly lines that were impossible in the old buildings. The technology hadn't improved much. The organization had finally been rebuilt around it, and productivity took off, forty years after the dynamo arrived.

The sentence to carry out of this chapter

A general-purpose technology pays off not when it's adopted, but when work is reorganized around it. Swapping the engine is fast. Redesigning the factory is slow, expensive, and disruptive, and it's where the gains were hiding.

General-purpose technology (the older GPT)

A technology that transforms nearly every industry rather than one: steam, electricity, computing. Economists used the term decades before it became a chatbot's initials. What people get wrong: they expect the payoff at installation. General-purpose technologies share a signature of enormous promise, long delays, and payoffs that arrive through reorganization.

2.3 The two margins: everyone's using it, firms aren't changing

If David's story is the right map, we should be able to locate ourselves on it. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab's AI Adoption Monitor tracks the two numbers that matter, and they're moving at very different speeds.

Individual adoption is fast. Surveys tracked by the Monitor find a large share of American workers already using generative AI, at rates that rival or beat the early PC and internet eras. Much of this use is informal: personal accounts, no permission asked.

Firm adoption is slow. The share of companies that have formally woven AI into how they produce things is a small fraction of workers' personal adoption, and much of what exists is shallow: a chatbot license, a pilot program, an engine swapped into an unchanged factory.

Economists split adoption into two margins. The extensive margin is how many people and firms use AI at all. The intensive margin is how deeply it's woven into actual workflows. The extensive margin looks like a revolution. The intensive margin looks like 1900: belts and shafts still on the ceiling, a shiny new motor bolted where the steam engine used to be.

Extensive vs. intensive margin

How many use it versus how deeply it's woven in. What people get wrong: they read the first as the second. A company where everyone has a chatbot license has adopted AI on the extensive margin only, which is the swapped engine.

2.4 Exposure, and the two ways to build a job

Now bring the dynamo down to the size of one job. Economists measure a job's AI exposure by asking how much of its tasks current systems could plausibly perform: low for electricians, nurses, and chefs; high for customer service, basic accounting, and entry-level software work. Exposure measures overlap, not destiny, because the same exposed job can be rebuilt two ways.

AI exposure

How much of a job's tasks current AI systems can plausibly perform, scored from low (electricians, nurses, chefs) to high (customer service, basic accounting, entry-level software work). What people get wrong: exposure is not destiny. It measures overlap between a job and the technology, not what employers will actually do.

Automating vs. complementing

The declines are concentrated where AI is used to automate tasks, where the system does the work and a person at most checks it. Where AI is used to complement workers, extending what a person can do, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers. Same technology, opposite outcomes. The difference is how the job is built around it, which is the dynamo lesson again, seen from the worker's side.

Automating vs. complementing use

Automating use: AI performs the task and the human role shrinks toward review. Complementing use: AI extends the person, drafting so they can edit, searching so they can judge. What people get wrong: they ask whether a job is "safe from AI." The better question is which way the employer is building the job.

Codified vs. tacit knowledge

The sharpest line in the study is one you can feel in your own education. Employment fell among young workers in jobs built on codified knowledge, the written-down kind you learn from textbooks and procedures. It rose among experienced workers in jobs built on tacit knowledge, the kind you get through practice and mentorship. That makes sense: language models trained on the world's written text reproduce written-down knowledge best. Reading a tense meeting, knowing which client actually decides, sensing a number is wrong before you can say why: that was never written down, so the models never trained on it.

The uncomfortable sentence

A college degree is largely a certificate of codified knowledge, and codified knowledge is what AI reproduces best. That doesn't make the degree worthless. It means the degree alone is no longer the differentiator it was. The differentiator is what we layer on top: judgment, experience, and the tacit knowledge no model trained on.

Try it · practice for Friday's quiz. Five situations: extensive or intensive margin, automating or complementing, codified or tacit? Decide, then click to check.
1. A law firm gives every associate a chatbot subscription and changes nothing else.

Extensive margin. Everyone has the tool; no workflow moved. The swapped engine.

2. A marketing team cuts its first-draft step entirely: the model writes, a senior editor approves, and the junior copywriter position isn't refilled.

Automating build, on the intensive margin. The workflow really did change, and the entry-level role is the thing that changed.

3. An analyst uses AI to pull and chart the data in minutes, then spends the afternoon in the client meeting explaining what it means.

Complementing build. The tool extended the person; the tacit part, the room, is where the job now lives.

4. A new hire can recite the whole procedures manual but can't tell which client is about to walk.

Codified knowledge without tacit. The manual is what the model trained on; the sense of the client is what it didn't.

5. A company reports 80 percent of employees use AI weekly and no change in output per employee.

The paradox in one company: extensive adoption, no intensive change, flat productivity. Section 2.3, in miniature.

The case: canaries in the coal mine

Background A lab, a payroll company, and a bird

Coal miners used to carry canaries underground. The birds were more sensitive to dangerous gas than the miners were, so if the canary stopped singing, you got out. The same Stanford lab borrowed the image for a study called "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts About the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence." The question: if AI is starting to change the job market, who feels it first?

The data source matters. Instead of surveys or predictions, the researchers, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen, used payroll records from ADP, a company that processes paychecks for millions of American workers. Real hires, real paychecks, month by month, since ChatGPT launched in late 2022. The August 2026 revision says two things at once, and we need to hold both.

First: there is no widespread, economy-wide job displacement from AI. Across the whole workforce, employment looks broadly normal.

Second: workers ages 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI are falling behind. Their employment now sits about 19% below where it would be if it had kept pace with less-exposed peers of the same age. Experienced workers in the same occupations show no comparable gap. A year earlier the number was 15%. It is widening. That second finding is about this room: the age group entering the job market in the next few years, in exactly the white-collar roles a business degree points toward.

What this AI is, and how it works Question 1: what is the AI system, and what does it do?

This week "AI" is an economic object rather than one product: a candidate general-purpose technology, like the dynamo, doing the tasks that used to fill someone's first job. The chatbot on your phone, the capital spending on Microsoft's books, and the missing entry-level posting are the same story at three scales. The system under the microscope, though, is the study itself, and it's worth seeing how it works, because a finding is only as good as its machinery.

One more detail: the gap doesn't come from firing. It comes from hiring. Companies aren't laying young workers off in large numbers. They're not opening the door in the first place. A wave of layoffs makes news. A hiring slowdown makes silence: fewer postings, longer searches, more "we went with another candidate." The canary doesn't drop. It just stops getting invited into the mine.

Who benefits, and who doesn't Question 2: why does it exist, and who benefits?

AI exists in workplaces to make work more productive, which pays the firm only when the work is reorganized around the tool. Who benefits: firms that reorganize, eventually; investors, if the boom arrives; experienced workers whose output multiplies, because the tacit layer they built is now the scarce thing. Who doesn't: the cohort that never gets the door opened, which is the study's darker point, and the towns and investors who built around a boom if the boom turns out to be a bubble. Notice who is missing from both lists: the companies selling the tools, who do fine either way. Sellers of shovels profit in every gold rush, real or imagined.

When it fails, who finds out Question 3: what happens when it fails, and who finds out?

Both failures here are absences. A productivity boom that never arrives shows up only in statistics years later, like the missed cure in Chapter 4. A job that never opens makes no news, because nobody was laid off. The canary doesn't drop; it stops getting invited into the mine. That's why the Adoption Monitor and the Canaries Dashboard matter more than any single headline: they make absences visible on a schedule, and they're the reason this chapter can be checked rather than believed.

Business implications What this means for a company deciding what to do with AI

The dynamo story is a warning to executives, and the Canaries study is its human cost. Three implications. First, a chatbot license is not a strategy. Buying the tool puts a firm on the extensive margin, which is nearly free and nearly worthless; the gains are in the redesign, which is slow, expensive, and punished by quarterly incentives, which is exactly why most firms haven't done it. Second, how a firm builds a job decides what happens to the people in it. The automating build is cheaper this quarter and hollows out the pipeline of people who will run the company in ten years; the complementing build costs more now and keeps the tacit knowledge that AI didn't train on. Third, the entry-level role was never just cheap labor. It was the company's training system, the way tacit knowledge got transferred, and firms that close the door are canceling their own apprenticeship program without noticing. The costs of doing this well: hiring the junior anyway and paying for training that used to happen by accident, and living through the dip while the redesign takes hold. The cost of doing it badly shows up in a decade, which is why almost nobody is measuring it.

Societal implications Question 4: who's saying that, and how would we know?

What if the bet is wrong?

The dynamo story says: be patient, the reorganization is coming, the boom follows. The same facts support a darker reading. The hundreds of billions flowing into data centers are a bet that reorganization will happen and that the gains will be enormous. If either half disappoints, the word for what we're in isn't "early." It's "bubble." Krugman's essay is about exactly this: innovation booms have repeatedly outrun the payoff, and the correction hits investors, workers, and towns that built around the boom.

Nobody knows which reading is right, and this book won't pretend otherwise. What we can do is name the evidence that would tell them apart. Watch the intensive margin: if firms deeply reorganize and productivity still doesn't move, the dynamo story weakens. Watch who profits in the meantime: sellers of shovels do fine in every gold rush, real or imagined.

Is AI actually causing the entry-level gap?

The Canaries researchers are careful: their findings are descriptive patterns, not causal estimates. The data shows the gap exists and is widening. It can't prove, on its own, that AI caused it. Interest rates, post-pandemic corrections, and remote work hit the same job market at the same time.

This is a live scientific fight. In an NPR story on struggling recent grads, one applicant describes 450 applications, 19 interviews, and no offers, and economists look at the same struggle and disagree about the cause. A New York Fed analysis found AI didn't explain the rise in young-grad unemployment; remote work fit better. Harvard's David Deming supplies a mechanism: training a junior hire from afar is costly, so remote work tilts hiring toward experienced people with no algorithm involved. Chicago's Anders Humlum offers a test: if AI were the cause, the firms adopting it fastest should be cutting entry-level roles fastest, and he doesn't see that. On the other side is Brynjolfsson, the Canaries study's lead author, defending the AI signal while agreeing a major shift is underway either way.

The researchers stress-tested their own finding. The gap survives when they exclude tech companies and computer jobs, and when they control for interest-rate exposure and remote work. It kept widening after interest rates peaked, and it concentrates in automating occupations with a clear age pattern, which interest rates alone wouldn't naturally produce. They also publish their reasons for doubt: the gap shrinks when education is accounted for, some trends predate generative AI, and their estimates run larger than national survey benchmarks. Their own conclusion is that no single study, including theirs, settles it. So they built a Canaries Dashboard that updates every month. Two rigorous readings of the same numbers, unresolved, in public. That's not a flaw in the science. That is the science, mid-stride, and you can watch it settle.

"AI will add trillions to GDP" is said by the companies selling it and the analysts who cover them. "It's a bubble" is said by people who remember 2000. "AI is closing the entry-level door" is said by the Stanford team, who publish their doubts; "it's remote work and the economy" is said by the economists NPR interviewed, with their own evidence. What would settle both disputes is the same thing: the two dashboards over the next twelve months. That is the rare case in this book where the evidence that would change your mind is scheduled.

How to make it beneficial Question 5: keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost?

  • Keep buying tools without reorganizing, and the cost is paying for a dynamo bolted to a steam layout.
  • Fix it by reorganizing through the dip, which quarterly incentives punish, and by hiring the junior anyway and paying for the training AI used to provide by accident. The cost is money now for a payoff later, which is the dynamo's whole lesson.
  • Drop it and wait, which is what many firms are doing, and the cost is being late when the redesign finally pays.
How to keep it good

The good version of this chapter exists: it's the complementing build, at scale. Keep AI where it extends people. Fix the hiring gap by treating entry-level roles as the training system they always were, and budgeting for them as such. Drop the assumption that a job that AI can do is a job that should be done by AI alone; the evidence says the firms that made that assumption cut the wrong people. The cost is a slower, more expensive first few years. The payoff is the forty years.

4

Decide: your own first job

Deciding lands close to home. On Day 1 we asked a question about the workflows you found: could this scale? Now you have the machinery. Scaling isn't more people using the tool; that's the extensive margin, and it's nearly free. Scaling is a workflow rebuilt so the tool changes what the organization can do; that's the intensive margin, and it's the forty years.

For your career, the history and the case point the same way. The person who merely uses AI is the swapped engine, and the swapped engine is the entry-level role that's quietly disappearing. The person who can judge the output and redesign how a team works around it is the new factory. Three moves follow from what the researchers actually documented:

  • Build tacit knowledge on purpose. Internships, jobs, and projects with real stakes are the layer AI didn't train on. The degree gets you considered; the tacit layer gets you kept. The cost is time you'd otherwise spend elsewhere, now, while you're still here.
  • Look for complement work. When you consider a role, ask whether the AI does the task while a person checks it, or extends what the person can do. You can ask that in an interview, and asking it well is itself a signal. The cost: complement roles often ask for experience you're still building.
  • Treat a long search as the mechanism, not a verdict. The gap works through hiring, so the first step is the steepest. Knowing that doesn't make it easy. It makes it navigable, and it's a reason to start earlier.

Wednesday, you run a firm and feel why the redesign is rare.

Key terms

Productivity

Output per hour worked: the scoreboard economists trust over user counts and stock prices.

Productivity paradox

Transformative technology, flat statistics. Solow's computers, and possibly AI's current moment.

General-purpose technology

A technology that transforms everything, slowly: steam, electricity, computing. The payoff arrives through reorganization.

Extensive vs. intensive margin

How many use it vs. how deeply it's woven in. The revolution is on the first margin; the money is on the second.

AI exposure

How much of a job's tasks current AI can plausibly perform. Measures overlap, not destiny.

Automating vs. complementing use

AI doing the task vs. AI extending the person. Entry-level employment is falling in the first and holding in the second.

Codified vs. tacit knowledge

Written-down knowledge AI reproduces well vs. knowledge earned through practice that it doesn't.

Descriptive vs. causal

Data can show a pattern exists without proving what caused it. The Canaries researchers say so themselves.
