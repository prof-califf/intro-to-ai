The Prototype Project

In a team of four, design your own AI system and pitch it. Your spec is the prototype on paper; building a working version is up to you. Find a problem worth solving, lay out how your system would work using the model we use every week, question your own design as hard as we question everyone else's, and pitch it to the class.

10 percent of your grade · teams of four · a three-page paper · a pitch of four slides or fewer · pitches in class Mon, Nov 30 and Wed, Dec 2 · paper and slides due Fri, Dec 4, 11:59 p.m.

In one paragraph

Form a team of four by Friday, October 30, and submit the four names plus one paragraph on your rough idea on Canvas that day. Browse the Rundown AI Workflow Hub for inspiration and pick a real problem you'd like an AI system to help with. Design that system as a spec: what it's for, who uses it, and its five parts (data, model, output, feedback loop, humans). Run the five critical AI questions on your own design and decide what to keep, fix, or drop, with the cost of each choice. Building a working version is up to your team; you can use Claude, ChatGPT, any other tool, or no AI at all. On Monday, November 30, or Wednesday, December 2, your team gives a five-minute pitch with four slides or fewer. By 11:59 p.m. Friday, December 4, your team submits the paper and slides, and each of you submits a short reflection. You are graded on whether your design survives the questions, not on whether you built it.

2

Get inspired

Prototyping is how ideas get tested in business now. Instead of writing a long plan and hoping, people build a rough working version fast, put it in front of someone, and learn what's wrong before it costs much. AI has made that much cheaper: a chatbot, a spreadsheet, or a no-code tool can turn a sketch into something you can click on in an afternoon. Companies increasingly ask for a prototype, not a proposal.

The Rundown AI Workflow Hub, where Day 1 left off, is a live feed of exactly those prototypes: workflows real people built with AI for real work, most of them rough, some of them clever, a few of them bad ideas that look good. Browse it for the shape of what's possible, then pick a problem of your own. It can come from your job, your classes, a club, a business you know, or your family.

A good problemA weak problem
Has a specific user: "the front desk at my gym," "first-year students choosing classes"Has no one in particular in mind: "people," "businesses"
Is a real task someone does now, slowly or badlyIs a task nobody actually needs done
Has data that exists or could be collectedDepends on data nobody has or could ethically get
Is small enough to describe in one sentenceIs "use AI to fix education" or "an app that does everything"
3

Write the spec

Describe your system clearly enough that someone could build it. Start with one sentence each: what problem it solves, who uses it, and what kind of AI it is (predictive, generative, or agentic). Then lay out its five parts.

PartAnswer these
DataWhat does it learn from or look at? Where does that data come from, and did the people in it agree? What's missing from it, and who does that leave out?
ModelWhat would it run on? Name an existing model or tool (you don't need to build one or write code) and say why that one.
OutputWhat does it produce: a judgment (a score, a flag, a ranking) or a creation (a draft, an image, a plan)? What does a person do with it next?
Feedback loopHow does it get better over time? What could go wrong as it does?
HumansWhere do people sit: in the loop (approving every output), on the loop (watching and able to step in), or out of it? Who checks the work, and who answers when it's wrong?

A simple diagram of your five parts is welcome. Boxes and arrows, like the one in Chapter 1, are plenty.

4

Question your own design

Now do to your system what we do to every system in class.

1

What is the AI system, and what does it do? Your spec answers this one.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Who gains if it works? Who pays for it? Is there anyone it could quietly work against?

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Describe one realistic failure. Who would notice, and how fast? Is it a loud failure or a quiet one?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Write the one claim you'd want to make about your system ("it saves an hour a day," "it's fair to every applicant"). What evidence would you need before anyone should believe it?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Look back at your own design. Name at least one feature you'd keep, one you'd fix, and one you'd drop or never build, with what each choice costs and who pays.

5

Build it, if you want to

Building is optional. Some of you will want to make a working version, and some won't; both are fine, and the grade is the same either way. A prototype can be a custom chatbot in Claude or ChatGPT, a no-code app, a spreadsheet, or a clickable mockup. If you build one, you can test it with something you tried in a lab, like giving it two inputs that differ in one detail and comparing the results, and put what you found under question 3.

6

How to write it up: the pitch paper

Write your paper like a business pitch: you're convincing a manager or an investor that this system is worth building, and showing you've thought hard about how it could go wrong. Use these nine headings, in this order. The whole paper runs about three pages, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words.

SectionWhat goes in it
1. Executive summary
About 100 words		In one paragraph: the problem, your solution, who pays for it, and what you're asking for. Write it last, put it first. Someone who reads only this should get the whole idea.
2. The problem and the customer
About 200 words		Who has this problem? How do they handle it today? What does it cost them in time, money, or mistakes? Give a number, even an estimate, and say where it came from. Why does this need solving now?
3. The solution: how it works
About 300 words		Start with a one-sentence pitch. Name the kind of AI (predictive, generative, or agentic). Then lay out the five parts: data, model, output, feedback loop, and humans, with a simple diagram. Finish by walking through one customer using it, step by step.
4. The business case
About 200 words		Who pays, and how: a subscription, a fee per use, or savings inside a company? Who benefits? Who could lose out? What does it replace, and what are the alternatives, including doing nothing?
5. Risks: how it could fail
About 250 words		Describe one realistic failure. Is it loud or quiet? Who finds out, and how fast? Then the data risks: did people agree to share it, what's missing, and who does that leave out? What could go wrong as the system learns?
6. Proof: what you'd need to show
About 150 words		Write the claim you'd put on your first slide ("cuts wait times in half"). What evidence would make an investor or a manager believe it? Describe a small pilot or test that would prove it or prove you wrong.
7. Design decisions: keep, fix, drop
About 200 words		Name at least one feature you'd keep, one you'd fix, and one you'd drop or never build. For each, say what it costs and who pays.
8. The ask and next steps
About 100 words		What do you need to get started: money, access to data, a pilot partner? What are your first three steps? What result would make you stop?
9. Prototype (optional) and AI use
A few sentences		If you built something, describe it using the checklist below. End with one line on which tools your team used, if any, and for what.

Your four pitch slides follow the same order: slide 1 is the problem and the customer, slide 2 is how it works, slide 3 is the risks and your design decisions, and slide 4 is the proof and the ask.

If you build a prototype, include

WhatFor example
The tool you used"A custom chatbot in Claude," "a Google Sheet," "a clickable mockup in Canva"
What you gave itThe instructions or prompt, and the sample data. Use made-up data, never real people's private information.
One to three screenshotsYour setup, and one or two outputs
What worked and what didn'tOne or two sentences each
One test and its resultTwo inputs that differ in one detail; a tricky question; a case it should refuse
A link, if it's shareableOptional

No prototype? Skip that part. A clear spec with honest answers earns full credit on its own.

7

The pitch

In the last week, half the teams pitch on Monday, November 30, after the Chapter 10 and 11 cases, and half on Wednesday, December 2. Each team gets five minutes to pitch, then two minutes of questions from the room. Everyone on the team should speak. Four slides at most:

1

The problem. Who the customer is, what goes wrong for them today, and what it costs them.

2

How it works. Your five parts, as a simple diagram.

3

Risks and decisions. The failure you worry about most, and what you decided to keep, fix, or drop because of it.

4

Proof and the ask. The claim, the test that would prove it, and what you need to get started.

While other teams pitch, yours has a job. Each team is assigned one pitch to question first, and after every pitch the room votes: would you fund it? If you built a prototype, a quick demo fits inside your five minutes.

8

What I look for

The question behind the grade is whether your design survives the five questions.

StrongWeak
A specific problem and a specific customer, with a sense of what the problem costs themA vague problem, or no customer in mind
A believable business case: who pays, who benefits, and who could lose outNo idea who would pay, or no one who could be harmed
All five parts, including honest answers about the data's gaps and where people sitParts left out, or a system with no humans in it and no explanation why
A realistic failure, and who would notice it"It won't fail" or a failure that could never happen
A claim with the evidence it would needClaims stated as facts
Real keep, fix, and drop decisions about your own design, each with a costEverything kept, nothing questioned
A clear pitch that fits in five minutes and four slides, with everyone speakingReading slides aloud, or running long
Your own reasoning and words, and an honest AI-use lineA spec a chatbot wrote, or no disclosure
9

Using AI on this project

Learn it first, then use AI. In your career you'll use AI all the time, and you should. But this project is where you prove you understand the five parts and the five questions. Work those out as a team first. If you hand them to a chatbot before you understand them, you won't be able to tell when it's wrong.

Building: use any tool you like, or none. The spec, the questions, and the pitch: AI can be your tutor once you've done your own thinking. It can poke holes in your design or suggest what you missed. The reasoning and the words you submit are yours.

At the end of the paper, one line: which tools your team used, if any, and for what. "None" is a fine answer. When in doubt, just tell me what you did.
