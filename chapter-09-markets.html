Markets: Where's the Boom?

Hundreds of billions in investment. Hundreds of millions of users. And economy-wide productivity numbers that have barely blinked. This chapter is about the gap between AI's adoption and AI's payoff — and the hundred-year-old story that predicts exactly this.

1

The paradox on the receipt

Here's the puzzle this chapter lives in. By any adoption measure, generative AI is one of the fastest-spreading technologies in history — hundreds of millions of weekly users within a few years of launch. By investment measures, it's a once-in-a-generation buildout; we saw the data centers and nuclear deals in Chapter 4. And by the measure economists care about most — productivity, the output an economy squeezes from an hour of work — the needle has barely moved.

This isn't a new kind of embarrassment. In 1987, economist Robert Solow quipped that the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The economist Paul Krugman, whose essay “AI and the Pitfalls of Innovation” anchors this week, calls it a recurring pitfall of innovation: transformative technology and transformed economies are two different events, sometimes decades apart. The question isn't whether AI is impressive. It's whether — and when, and for whom — impressive becomes productive.

Productivity (and why economists obsess over it)

Output per hour worked. In the long run it's close to the whole ballgame: wages, living standards, and whether the next generation lives better than the last all ride on it. When economists measure a technology's true impact, this — not user counts, not stock prices — is the scoreboard.

2

The case: the dynamo's forty years

The best case study for this moment is a hundred years old, and Krugman's essay leans on it for good reason. The economic historian Paul David studied what happened when American factories got electricity — and found that for decades, almost nothing happened.

Here's why. A steam-powered factory was built vertically around one giant engine, with power distributed by belts and shafts through the whole building. When electric motors arrived, factory owners did the obvious thing: swapped the steam engine for a big electric one and kept everything else. Same building, same layout, same workflow — new power source. The productivity gain was marginal, and it stayed marginal for roughly a generation.

The payoff came only when a new generation of managers stopped retrofitting and started redesigning: small motors on each machine, single-story factories laid out around the flow of work instead of the flow of power, assembly lines that were physically impossible in the old buildings. The technology hadn't improved much. The organization had finally been rebuilt around it — and productivity took off, forty years after the dynamo arrived.

The sentence to carry out of this chapter

A general-purpose technology pays off not when it's adopted, but when work is reorganized around it. Swapping the engine is fast. Redesigning the factory is slow, expensive, disruptive — and it's where all the gains were hiding.

General-purpose technology (GPT — the older GPT)

A technology that transforms nearly every industry rather than one: steam, electricity, computing. Economists' term decades before it became a chatbot's initials. GPTs share a signature: enormous promise, long delays, and payoffs that arrive through reorganization, not installation.

3

The data: everyone's using it, firms aren't changing

If David's story is the right map, we should be able to locate ourselves on it. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab's AI Adoption Monitor — same lab as Chapter 11's Canaries study — tracks exactly the two numbers that matter, and they're moving at wildly different speeds.

Individual adoption is fast. Surveys tracked by the Monitor find a large share of American workers already using generative AI, at rates that rival or beat the early PC and internet eras. People didn't wait for permission; much of this use is informal, personal-account, bring-your-own-AI.

Firm adoption is slow. The share of companies that have formally woven AI into how they produce things remains a small fraction of the workforce's personal adoption — and much of what exists is shallow: a chatbot license, a pilot program, an engine swapped into an unchanged factory.

Economists studying the gap also split adoption into two margins worth keeping: the extensive margin (how many people and firms use AI at all) and the intensive margin (how deeply it's woven into actual workflows). The extensive margin looks like a revolution. The intensive margin looks like 1900 — belts and shafts still on the ceiling, a shiny new motor bolted where the steam engine used to be.

4

The other reading: what if the bet is wrong?

Fairness requires the other hypothesis on the table. The dynamo story says: be patient, the reorganization is coming, the boom follows. But the same facts support a darker reading — that the payoff might be smaller than the investment assumes. The hundreds of billions flowing into data centers are a bet that reorganization will happen and that the gains will be enormous. If either half disappoints, the word for what we're in isn't "early." It's "bubble." Krugman's pitfalls essay is precisely about this: innovation booms have repeatedly outrun the underlying payoff, and the correction lands on investors, workers, and towns that built around the boom — our externality lesson wearing a financial costume.

The honest position is that nobody knows which reading is right, and this book won't pretend otherwise. What we can do — the whole skill of this course — is name what evidence would distinguish them. Watch the intensive margin: if firms deeply reorganize and productivity still doesn't move, the dynamo story weakens. Watch who profits meanwhile: sellers of shovels do fine in every gold rush, real or imagined. And watch the Monitor, monthly, the way Chapter 11 watches the Canaries Dashboard. Live instruments beat loud opinions.

5

Question it & decide

The five questions, applied
1

What is it, and what does it do? This week "AI" is an economic object: a candidate general-purpose technology, like the dynamo. The chatbot on your phone and the capital spending on Microsoft's books are the same story at two scales.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? To make workers more productive, which pays the firm only when the work is reorganized around the tool, not when the tool is bought. Who benefits: firms that reorganize, eventually; investors, if the boom arrives; workers, depending entirely on who captures the gain.

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? A productivity boom that never arrives isn't an event; it's an absence, visible only in statistics years later, like the missed cure in Chapter 3. The Adoption Monitor exists to make it visible early.

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? "AI will add trillions to GDP" is said by the companies selling it and the analysts who cover them. "It's a bubble" is said by people who remember 2000. Krugman's dynamo essay is the evidence that both can be true in sequence. What would settle it is the Monitor's next twelve months.

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Keep buying tools without reorganizing and the cost is paying for a dynamo you bolted to a steam layout. Fix it by reorganizing through the dip, which quarterly incentives punish. Drop it and wait, which is what most firms are doing, and say what a firm loses by being late.

Deciding lands close to home, because on Day 1 we asked a question about the workflows you found — could this scale? — and promised to answer it here. Now you have the machinery: scaling isn't more people using the tool (extensive margin — that part's nearly free). Scaling is a workflow rebuilt so the tool changes what the organization can do (intensive margin — that part is the forty years). For your own career the implication is direct, and it compounds Chapter 11's advice: the person who merely uses AI is the swapped engine. The person who can redesign how a team works around it is the new factory. This course has been quietly training the second skill all quarter. Wednesday, you run a firm and feel why the redesign is rare.

6

Key terms

Productivity

Output per hour worked — the scoreboard economists trust over user counts and stock prices.

Productivity paradox

Transformative technology, flat statistics — Solow's computers, and possibly AI's current moment.

General-purpose technology

A technology that transforms everything, slowly: steam, electricity, computing. Payoff arrives via reorganization.

The dynamo lesson

Paul David's finding: electrification paid off only when factories were redesigned around it — roughly forty years in.

Extensive vs. intensive margin

How many use it vs. how deeply it's woven in. The revolution is on the first margin; the money is on the second.

The adoption gap

Workers far ahead of firms — bottom-up use inside unreorganized organizations. Today's version of belts and shafts.

Discussion questions

For Monday's discussion · read the Krugman essay first
  1. Explain the dynamo story to someone who's never taken economics, in four sentences, ending with what it predicts about AI. No course vocabulary allowed.
  2. Find the belts and shafts in a job you've actually held: one workflow that exists only because of how things used to work. What would "redesign around the new motor" mean there — and why hasn't anyone done it?
  3. You (probably) use AI personally; most organizations you belong to (probably) haven't reorganized around it. Give one concrete example of that gap from your own life, and name what closing it would require — and what it would risk.
  4. Steelman the bubble reading in three sentences, using something real from this course (the buildout, the adoption data, anything). Then say which single piece of future evidence would most change your mind, in either direction.
  5. Day 1 you flagged a workflow and we asked "could this scale?" Answer it now, properly: what's the extensive-margin version of your workflow scaling, what's the intensive-margin version, and which one did you actually mean back then?
