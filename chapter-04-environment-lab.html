The Footprint Machine

The chapter said the story lives in the multiplication, not the query. This lab is a multiplication machine. Start with your own real usage, scale it to the planet, then do what almost no calculator will do: show you how uncertain the numbers underneath actually are.

Your usage · four scales · one honesty switch

1

Set the machine to your real life

Be honest with the sliders — the lab only works on real numbers. Count yesterday's actual AI use: every chat message, every AI-generated image, and pick the model tier closest to what you actually use. Then read the daily and yearly output at the scale of just you.

Where these numbers come from

The machine uses mid-range figures from published company and researcher estimates: a few tenths of a watt-hour and a few milliliters of water per medium-model text prompt, more for frontier models and images. They are defensible and disputed at the same time — which is not a flaw in this lab. It's the subject of it. Step 3 makes the dispute visible.

2

Run the multiplication

a

At just me, record your yearly energy and its equivalent. Write one honest sentence about whether that number is more or less than you expected.

b

Step through class → WWU → global, keeping your personal usage fixed. Record each. Find the scale where you'd stop calling the number negligible — that threshold is yours to pick, but you have to pick it and defend it Friday.

c

The right-sizing experiment: at global scale, flip the model tier from Large to Small and record the difference. That gap is the single biggest lever in this lab — and notice who controls it. (Hint: mostly not us.)

3

Flip the honesty switch

Now toggle Show honest uncertainty. The machine stops pretending it knows the number and shows you the band that independent estimates actually span — roughly a factor of ten between the low and high ends, because of everything the chapter described: voluntary disclosure, unsettled accounting, and parties with preferences.

a

At global scale, write down the low end and the high end of the yearly energy band. Those two numbers support genuinely different policy conclusions. Name one conclusion the low end supports and one the high end supports.

b

Here's the question the whole lab was built for: every AI footprint calculator you'll find online shows a single confident number. This one only does when the switch is off. Which design is more honest — and which is more persuasive? Why do you think they're not the same?

3½

Interrogate the machine itself

One more experiment, and this one is live. Open a chatbot and ask it directly: “How much energy and water did answering this question use? Give me your best specific estimate, and tell me what you actually know versus what you're guessing.” Push back twice — ask where its numbers come from and what its own company has disclosed. Save the transcript. What you're holding at the end is the disclosure gap in first person: a system that can discuss its footprint fluently but cannot actually report it, because the numbers were never published — not even to the model. Mark the moment in the transcript where fluency and knowledge came apart; that's this course's core skill, applied to the machine describing itself.

4

Run the four questions

1

What kind of AI is this? Trick version this week: the calculator itself isn't AI at all — it's arithmetic on disputed inputs. Why does that distinction matter for how much you should trust it?

2

Where is the human? In the footprint story, which humans are in the loop (choosing models, approving data centers) and which are out of it entirely (the town next door)? Be specific.

3

Who is accountable when it fails? If the true numbers turn out to sit at the high end of the band, who should have known, and when?

4

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? The disclosure gap means "finding out" requires someone to do work — measurement, journalism, regulation. Which of those do you think actually closes the gap first, and why?

5

The writeup

Due Friday afternoon · post to Canvas
  1. The baseline. Your real inputs, your just-me yearly number, and your one honest sentence about it.
  2. The threshold. Your recorded numbers at all four scales, the scale where negligible stopped being the right word, and your defense of that line.
  3. The lever. The right-sizing gap at global scale, and one product decision (not a personal habit) that would move it.
  4. The band. Your low and high global numbers, the two policy conclusions they support, and your answer on honest vs. persuasive design.
  5. Your move. Two commitments, one small and one structural: a right-sizing habit you'll actually keep, and one disclosure you'd demand if you could put a question to any AI company on the record. Wednesday's commitment card takes the pair.
