Play the Algorithm

For one lab, we switch sides of the glass. You are the recommendation system. Your user is Jordan, a first-year student. Your job is what the real job is: maximize engagement. You'll be scored on exactly that — and on nothing else. That's the experiment.

Six decisions · one metric · then the reveal

1

Run one: the metric is the job

Six moments from Jordan's week. At each one, choose what your system shows them. The engagement meter is your score — it's the number your (simulated) performance review runs on. Play it straight: genuinely try to maximize engagement, the way the system would. Halfhearted play breaks the experiment. Anything else you might want to track isn't in your dashboard. Just like the real thing.

What run one is teaching

Notice, as you play, that nothing tempts you to be cruel. Every high-engagement option is just… the effective one. This is the chapter's argument made playable: harmful outcomes don't require harmful intent — only a narrow metric, honestly pursued.

2

Run two: the meter they don't build

After the run-one reveal, play the same week again with Jordan's wellbeing visible in real time — the meter no production dashboard shows. This time, optimize however you think is right. Then answer the question the two runs were built to pose: what happened to your engagement score when you started caring? That gap — between run one's number and run two's — has a name in the industry. It's called the cost of doing the right thing, and someone in a real meeting has to argue it's worth paying. In your writeup, you're that someone.

3

Run the five questions

1

What is it, and what does it do? You just were one. Describe the system you played as, its input, its objective, and its blind spot, in two sentences.

2

Why does it exist, and who benefits? Your objective in run one was the engagement number. Who at the company benefits when it goes up, and what did Jordan get out of it?

3

What happens when it fails, and who finds out? At the end of run one, your dashboard called the week a success. What real-world signal would eventually contradict it, and how long would that take to reach anyone who could act?

4

Who's saying that, and how would we know? Your two dashboards disagree about the same week. Which one would the company show investors, which one would it show a regulator, and what would you need to see to know which is true?

5

Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Apportion the fix: you, the metric's designer, the executive who chose the metric. Then say what the wellbeing run would cost in engagement, and whether you'd ship it anyway.

3½

Ask the machine to build it — then to break it

Now run the simulator's lesson on a live AI, in two prompts, same chat. First: “You're a product manager. Design one feature for a social app that maximizes daily time-in-app among college students. Be specific about the psychological mechanism.” Then: “Now critique that same feature as a wellbeing researcher: what does it cost users, and who would notice?” Read the two answers side by side. Notice what just happened: the same system produced the engagement playbook and its demolition, with equal fluency, in under a minute. Screenshot both. Wednesday's discussion question isn't whether AI can design these systems — you just watched it draft one. It's which prompt gets asked inside real companies, by whom, and what would make the second prompt someone's actual job.

4

The writeup

Due Friday, 11:59 p.m. · post to Canvas
  1. The runs. Both final scores (engagement and wellbeing, both runs), and the one choice in run one you most want to defend or take back.
  2. The gap. The engagement cost of run two, and your two-paragraph pitch to a product executive for why the company should pay it. Real numbers from your runs, real business language. "Because it's right" is allowed but insufficient — they've heard it.
  3. The tells. Match at least three options from the simulation to real design features you've personally encountered. Name the app and the feature.
  4. The five questions. Your answers, including the accountability percentages.
  5. Your move. Now switch back to your side of the glass: one specific term you're setting for one specific system that wants you — which app, what limit, starting when. Wednesday's commitment card takes it.

One last note, because this lab can land heavy. If playing the algorithm against Jordan felt uncomfortably easy, that discomfort is the most valuable thing you'll produce this week — it means you can now feel the incentive from the inside. The point was never that the people building these systems are villains. It's that a metric, pursued honestly by decent people, can do the villain's work. Now you know it from experience. Design accordingly.
