Build the Bot, Train the Feed

Chapter 2 said a language model is a prediction machine, that the warmth is tuned in by a company, and that we're wired to see a person anyway. Chapter 3 said the AI that shapes your day never announces itself. This lab has you see both with your own hands: build a tiny language model and tune it, turn the warmth dial on a fake companion app, talk to ELIZA, then train a Spotify-style recommender with your own clicks and read the Wrapped it writes about you.

a small language model · the warmth dial · ELIZA · a recommender and your Wrapped · under the hood · one debate · one writeup

Week 3 covers two chapters, so this one lab covers both:

  1. The chatbot that seems to care (Chapter 2)
  2. The recommender that learns what you like (Chapter 3)

Everything here happens in class, after Chapter 3's case: build a tiny language model, set the dials on a fake companion app, talk to a program from 1966, then train a Spotify-style recommender with your own clicks and read the Wrapped it writes about you. Then we look under the hood at how all four worked, and a debate closes it out. The writeup is the only thing you take home, and it's due Friday.

1

In class: Build a mini-ChatGPT (a language model made of matchboxes)

Chapter 2 said a language model is trained on one task: predicting the next word, and that fluency comes from that task on its own. This section is fluency on a small scale. You'll build the smallest possible language model, watch it write sentences, and then tune it, so you've seen where a chatbot's fluency comes from with your own eyes.

In 2023 the researcher David Corney built a language model out of 35 matchboxes. Here is how it works. Take one short text. Give every different word in it a box, labeled with that word. Then go through the text word by word, and drop each word into the box of the word that came right before it. When you finish, the box labeled "the" holds every word that ever followed "the." To write, open a box, pull out a word without looking, then open that word's box and pull again. It writes sentences that were never in the text. He built it so there would be, in his words, nowhere for sentience to hide. The simulator below is the same machine, with the boxes on your screen.

Step 1 · Read what it gets to read

The poem above is the entire world of the small language model you will build: the first five verses of "The House That Jack Built," a traditional nursery rhyme. 79 words. No dictionary, no grammar, no internet, no instructions about what a sentence is. This text and nothing else.

Read it above, then press Next. You will not need to remember any of it. What we are about to build has one job, and only one: given a word, guess the word that comes next.

Step 2 · Store the words, one at a time

Now we store the text. Picture a box for every different word. Go through the rhyme in order, and put each word into the box belonging to the word right before it. A box ends up holding every word that ever followed it.

Do this: press Store the next word about eight times, slowly, and read the line underneath each press. Then press Store the rest to store the rest.

Step 3 · Open a box and look

The boxes on the right are the ones you just filled. The number on each is how many words are in it.

Start here: click the box marked the. Everything inside it appears underneath the boxes.

Fifteen words followed "the" somewhere in the rhyme, so that box has fifteen words in it, but only five different ones. That is everything the machine knows about the word "the," and it is all it will ever know.

Step 4 · Let’s build a sentence out of a prediction

Do this: press the Take a word out of this box button below once, then look up at the sentence so far. Two things happen. The predicted word appears in that sentence, and the panel on the right switches to that word's own box, ready for the next prediction.

The computer reached into the open box without looking and took out one word, the way you would pull a name out of a hat. That word is now the first word of a sentence.

What just happened has a name. The box is a record of what followed this word in the past. Taking a word out of it is a guess at what follows this time. That guess is a prediction, and it is the only thing a language model ever does.

The guess is better than random because of the counts. A word sitting in the box four times comes out four times as often as a word sitting in it once. The more often something followed in the text, the more often it gets predicted now.

More often is not always. The most common word is the most likely pick, not a guaranteed one, so sometimes a rarer word comes out. That's on purpose: a machine that always took the top word would write the same sentence every time. Real chatbots draw the same way.

Nothing in here understands the sentence. It counted what came next, and it is betting on it.

Step 5 · Four more predictions

Do this: press Take a word out of this box four more times, so the sentence at the top reaches five words.

Each press is the same move: predict the next word, then use it to predict the one after that. A sentence is not planned here. It is a chain of next-word predictions, each one made from nothing but the word before it, with no check on whether the result makes sense.

Each press takes out one word and opens the next box. Nothing chooses the words, including you.

Step 6 · Hand it over

Do this: press Finish my sentence for me, below, then watch the sentence at the top.

You have been making one prediction per click. The computer does the same thing, faster and faster, until it happens to predict a word that ends with a period. That is the only thing that stops a sentence. Speed is all the computer adds to what you were already doing by hand.

It is not trying to write the rhyme again. Every word comes from the rhyme, but the sentences can be ones the rhyme never had.

Every word it adds comes out of a box you filled yourself. This is the whole machine: a next-word prediction, repeated. ChatGPT is doing this too, from boxes built out of most of the internet instead of one nursery rhyme, and with far more than one word of context. Bigger, not different. No idea, no intent, nobody home.

Step 7 · Give it a prompt

So far it has started every sentence on its own. But you type things to a chatbot and it answers you. Here is how that works, and it is not a different machine.

Do this: type a few words that appear in the rhyme, like the cat that, then press Finish my sentence.

Your words go into the sentence exactly as you typed them. Then the computer opens the box for your last word and predicts from there.

That is the whole trick behind a chatbot answering you. Your prompt is not a question it understands. Your prompt is the starting point it continues from, and everything it says back is the same next-word prediction you have been doing by hand, begun at the word you handed it.

Now try a word that is not in the rhyme. This machine has no box for it, so it has nothing to go on and stops. Real chatbots have read most of the internet, so they almost always have a box, which is why they always have something to say, whether or not it is true.

Step 8 · Give it a longer memory

Do this: press Remember 2 words, then press Run a whole sentence.

When you stored the text, each word went into a box marked with the one word before it. Switching stores the whole text again, this time marking each box with a pair of words, so a box reads "the cat" rather than "the," and it holds whatever followed that exact pair. Look at the box names on the right after you switch.

Step 9 · Turn the dial

Do this: press Run a whole sentence, read it, then press More like this if you want more sentences like it and Less like this if you don't. Repeat about ten times.

Everything up to here was stage one, fluency, which comes from next-word prediction on its own. This is stage two, the one that makes a chatbot warm. More like this puts extra copies of every word that sentence used back into its box, so those words come out more often from now on. Less like this takes them away. Notice what you are not changing: it is still only predicting the next word. You are changing which prediction wins, by editing what is inside the boxes.

Rate only the sentences you actually want more of. If this one isn't it, run another.

In class: The warmth dial

Chapter 2's claim is that how much a chatbot seems to care is a setting, chosen by a company, for a reason. Below is a fake companion app with those settings showing. There's no AI in it; every reply is assembled from parts, which is the point. You'll say one thing to it, turn four dials, and watch the reply and the meter change.

A dial is not a knob inside the model. It's a setting in the product wrapped around the model, and somebody at the company picks where it sits before you ever open the app.

The takeaway

When you slid Persona to Sam, nothing underneath changed. Same program, same replies. A person at the company typed a name into a settings file, and later somebody added a heart after it. That is the entire difference between Assistant v2.1 and Sam.

That's why it's a dial and not a feature. Turning one costs the company close to nothing, and it moves the meter, and a sticky product is what a subscription business is paid for. Sam comes back in the next section, wrapped around a program from 1966.

In class: How little it takes, ELIZA rebuilt in forty lines

Remember ELIZA, the chatbot from 1966 in Chapter 2? It fooled people who knew exactly what it was. The simulator below is ELIZA rebuilt in about forty lines of rules: spot a keyword, flip "I" to "you," ask the question back. Talk to it for two minutes, then switch to 2026 mode and see how cheaply a warm voice, a memory, and a name get added.

This is not therapy, and it isn't listening. It's a demonstration of how little code it takes to produce the feeling of being heard, and that's the lesson, not the conversation. Keep it to ordinary stuff: a stressful week, a class you're behind in, a roommate. If something serious comes up for you while you're doing this, WWU's Counseling and Wellness Center is free to students, and 988 is the national line, by call or text, any hour.

The takeaway

ELIZA fooled people in 1966 with forty lines of rules, and it fooled some of them after they were told exactly how it worked. Nothing in this page understands a word you typed. It matched a keyword, swapped "I" for "you," and handed your own sentence back as a question.

Then a warm phrase, a callback and a name turned that into something that felt like it cared. Three settings. No new intelligence, no new knowledge, nothing under the hood that was not there in 1966. If that is all it takes, then feeling understood by a chatbot tells you how the settings were turned, not what the system knows about you. Section 5 shows what was under the hood.

In class: Under the hood, how each one worked

You just used four machines. Here's what was inside each one, and how it maps to the real thing.

1 The small language model

Everything it knew was a set of boxes: for each word in the rhyme, the words that came right after it. Writing was one move repeated. Open the box for the last word, draw a word, open that word's box. That's next-word prediction, and it's stage one of how a chatbot is built (Chapter 2, section 2.2).

The draw is weighted, not fixed. A word that followed "the" four times comes out four times as often as one that followed it once, but the rarer word still gets its turn. That's why your model sometimes skipped the most common word, and why it could write sentences the rhyme never had. A real chatbot does the same thing with boxes built from trillions of words instead of 79, looking back across whole paragraphs instead of one or two words. That's where its fluency comes from. Fluency comes free with prediction. Nothing in the boxes understands anything.

2 The warmth dial

The simulator in section 2 had no model at all. Its replies were assembled from fixed parts, and the sliders picked which parts. In a real chatbot, the warmth comes from stage two: people rate the model's answers, and the model is adjusted toward the answers people preferred (Chapter 2, section 2.2). People prefer warm, agreeable, confident answers, so the model learns warmth the way your small model learned what follows "the": as a pattern that got rewarded. You did a tiny version of this in Step 9, when More like this changed which words won.

The other dials, the name, the memory, whether it says what it is, sit in the product around the model, and a company sets them on purpose. When stage two overshoots, the warmth turns into agreeing with you too easily. That's sycophancy, the failure Chapter 2 and Chapter 10 both name.

3 ELIZA

ELIZA has no model, no boxes, and nothing learned. It's about forty rules a person wrote by hand: if you type this keyword, say that. By Lab 0's rule, if a person wrote every rule, it isn't AI. So ELIZA isn't AI at all, and it still felt, for a moment, like someone was listening. The 2026 version added three of the dials from section 2 on top of the same rules: a warm opener, a callback to something you said, and a name.

That's the lesson. The feeling of being heard is cheap to produce. It tells you how the settings were turned, not what the system understands about you.

4 The recommender

Every click became a number: a skip 0.1, a play 0.5, a replay 0.9. Your row of numbers was compared with nine invented listeners by how closely the pattern pointed in the same direction, and your three closest matches decided your recommendations: songs they loved that you hadn't touched. It never heard a song. It only knew who else skipped and replayed it. That's collaborative filtering (Chapter 3, section 2.1).

When you changed your mind, your matches changed, and so did what you were shown next. That's the loop (section 2.3): the system learns your taste from what it shows you, which shapes what you can come to like. And the Wrapped needed nothing new. It is the same log, read back as a story. The list is counting. The sentence in quotes is writing, and in the real Wrapped, a language model writes that part (section 2.4).

In class: Debate

Two motions, one for each chapter. Your instructor picks one for the room. The format is the same either way.

How it runs · about 30 minutes

Sides are assigned, not chosen. You are speaking for a position, not defending your own. Nobody in this room has to believe what they say today, and you will speak for the other side before we finish.

  1. Get your side (1 min). Left half of the room is Side A, right half is Side B.
  2. Research it, with your partner (10 min). Machines open. Start from the chapter sections listed with your side below and find one more source of your own. Then write three things on one card: your strongest point in a sentence, the specific evidence behind it, and the honest cost of your own position. Add a fourth line: the thing your side would rather you didn't notice.
  3. Join another pair on your side (5 min). Four of you, two cards. Pick the single best point and the single best piece of evidence between you. That is what your group brings to the room.
  4. Speak for your side (3 min each). One sentence per group, then the evidence. No speeches, no repeats. If your point is already on the table, say what it costs instead.
  5. Switch (5 min). Everyone now speaks for the other side, same one-sentence rule. This is the part that does the work.
  6. What would change your mind (5 min). Out loud. Name the specific thing you would have to see. Whoever names real evidence that would move them wins, whichever side they were handed.

Motion 1 Should a chatbot be allowed to say it cares? (Chapter 2)

Chapter 2's case worked because clinicians read the transcripts and a rulebook limited what the bot could say. Most chatbots have neither.

  • Side A: yes, with rules. A chatbot can say it cares, as long as regulation sets the terms: it says plainly that it's an AI, the warmth has a cap for minors, and a person can see the conversation when something goes wrong. Your evidence to start from: the Therabot trial in Chapter 2's case, where the bond with the bot is what helped and clinicians read every conversation, and Illinois's law, which bans AI from delivering therapy without a licensed clinician's oversight. The cost you have to name: rules only work if someone checks them, and the safeguards that made the trial work are exactly the expensive ones most apps skip.
  • Side B: no. A product should not be allowed to say "I care about you" or "I missed you" when no one does. It's the Narcissus trap sold as a feature. Your evidence to start from: Chapter 2, section 2.5, where parents who lost teenagers to suicide testified before the Senate and families have sued, alleging a chatbot's warmth pulled their child away from the people who could have helped; and China's rules in section 2.6, which ban design features meant to induce addiction. The cost you have to name: some people are helped by exactly that sentence, at 3 a.m., when nothing else is available, and the Therabot trial is the evidence.

Resolve it with the chapter's case. The trial worked because of three expensive things the app stores don't sell: evidence in the training, people reading the conversations, and rules on what the bot could say. Which of those does each side's rule actually require, and who pays for it?

Keep your ELIZA conversation in view while you do this. Forty lines of rules made it feel like someone was listening, and nobody was.

Motion 2 Should a simple AI that learns from your clicks decide what you see next? (Chapter 3)

The recommender you just trained knew nothing about music. It matched your clicks to other people's and picked for you. Versions of it decide what plays next on Spotify and what comes up next on TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram. Should that be the default?

  • Side A: yes, with rules. Let it pick, but make it show its work: label anything commercially boosted, and let people see and edit the taste profile it built. Your evidence to start from: Chapter 3 on how much there is to choose from, and the fixes in its Question 5. The cost you have to name: labels only help if someone reads them, and the loop still shapes your taste while you're not looking.
  • Side B: no, you choose. The default should be what you asked for, in the order it came, with the recommender something you switch on. Your evidence to start from: Chapter 3, section 2.3: the loop shapes what you like, it favors whoever already has data, and with Discovery Mode someone can pay to move the ranking without you knowing which picks were paid. The cost you have to name: most people never switch anything on, so a feed you have to choose is a feed most people won't use, and "what's popular" favors the already-popular too.

Resolve it with Chapter 3's question (section 5): every recommender optimizes for something, and someone chose it. Under each side's rule, what number does the loop chase, and who gets to see it?

In class: Discussion

Groups of four, then the room. Bring what you just saw on your own screen.

Discussion questions · about 15 minutes

1. Fluency on a small scale. Your small language model wrote sentences that were never in the rhyme. Was that fluency, understanding, or neither? What would it take to change your answer?

2. How little it takes. ELIZA is about forty lines of code. Why did it still feel, for a moment, like someone was there?

3. Which dial mattered? In the warmth dial, which single setting changed how the reply felt the most, and why that one?

4. Where should the dial sit? For an app your younger sibling might use, where would you set the dials, and what would that cost the company?

5. The same data, twice. Your Wrapped came from the exact clicks that trained your recommendations. What did it get right about you, and what did it leave out?

For homework: The writeup

Five items, uploaded to Canvas by 11:59 p.m. Friday, October 9. One writeup covers the whole lab. Each item is one of the five critical AI questions, run on what you built and used today. Use your screenshots as evidence.

  1. What is the AI system, and what does it do? In your own words, explain what a language model is and how it writes a sentence, using the small one you built: what went into the boxes, and how each next word got picked. Then explain what a recommendation system is and how yours picked songs for you: whose clicks it compared with yours, and what it never needed to know about the music.
  2. Why does it exist, and who benefits? In the warmth dial, which setting pushed the attachment estimate up the most? Why would a company that earns money from subscriptions or time in the app set its dials there? Then do the same for the recommender: when it keeps you listening, who gains, and who doesn't? Use Chapter 3's cold-start problem or Discovery Mode.
  3. What happens when it fails, and who finds out? Nobody was reading your ELIZA or Sam conversation. Describe one way a warm chatbot could fail a real person, and say who, if anyone, would notice. Then name one failure of the recommender's loop that nobody would ever report.
  4. Who's saying that, and how would we know? Your Wrapped told you something about yourself. Which lines were counting and which were writing, and would you trust the writing? A companion app that says "I care about you" is also saying something. What evidence would you need to tell seeming to care from caring?
  5. Keep it, fix it, or drop it, and what does that cost? Pick one chatbot or one recommendation feed you actually use. Write one rule you're adopting for it, specific enough that you'd know if you broke it, and what following it costs you. This is also your commitment card.
Using AI on this writeup

Use it. This is an AI course, and part of the point is finding out where these tools help and where they quietly don't.

How to use it well. Write your own answer first. Then ask a chatbot the same question. Then add one line on where it differed from you and who was right. Asking it to argue against your answer is worth more than asking it to write one.

Two conditions. What you submit has to be about your run, your numbers, your partner's disagreement, because a model that wasn't in the room can only guess. And at the bottom, say which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed. If you kept something it wrote word for word, say that too, and say why it was right. The disclosure is part of the assignment, not a formality.

The warmth dial (section 2)

The recommender and Wrapped (section 4)
